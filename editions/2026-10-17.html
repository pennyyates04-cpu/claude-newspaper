Late-Night Eating Is Linked to Sharply Higher Risk of Fatty Liver Disease and Cirrhosis

What happened: Researchers at Oregon Health & Science University and Massachusetts General Hospital, led by Robert Wilechansky, analyzed decades of dietary-timing data from nearly 200,000 participants across the Nurses' Health Study, Nurses' Health Study II, and the Health Professionals Follow-Up Study. People who regularly ate after dinner or close to bedtime had roughly 20–30% higher rates of metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD, formerly called NAFLD) and cirrhosis than people who didn't eat late, even after adjusting for total calorie intake and other factors. The findings were published in Clinical Gastroenterology and Hepatology and widely covered in mid-October 2026. Why it matters: MASLD affects roughly a third of adults and can progress silently to cirrhosis and liver failure. If meal timing independently raises that risk, it's a modifiable behavior doctors could flag now, not just a future drug target.

Evidence
Mixed / uncertain evidence
In humans
Yes — nearly 200,000 people across three long-running cohort studies with decades of follow-up, but purely observational.
How promising
A large sample and consistent association across three cohorts is statistically convincing and fits known circadian-metabolism biology, but the diet data was self-reported and can't prove causation.
Unknowns
Whether eating-timing itself drives the excess risk, independent of diet quality, calories, sleep, and other confounds. A follow-up interventional trial is now recruiting.
Availability
Not a medical treatment — meal timing is a free behavior change.

Gene Therapy and Light-Stimulating Goggles Let Blind Patients With Retinitis Pigmentosa Detect Objects Again

What happened: GenSight Biologics and the Quinze-Vingts National Hospital in Paris published multi-year results from the Phase 1/2a PIONEER trial of GS030 in the October 8, 2026 issue of the New England Journal of Medicine. GS030 pairs an eye injection carrying a gene for the light-sensitive protein ChrimsonR — which reprograms surviving retinal ganglion cells to respond to light, bypassing the photoreceptors destroyed by retinitis pigmentosa — with goggles that convert a camera image into pulses of amber light those reprogrammed cells can detect. Patients with advanced retinitis pigmentosa and little or no usable vision were followed for up to several years. The therapy was safe and well tolerated at every dose tested, with no treatment-related serious adverse events leading to discontinuation, and several participants showed exploratory improvements in light sensitivity and in tasks like detecting, locating, and judging the orientation of objects. Why it matters: Retinitis pigmentosa has no treatment that restores vision once photoreceptors are lost. Years of favorable safety data published in a top journal, alongside functional signals, is a meaningful step toward testing whether this approach can give some blind patients usable, if crude, vision.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — a Phase 1/2a trial in a small number of patients with advanced retinitis pigmentosa, followed for multiple years; designed primarily to test safety.
How promising
Years of favorable safety plus functional gains in several patients is encouraging, but the trial wasn't powered for efficacy, and the restored vision is coarse light/motion/shape detection, not detailed sight.
Unknowns
How consistently patients respond, how much real-world benefit it provides, and whether a larger controlled efficacy trial will confirm the signal.
Availability
Experimental only — not approved; the company is now working toward an efficacy-focused trial.

Semaglutide Slowed Several "Biological Age" Clocks in a Randomized HIV Trial

What happened: Researchers led by Michael Corley at UC San Diego ran a post-hoc analysis of a 32-week, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase 2b trial of semaglutide (the GLP-1 drug sold as Ozempic/Wegovy) in 108 people with HIV-associated lipohypertrophy (abnormal fat accumulation), with 84 participants providing blood for epigenetic analysis. Compared with placebo, semaglutide slowed several DNA-methylation "aging clock" measures — including roughly a 9% slower pace of aging on the DunedinPACE clock and a multi-year reduction on the PhenoAge clock — and the effects held up after adjusting for weight change and inflammation markers. The analysis was published in Nature Communications in 2026. Corley cautioned: "We are not saying that semaglutide reverses aging or makes people younger," only that it may slow some biological processes associated with aging. Why it matters: GLP-1 drugs are already used by millions for diabetes and weight loss. If they also measurably slow biological-aging markers, that reframes them as a potential longevity intervention, not just a metabolic one — but that's a big "if" resting on this evidence alone.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — a randomized, placebo-controlled trial, though this is a secondary/exploratory analysis in people with HIV, with a modest sample (84 with paired blood samples).
How promising
A consistent effect across multiple independent epigenetic clocks is more credible than a single-measure finding, but those clocks are imperfect proxies for aging and this hasn't been tied to any hard clinical outcome.
Unknowns
Whether the epigenetic changes translate into fewer age-related diseases or longer life, whether they persist after stopping the drug, and whether they'd appear in the general population.
Availability
Clinically available — semaglutide is FDA-approved for diabetes and weight management, but using it to slow aging is not an approved use and remains unproven.

Some Gut Bacteria "Species" Are Actually Several Hidden Lineages — And Some Are Tied to Aging and Disease

What happened: Researchers at the University of Vienna's Centre for Microbiology and Environmental Systems Science, led by Xiaoqian Annie Yu and Martin Polz, used a genomics-first "reverse ecology" approach — starting from genetic sequence data rather than species labels — to analyze thousands of gut bacterial isolates and metagenomic samples from people of different ages, countries, and health states. They found that many bacterial groups classified as a single species actually contain several genetically distinct, ecologically specialized lineages shaped by competition inside the gut. Some of these lineages were associated with older age, inflammatory bowel disease, colorectal cancer, or type 2 diabetes, and some competing lineages had spread between people across continents within just a few decades — a pattern previously documented mainly in disease-causing pathogens. The study, "Genome-wide sweeps create ecological units in the human gut microbiome," was published in Nature in 2026. Why it matters: Microbiome research usually treats a bacterial species as a single unit, which may hide biologically important differences. If specific hidden lineages genuinely track with aging and disease, they could become more precise biomarkers or targets than today's broad, species-level microbiome measurements.

Evidence
Mixed / uncertain evidence
In humans
Yes — a large-scale analysis of human gut bacterial genomes and metagenomic data across many cohorts, but observational and associational, not interventional.
How promising
The scale and genomic rigor make these hidden lineages credible, and the cross-continental spread finding is genuinely new, but the aging/disease links are correlations in existing data, not proof of causation.
Unknowns
Which genes or functions distinguish the disease-associated lineages, whether they play any causal role, and whether they could become diagnostic markers or treatment targets.
Availability
Not a treatment or clinical test — basic research only.

A Topical Drug That Kills "Zombie" Skin Cells Nearly Halved Precancerous Lesions in Early Testing

What happened: Rubedo Life Sciences reported preliminary results from an open-label Phase 1b/2a U.S. trial of RLS-1496, a topical cream designed to selectively eliminate senescent ("zombie") skin cells that accumulate with age and sun damage. The drug modulates GPX4, an enzyme that normally protects cells from oxidative damage, making senescent cells more vulnerable to ferroptosis (an iron-dependent form of cell death) while aiming to spare healthy cells — a strategy the company calls "Adaptive SenoTherapeutics." In adults with actinic keratosis (precancerous, sun-damaged skin patches) treated on the forearm, the first 18 of 24 evaluated patients showed a 46% reduction in lesion count at four weeks, versus 11% in an untreated comparison area, with minimal irritation reported. The company presented the data at the RBC Capital Markets healthcare conference on May 19, 2026; a separate, earlier Phase 1 trial of the same drug in psoriasis, atopic dermatitis, and skin aging had already met its primary safety endpoint. Why it matters: Actinic keratosis affects millions of older adults and can progress to skin cancer. Current treatments (freezing, topical chemotherapy) often cause significant irritation, so a well-tolerated drug that clears the underlying senescent cells — rather than just removing visible lesions — would be a genuinely different approach, and a real-world test of whether targeting cellular senescence can treat a common condition, not just a longevity concept.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — two small, early-stage (Phase 1 and Phase 1b/2a) open-label or single-center trials; not yet randomized, double-blind, and placebo-controlled.
How promising
The effect size is large for an early trial and pairs with a plausible mechanism, but the results are company-reported, preliminary, and not yet peer-reviewed; some senescent cells also aid wound healing, so broadly clearing them could carry unknown tradeoffs.
Unknowns
Whether the effect holds up in a larger, placebo-controlled, peer-reviewed trial, how durable the lesion reduction is, and whether broadly clearing senescent cells causes longer-term side effects.
Availability
Experimental only — an investigational drug, not approved or commercially available.
