Three Months of Exercise Measurably Shifted Mood and Neuron-Formation Markers in Middle Age

In King's College London's NeuroFit randomized controlled trial, healthy middle-aged adults who completed a three-month structured exercise program showed significant improvements in depressive-symptom scores (measured by the PHQ-9) compared with a control group. Researchers also tested each participant's blood serum on human neural stem cells in the lab and found a shift in how that serum affected the cells' behavior over time, consistent with exercise remodeling circulating factors that influence neurogenesis. It's a rare human trial directly linking a structured, time-limited exercise dose to both a mood outcome and a laboratory proxy for how the brain makes new neurons — most neurogenesis evidence in humans is indirect.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — a randomized controlled trial in middle-aged adults, published in npj Aging
How promising
Clear, significant mood effect; the neurogenesis-proxy result was unexpected — exerciser serum reduced neural stem cell proliferation in the lab dish, which researchers speculate reflects the brain conserving a finite stem-cell pool
Unknowns
Whether the blood-serum assay reflects what happens inside a living human brain, and whether the mood benefit persists beyond three months
Availability
Not a clinical product — describes general aerobic exercise; the neurogenesis assay is a research tool only

FDA Advisory Panel Recommends the Wellness Peptide BPC-157 for Compounding, Over Staff Objections

On July 23-24, 2026, the FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee voted 8-6 to recommend adding BPC-157 to the Section 503A Bulks List, which would let compounding pharmacies legally prepare it. The vote came despite FDA staff's own briefing documents recommending against inclusion, citing sparse human trial data and potential safety risks, including immune reactions and product-purity concerns. BPC-157 is one of the most widely sold "healing peptides" in wellness and biohacking circles, marketed for tendon, gut, and injury repair — but it has never been FDA-approved, and this vote is a real regulatory flashpoint over how much evidence should be required before a popular but unproven peptide gets an easier path to legal compounding.

Unknowns
Whether BPC-157 is actually effective for the injuries it's marketed for in humans, and its long-term safety profile
Availability
Not FDA-approved; the recommendation is non-binding and a formal 8-12 month rulemaking process must still follow

Off-the-Shelf Stem-Cell Therapy for Parkinson's Clears 12-Month Safety Milestone

At the ISSCR 2026 Annual Meeting, Lund University researchers presented 12-month results from the STEM-PD Phase I/II trial, which transplants a cryopreserved, off-the-shelf dopaminergic progenitor cell product — derived from human pluripotent stem cells — into the brains of patients with moderately advanced Parkinson's disease who no longer respond well to medication. The trial met its primary endpoint of demonstrating feasibility, and the transplanted cells showed signs of surviving and functioning as dopamine-producing neurons. Parkinson's disease progressively kills the dopamine-producing neurons that control movement, and current drugs only manage symptoms; an off-the-shelf cell product that can be manufactured in advance and safely transplanted would be a major manufacturing and access advantage over custom cell therapies if it eventually proves effective.

Yes — an open-label Phase I/II trial in patients with moderately advanced Parkinson's disease
How promising
Encouraging on safety and biological activity, but this trial phase wasn't designed or powered to prove the therapy improves symptoms
Unknowns
Whether the biological dopamine activity translates into meaningful, lasting symptom improvement, and how it performs in a larger, controlled trial

Experimental only — an investigational cell therapy in early-phase clinical trials

FDA Panel Votes Against Capricor's Duchenne Heart-Damage Cell Therapy; Decision Pushed to November

On July 29, 2026, an FDA advisory committee voted 9-3 that the available evidence does not demonstrate substantial effectiveness for deramiocel, Capricor Therapeutics' cardiac stem-cell-derived therapy for the heart damage (cardiomyopathy) caused by Duchenne muscular dystrophy. Reviewers found that neither of the therapy's two pivotal trials (HOPE-2, HOPE-3) met its prespecified primary or key secondary endpoints, and that Capricor's post-hoc analyses didn't fill the gap. The FDA's original August 22 decision deadline has since been pushed to November 22, 2026, after Capricor submitted new 24-month HOPE-3 data and asked the agency to consider a narrower approval focused on upper-limb function, an endpoint the company says HOPE-3 met with statistical significance. Duchenne muscular dystrophy is a fatal, progressive genetic disease, and heart failure is a leading cause of death in patients; there's real pressure to approve promising therapies for a small, underserved patient population, colliding here with a trial record that an independent panel found didn't clear the effectiveness bar.

Mayo Clinic Finds a Blood Marker for "Zombie" Cells — and a Tomato Compound That Calms Them in Mice

A series of seven Mayo Clinic studies, part of the NIH-funded Cellular Senescence Network (SenNet) Consortium, reported new findings on senescent ("zombie") cells — aging cells that stop dividing but stay metabolically active and pump out inflammatory molecules linked to age-related disease. Researchers identified IL-23R, a plasma protein that rises with age in human blood samples, as a candidate biomarker of senescence burden. In separate work using aged mice, a naturally occurring tomato-derived compound, along with existing senolytic drugs and genetic approaches that selectively clear senescent cells, reduced inflammation and improved measures of physical frailty, brain inflammation, and cognitive performance. Senescent-cell buildup is one of the leading biological theories of aging, but tracking it in living people has been difficult; a blood biomarker like IL-23R could let researchers measure biological aging and test whether anti-senescence treatments actually work, in people rather than just mice.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Partially — the IL-23R biomarker discovery used human blood plasma samples; the interventions that reduced inflammation and improved frailty were tested only in aged mice
How promising
The biomarker angle is a meaningful tool-building step; the tomato-compound and senolytic results are encouraging animal data but a long way from a human treatment
Unknowns
Whether lowering IL-23R or the senescent-cell burden it reflects actually slows aging or disease in humans, and whether the tomato-derived compound is safe or effective in people
Availability
Experimental only — biomarker and mouse-study findings, not a clinical test or treatment
