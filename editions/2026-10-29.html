One-time CRISPR gene edit cut cholesterol in half — and it held for a year

What happened: Cleveland Clinic researchers published one-year results from a first-in-human trial of CTX310, a CRISPR-Cas9 gene-editing therapy delivered as a single IV infusion that permanently switches off the ANGPTL3 gene in the liver. In the four patients who received the highest dose, LDL ("bad") cholesterol fell 52.5% and triglycerides fell 47.8%, with the effect still holding a full year after treatment. The results were published as a letter in the New England Journal of Medicine and presented at the European Society of Cardiology Congress 2026. Why it matters: Current cholesterol drugs require lifelong daily dosing. A one-time gene edit with a durable effect could change how doctors manage cardiovascular risk, especially for people who don't respond to or can't tolerate statins.

Harvard: 90 minutes of weekly strength training tied to a 27% lower risk of dying from neurological disease

What happened: A Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health analysis drew on roughly 30 years of data from three large cohorts — the Health Professionals Follow-up Study and the two Nurses' Health Studies — and found that 90–119 minutes of resistance training per week was associated with a 13% lower risk of death from any cause, a 19% lower risk of cardiovascular death, and a 27% lower risk of death from neurological disease, including dementia, compared with no resistance training. Benefits plateaued beyond about 120 minutes per week. People who combined resistance training with regular aerobic exercise had up to a 45% lower mortality risk than those who did neither. Why it matters: Most exercise guidance centers on cardio. This gives a specific, modest, achievable weekly strength-training target tied to lower death risk — including a dementia link with less prior long-term human data behind it.

Evidence
Strong, for an observational study — large sample, decades of follow-up, clear dose-response
In humans
Yes — roughly 140,000 health professionals tracked for up to 30 years
How promising
One of the more robust long-term datasets tying a realistic weekly dose to lower mortality
Unknowns
Observational, not randomized, so causation isn't proven; cohort is mostly white health professionals; self-reported habits
Availability
Not a treatment — exercise guidance anyone can act on today

Yale study finds most "biological age" tests don't reliably track whether anti-aging treatments work

What happened: Yale researchers led by Raghav Sehgal, in Albert Higgins-Chen's lab, systematically evaluated 111 DNA-methylation "epigenetic clocks" — including 16 of the most widely used — against 51 real human longevity-intervention studies, to see which clocks actually detect change when an intervention works. The findings were published in Nature Medicine, and the full dataset was released as an open platform called TranslAGE. Why it matters: The booming longevity industry leans heavily on epigenetic "age" tests to claim a supplement, protocol, or clinic is working. This study is a reality check: many popular clocks are poor at detecting real intervention effects, while a handful of newer, reliability-optimized clocks (including DunedinPACE and PCGrimAge) responded far more consistently.

Evidence
Mixed/uncertain — specifically regarding what today's epigenetic clocks can tell us
In humans
Yes — built from existing human intervention-study data; the study itself is a measurement-tool evaluation, not a treatment trial
How promising
Useful going forward for evaluating longevity claims, but validates no specific treatment
Unknowns
Lead author noted there's still no direct evidence a clock change predicts a real future health outcome
Availability
Not a treatment — TranslAGE.io is a research tool, and a reference point for evaluating longevity-clock marketing

No evidence glucose monitors help healthy people, major review finds

What happened: A JAMA Internal Medicine narrative review by Dower, Johansson, Camp, Montori, and Lipska examined the evidence behind continuous glucose monitors (CGMs) — increasingly marketed to healthy people for "metabolic optimization" — and concluded there is no scientific evidence that CGM use improves health or prevents disease in people without diabetes. Why it matters: CGMs have become a flagship wellness and biohacking product worn by people with no diabetes risk to chase flat glucose curves. This review, co-authored by a senior Yale endocrinologist, says popularity has run well ahead of the evidence.

Evidence
Little or no credible evidence, specifically for non-diabetic use
In humans
Review synthesizes existing human studies rather than presenting new data
How promising
Not promising for healthy users — normal glucose swings can be overinterpreted, causing unnecessary restriction or anxiety
Unknowns
Whether a subgroup at elevated future diabetes risk benefits from early CGM use; authors say more research is needed
Availability
Widely available now (Dexcom, Abbott) — availability isn't the issue; proof of benefit is

JAMA Internal Medicine (DOI 10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.2772), via Yale School of Medicine, August 4, 2026 — medicine.yale.edu

Mass General Brigham to help design a large-scale NAD+ trial — but it hasn't started yet

What happened: Niagen Bioscience, maker of the NAD+ precursor supplement Tru Niagen, announced a start-up agreement with Mass General Brigham investigators — led by Brigham and Women's Hospital epidemiologist Howard Sesso — to begin detailed planning for what the company describes as the largest, most comprehensive randomized, placebo-controlled trial yet of nicotinamide riboside (NR) in adults 50 and older. Why it matters: NAD+ boosters, sold as pills and IV infusions, are heavily marketed for anti-aging and energy despite thin human outcome data. A trial at this scale, if it launches, could finally produce a real answer on clinical benefit rather than just NAD+ blood-level changes.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary — this is a trial-design announcement, not results
In humans
Smaller NR studies exist (incl. a 2025 long-COVID trial showing higher NAD+ levels), but no large healthy-aging outcome trial has completed
How promising
Scale and academic partner are notable, but trial hasn't enrolled and announcement comes from the supplement maker
Unknowns

Availability
NAD+ supplements/IV infusions already widely sold; for prevention/longevity benefit, availability has outpaced proof
