## Lab-Grown Bacterial Cocktail Matches Fecal Transplants for Recurrent C. diff

**What happened:** Researchers at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai built a manufacturing platform that grows a defined 15-strain mixture of gut bacteria — a "live biotherapeutic product" (LBP) — isolated from donor stool but produced under controlled lab conditions rather than harvested fresh. In a phase 1b trial of 18 patients with recurrent C. difficile infection, the LBP was compared head-to-head with standard fecal microbiota transplant (FMT) made from the same donor source. The work was published in the June 2, 2026 issue of Nature Medicine.
**Why it matters:** FMT already works well for recurrent C. diff, but it depends on screening individual stool donors and produces a variable, hard-to-standardize product. A manufactured bacterial mixture that performs as well could be easier to quality-control, scale, and eventually get through regulatory approval.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** Yes — phase 1b trial, 18 patients
**How promising:** The LBP matched FMT's safety and efficacy across every dose group tested — a clean early head-to-head signal, though from a small trial.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether the results hold up in a larger randomized trial, how durable the effect is, and whether the manufacturing process is affordable at scale.
**Availability:** Experimental only — no product of this kind is approved yet.

**Source:** Mount Sinai Newsroom, June 2, 2026 — https://www.mountsinai.org/about/newsroom/2026/engineered-gut-bacteria-therapy-emerges-as-scalable-potential-alternative-to-fecal-microbiota-transplants-following-clinical-trial

## New Review Finds No Real Evidence Behind Popular NAD+ IV Anti-Aging Claims

**What happened:** A retrospective pilot study published February 2, 2026 in Frontiers in Aging compared real-world tolerability of IV infusions of NAD+ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) against IV nicotinamide riboside (NR), a related compound. Around the same study, a broader systematic search for outcome trials testing IV NAD+ specifically for anti-aging or wellness benefits came up empty — no eligible trials were found showing it improves energy, longevity, or function in healthy people.
**Why it matters:** IV NAD+ drips are marketed heavily at wellness clinics as an anti-aging and energy treatment, often at significant cost, but until now the evidence base behind that specific claim had not been mapped rigorously.

**Evidence:** Little or no credible evidence (for the anti-aging/wellness claim specifically)
**Studied in humans:** Yes, but only for tolerability and safety — not for anti-aging efficacy. The strongest actual clinical evidence for IV NAD+ is in a different context entirely: opioid and alcohol withdrawal.
**How promising:** Not promising as an anti-aging intervention based on current evidence. The underlying biology (NAD+ levels fall with age) is real, but IV delivery hasn't been shown to translate that into a measurable benefit.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether IV infusion meaningfully raises NAD+ inside cells (as opposed to just blood levels), and whether that would produce any measurable healthspan benefit over cheaper oral precursors.
**Availability:** Clinically available now, widely, at wellness and IV-therapy clinics — availability is not the same as proven benefit.

**Source:** Frontiers in Aging, February 2, 2026 — https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/aging/articles/10.3389/fragi.2026.1652582/full

## Implanted Vagus Nerve Stimulator Shows Early Safety Signal in Alzheimer's Pilot

**What happened:** Neurosurgeons at Beijing Tiantan Hospital surgically implanted vagus nerve stimulators in five patients with Alzheimer's disease (mean age 62) and tracked cognition, mood, inflammation markers, and Alzheimer's-specific biomarkers at baseline and again at 3 and 6 months. The open-label pilot was published September 29, 2026 in Frontiers in Neuroscience.
**Why it matters:** Vagus nerve stimulation is already an approved treatment for epilepsy and depression, partly through its effect on inflammatory signaling. If it also nudges Alzheimer's-related biology, it could offer a non-drug add-on for a disease with very few effective treatment options.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** Yes — five patients, open-label, no control or sham group.
**How promising:** Early signals were positive across cognition, mood, inflammation, and Alzheimer's biomarkers, but this was explicitly a feasibility-and-safety pilot, not a test of whether the device actually works.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether these changes reflect a real treatment effect or expectation/practice effects — the authors themselves say a larger, randomized, sham-controlled trial is needed before drawing conclusions.
**Availability:** Experimental only for Alzheimer's — invasive VNS devices are approved for epilepsy and depression, not for dementia.

**Source:** Frontiers in Neuroscience, September 29, 2026 — https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2026.1934496/full

## Adding a Cold Plunge to Sauna Cooling Breaks Cuts Heat Strain, Study Finds

**What happened:** Sixteen healthy adults completed two 15-minute rounds of 85°C (185°F) sauna, separated by a 10-minute cooling break — either resting in room-temperature air, or the same break with a 90-second dip in 20°C water added. The crossover study was published July 2, 2026 in Experimental Physiology.
**Why it matters:** Sauna-then-cold-plunge routines are a popular recovery and wellness trend; this is one of the first controlled studies to isolate exactly what the cold portion adds, physiologically, beyond the sauna alone.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** Yes — 16 healthy adults, controlled crossover design.
**How promising:** The effect was real and measurable: adding the cold-water dip left core temperature about 0.5°C lower (37.55°C vs. 38.03°C) and heart rate 17 beats per minute lower (108 vs. 125 bpm) at the end of the second sauna bout, compared with cooling in air alone, along with less subjective heat discomfort.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether reduced acute heat strain in a single session translates into any longer-term cardiovascular or longevity benefit — this study measured same-day physiology, not health outcomes over time.
**Availability:** Not a medical therapy — a recovery practice people can already do, with this study mainly showing it reduces heat strain and improves tolerance.

**Source:** Experimental Physiology, July 2, 2026 — https://physoc.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1113/EP093626

## Massive Review Shows Which "Biological Age" Clocks Actually Move — and What Moves Them

**What happened:** Researchers built a database (called TranslAGE) pooling 51 human longevity-intervention studies and tested 16 different epigenetic "biological age" clocks and 94 related DNA-methylation biomarkers, to see which ones reliably change in response to interventions such as metformin, Mediterranean-style diets, and TNF-targeting drugs. The analysis was published in Nature Medicine in September 2026.
**Why it matters:** Dozens of companies now sell "biological age" blood tests direct to consumers, but which of these clocks are actually sensitive enough to detect a real intervention effect, versus just noise, has been unclear.

**Evidence:** Mixed/uncertain (for using these clocks to prove an anti-aging benefit — though the responsiveness findings themselves are well-supported)
**Studied in humans:** Yes — a synthesis of 51 existing human intervention studies, not a new standalone trial.
**How promising:** Useful as a measurement tool: newer "generation 2+" clocks built to predict mortality or pace of aging were consistently the most responsive, and metformin and Mediterranean diets produced the clearest changes.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether moving one of these clocks actually means a person's health or lifespan improved. The authors are explicit that responsiveness alone doesn't validate a biomarker as a proven stand-in for slower aging.
**Availability:** Not a clinical treatment — a research and measurement framework, though several of the clocks studied are already sold commercially as consumer tests.

**Source:** Nature Medicine, September 2026 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04562-9
