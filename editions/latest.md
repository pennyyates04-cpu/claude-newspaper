## First human data on a "cell rejuvenation" gene therapy shows early signs in glaucoma patients

**What happened:** Life Biosciences, co-founded by longevity researcher David Sinclair, announced interim Phase 1 results for ER-100, a gene therapy using a single intravitreal (eye) injection of three "Yamanaka factors" plus daily oral doxycycline to partially reprogram aging retinal cells. In the first three open-angle glaucoma patients, dosed at the lowest level (2×10¹¹ vg), the therapy was well tolerated through day 56 with no dose-limiting toxicities, serious adverse events, or events of special interest. Two of the three patients showed improvement on a standard visual-field test, with at least 28% of test points improving by more than 4 decibels. A safety board has since cleared the trial to escalate to a higher dose.
**Why it matters:** This is the first time a "partial epigenetic reprogramming" therapy — an approach that tries to reverse cellular aging markers without erasing a cell's identity — has been dosed in humans at all, after years of promising but preclinical mouse and primate data.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — a first-in-human, open-label Phase 1 trial with no control group; results so far cover only 3 of up to 18 planned participants.
**How promising:** The clean safety readout at eight weeks is the headline result; the visual-field improvement in 2 of 3 patients is an encouraging early signal but far too small a sample to call a treatment effect.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether the vision signal holds up, replicates at higher doses, or persists over time, and whether any of this translates to NAION (the trial's other target condition) or to reprogramming elsewhere in the body.
**Availability:** Experimental only; the Phase 1 trial (NCT07290244) is ongoing at four U.S. sites.

**Source:** Life Biosciences press release, October 2026 — https://www.lifebiosciences.com/life-biosciences-announces-first-in-human-data-from-ongoing-phase-1-trial-evaluating-er-100-in-optic-neuropathies/

## A rapamycin-plus-exercise trial undercuts a popular longevity claim

**What happened:** RAPA-EX-01, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial published in the Journal of Cachexia, Sarcopenia and Muscle, gave 40 sedentary adults aged 65–85 either weekly 6 mg sirolimus (rapamycin) or placebo alongside an identical 13-week home exercise program (resistance "chair stands" plus stationary cycling). Both groups improved on the primary measure, but sirolimus did not add benefit: the intention-to-treat difference was -2.13 chair-stand repetitions favoring placebo (95% CI -4.61 to 0.34; p=0.089), and some pre-specified sensitivity analyses found placebo significantly outperformed rapamycin. The rapamycin group also had more total adverse events (99 vs. 63), including one possibly drug-related serious infection.
**Why it matters:** Rapamycin is widely promoted in longevity circles as a way to amplify the benefits of exercise through mTOR inhibition. This is one of the first controlled human trials to test that specific combination directly, and it found no benefit — with a hint of possible interference.

**Evidence:** Little or no credible evidence, specifically for the claim that rapamycin boosts exercise-driven strength and endurance gains in older adults.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — a small (n=40), exploratory randomized controlled trial.
**How promising:** Not promising as currently used for this purpose; the authors call the trial exploratory given its size, single dose, and 13-week duration.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether a different dose, schedule (e.g., intermittent rather than weekly), or longer duration would change the result, and whether rapamycin's broader longevity effects (seen mainly in animal studies) hold regardless of this null exercise finding.
**Availability:** Rapamycin is FDA-approved only for organ-transplant rejection and certain rare conditions; it is prescribed off-label for longevity purposes at some clinics despite no approved anti-aging indication.

**Source:** Society of Cachexia, Sarcopenia and Wasting Disorders summary, Journal of Cachexia, Sarcopenia and Muscle, 2026 — https://society-scwd.org/exercise-and-weekly-sirolimus-rapamycin-in-older-adults-rapa-ex-01-randomised-double-blind-placebo-controlled-trial/ ; trial registration, ANZCTR — https://anzctr.org.au/ACTRN12624000790549.aspx

## A topical "senolytic" cream shows early, company-reported promise for psoriasis and eczema

**What happened:** Rubedo Life Sciences reported preliminary Phase 1 results for RLS-1496, a topical cream that modulates GPX4 to push disease-driving senescent ("zombie") skin cells toward ferroptosis, a form of programmed cell death. In a single-center, randomized, double-blind, vehicle-controlled EU trial of roughly 44 patients across three dose strengths, the company says it met its primary safety and tolerability endpoint. Psoriasis cohorts showed a dose-dependent average 20% reduction in epidermal thickness after one month, and in a separate atopic-dermatitis cohort, 25% of RLS-1496 patients had a clinically meaningful drop in itch score versus none on vehicle.
**Why it matters:** Senescent cells are thought to drive both chronic inflammatory skin disease and visible skin aging; a topical drug built around clearing them would be a concrete bridge between aging biology and everyday dermatology, rather than a systemic anti-aging pill.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — a small, single-center Phase 1 trial, one month of treatment, with results so far only company-announced.
**How promising:** The reported safety and early biological signals are notable, but the psoriasis and atopic-dermatitis data come from different patient cohorts, and none of it has yet appeared in a peer-reviewed publication.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether benefits persist beyond one month, how the drug performs in a larger independently reviewed trial, and whether "senescent cell clearance" is really the mechanism at work.
**Availability:** Experimental only.

**Source:** Longevity.Technology, March 2026 — https://longevity.technology/news/rubedo-reports-positive-phase-1-results-for-rls-1496/ ; EU Clinical Trials Information System registration — https://ctis.eu/trial/2025-520514-64-00

## NAD+ IV drips cause more side effects than a cheaper alternative — and neither has proven anti-aging benefits

**What happened:** A retrospective review of wellness-clinic records, published in Frontiers in Aging in February 2026, compared patients who received four consecutive days of 500 mg NAD+ IV infusions against patients who received the same regimen with nicotinamide riboside (NR) instead. The NAD+ group reported more side effects during infusion — including gastrointestinal symptoms, faster heart rate, and chest pressure — and their infusions took roughly 97 minutes on average, versus about 37 minutes for NR. Neither group showed significant changes in liver, kidney, or thyroid markers over 30 days of follow-up. The study was funded in part by Restore Hyper Wellness and Niagen Biosciences, which sells the NR product used in the comparison.
**Why it matters:** NAD+ IV therapy is marketed heavily as an anti-aging and energy treatment, often costing hundreds of dollars per session, despite lacking FDA approval for any indication and despite this study — like most NAD+ IV research to date — measuring tolerability rather than any actual health benefit.

**Evidence:** Little or no credible evidence for anti-aging or longevity benefit; mixed/uncertain evidence even on which IV formulation is better tolerated, given the funding conflicts.
**Studied in humans:** Yes, but only as a small retrospective chart review of real-world patients, not a trial designed to test whether either infusion actually works.
**How promising:** Not promising as an anti-aging therapy based on current data; the main finding is that NAD+ IV is harder on the body to deliver than NR IV, not that either extends healthspan.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether IV infusion meaningfully raises NAD+ levels inside cells at all — some researchers argue it's an inefficient delivery route — and whether any clinical outcome beyond lab safety markers improves.
**Availability:** Widely available now at wellness clinics nationwide, used off-label with no FDA-approved indication.

**Source:** Frontiers in Aging / PMC, published February 2, 2026 — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12907335/

## Largest study yet on strength training and lifespan finds a clear, modest dose works best

**What happened:** Harvard researchers followed more than 147,000 adults across three long-running cohort studies for up to 30 years, publishing their analysis in the British Journal of Sports Medicine in June 2026. Compared with doing no resistance training, people who did 90–119 minutes of resistance training per week had a 13% lower risk of death from any cause, a 19% lower risk of cardiovascular death, and a 27% lower risk of death from neurological disease. The benefit leveled off above roughly 120 minutes per week. People who also did substantial aerobic activity (45+ MET-hours weekly) had 53–58% lower mortality risk regardless of how much resistance training they did.
**Why it matters:** Strength training is one of the cheapest, most accessible interventions in the "exercise as medicine" category, and this is one of the largest and longest-running looks yet at how much of it is actually associated with a longer life.

**Evidence:** Strong evidence for the association, drawn from a large, long-running dataset; it's observational, so it cannot prove the training itself caused the lower mortality.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — a large observational cohort study, not a randomized trial.
**How promising:** It reinforces a pattern seen across many smaller studies, with a useful new detail: benefit appears to plateau around two hours per week, so more volume isn't clearly better.
**What we don't know yet:** The exact mechanism, whether intensity or type of resistance training matters, and the standard observational caveat that healthier or wealthier people may simply be more likely to lift weights regularly.
**Availability:** Not a medical treatment requiring approval — a lifestyle recommendation already accessible to most people.

**Source:** British Journal of Sports Medicine, June 2026, doi:10.1136/bjsports-2025-110503 — https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2025-110503 ; Harvard Health Publishing summary — https://www.health.harvard.edu/exercise-and-fitness/strength-training-over-decades-linked-to-longer-life
