## A Single CRISPR Infusion Kept Cholesterol Down for a Full Year

**What happened:** In a Cleveland Clinic-led Phase 1 trial, 15 patients with medication-resistant lipid disorders received a one-time IV infusion of CTX310, a CRISPR-Cas9 therapy from CRISPR Therapeutics that permanently switches off the ANGPTL3 gene in liver cells. Twelve months after dosing, the highest-dose group showed average reductions of 79% in ANGPTL3 protein, 53% in LDL cholesterol, and 48% in triglycerides from baseline, with no treatment-related dose-limiting toxic effects. Results were presented at the European Society of Cardiology Congress 2026 and published simultaneously in the New England Journal of Medicine.
**Why it matters:** Most cholesterol-lowering drugs require daily or periodic dosing for life. This suggests a single treatment could produce durable, whole-body metabolic change — a landmark test case for in-vivo gene editing beyond cholesterol.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary — a real human trial with a clean one-year durability signal, but small (n=15), open-label, and without a placebo comparison group.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — this is a first-in-human Phase 1 trial.
**How promising:** The size and persistence of the lipid drop at one year is striking for a single infusion, but the trial wasn't designed or powered to show it prevents heart attacks or strokes.
**What we don't know yet:** Long-term safety beyond one year, whether the effect holds indefinitely, and whether it translates into fewer cardiovascular events.
**Availability:** Experimental only — Phase 1 data; years from possible approval.

**Source:** New England Journal of Medicine, published August 28, 2026 (presented at ESC Congress 2026) — https://www.nejm.org/doi/10.1056/NEJMc2609825

## Walking Faster Can Make Up for Walking Less, Large UK Study Finds

**What happened:** Researchers analyzed data from 103,684 UK Biobank participants (ages 40–69) who wore wrist accelerometers for seven days between 2013 and 2015, then tracked mortality over roughly the following decade. People logging 7,500–10,000 steps a day had the lowest death risk, in line with existing guidance. But among people who took fewer than 5,000 steps a day, those who walked at a brisker pace (more than about 80 steps per minute during their walking bouts) had mortality risk comparable to people logging far more steps at a slower pace.
**Why it matters:** Step-count targets can feel unreachable for people with mobility limits, packed schedules, or sedentary jobs. This offers a second, equally evidence-backed lever — intensity — for people who can't easily add volume.

**Evidence:** Strong evidence in scale — a large, device-measured (not self-reported) prospective cohort — but still observational, so it shows association rather than proof that speeding up causes the benefit.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — entirely human, objectively measured activity data.
**How promising:** Practical and immediately actionable; reverse causation (healthier people naturally walk faster) remains the main alternative explanation researchers can't fully rule out.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether deliberately training to walk faster produces the same risk reduction as being a naturally brisk walker.
**Availability:** Not a treatment — free, actionable lifestyle guidance today.

**Source:** British Journal of Sports Medicine, published online September 8, 2026 — https://bmjgroup.com/different-daily-step-count-and-stepping-pace-combos-may-all-lower-risk-of-early-death/

## UCLA Review: The Evidence for Trendy "Healing" Peptides Doesn't Match the Hype

**What happened:** UCLA Health researchers systematically reviewed 565 studies on six peptides widely marketed online and in wellness clinics for injury recovery, muscle building, and anti-aging: BPC-157, TB-500, CJC-1295, MK-677, ipamorelin, and GHK-Cu. More than two-thirds of the studies were animal-only, and the handful of human studies showed at most modest benefits for non-orthopedic uses, with essentially no rigorous evidence of musculoskeletal benefit. Reviewers also flagged a halted MK-677 trial linked to congestive heart failure risk and a conflict of interest in one of the largest BPC-157 studies.
**Why it matters:** These unregulated peptides are a fast-growing part of the biohacking and regenerative-medicine market, often sold through compounding pharmacies without FDA approval for the uses being marketed.

**Evidence:** Little or no credible evidence for the marketed musculoskeletal/anti-aging claims — this is a systematic review of existing (mostly weak) studies, not new data.
**Studied in humans:** Partially — most underlying research is in animals; human data is sparse and low quality.
**How promising:** Not promising as currently sold; authors say the safety and efficacy evidence for long-term human use "just isn't there."
**What we don't know yet:** Whether properly designed human trials at clinically relevant doses would show real musculoskeletal benefit, and what's actually in unregulated products.
**Availability:** Not FDA-approved for the marketed uses; sold off-label through wellness clinics and online sources.

**Source:** The American Journal of Sports Medicine, published online August 11, 2026 (UCLA Health coverage September 8, 2026) — https://www.uclahealth.org/news/article/with-peptide-supplements-marketing-outpaces-medicine

## First Human Study Links Specific Gut Bacteria Directly to Brain Chemical Levels

**What happened:** University of Surrey and University of Roehampton researchers studied 61 healthy young women, combining shotgun metagenomic sequencing of stool samples with proton MR spectroscopy brain scans measuring GABA and glutamate in three brain regions. Microbial genetic pathways for producing and breaking down GABA, glutamate, short-chain fatty acids, and other neuroactive compounds correlated with region-specific brain chemical levels, and with participants' anxiety, depressive symptoms, and sleep quality.
**Why it matters:** The gut-brain axis is usually inferred indirectly, through behavior or blood markers. This is a more direct human link between what gut microbes are metabolically capable of doing and actual brain neurochemistry.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary — a well-designed mechanistic study, but small and narrow in population.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — entirely human, using live neuroimaging plus stool metagenomics.
**How promising:** Mechanistically compelling, but the study only shows correlation in one demographic slice, with no intervention (like a probiotic) tested.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether the relationship holds in men, older adults, or people with diagnosed mental health conditions, and whether changing gut microbes actually shifts brain chemistry or mood.
**Availability:** Research only — no diagnostic or therapeutic product exists from this yet.

**Source:** Molecular Psychiatry, published online September 1, 2026 (University of Surrey release) — https://www.surrey.ac.uk/news/gut-bacteria-linked-key-brain-chemicals-first-its-kind-human-study

## Gut Microbiome Predicts Inflammation and Disease Risk Better Than Age Alone

**What happened:** Researchers analyzed fecal microbiota, 30 plasma cytokines, and 19 metabolic/physiological measures in 1,199 adults ages 20–72 from Denmark's population-based DanFunD cohort. Gut microbiota composition explained more of the variation in inflammatory markers and physiological measures than chronological age did — outperforming age for 97% of the cytokines and 84% of the physiological variables studied. People with a specific "Bacteroides 2" gut-microbiome profile showed elevated inflammatory markers and adverse metabolic signs as early as their 20s and 30s, resembling an "inflammaging" pattern up to 37 years ahead of schedule.
**Why it matters:** If confirmed, gut-microbiome typing could flag people heading toward chronic inflammation and metabolic disease decades before symptoms appear, independent of their actual age.

**Evidence:** Mixed / uncertain evidence for real-world use — a large, well-characterized human cohort, but purely observational (cross-sectional), so it can't establish cause and effect.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — 1,199 adults, entirely human data.
**How promising:** A striking association in a sizable cohort, but it's a single-country (Danish) population, and no intervention has been tested to see if changing this microbiome profile lowers actual disease risk.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether the Bacteroides 2 profile is a cause of inflammaging or just a marker of it, and whether the pattern replicates in other populations.
**Availability:** Research-stage only — not an available clinical test.

**Source:** Nature Communications, published online September 8–9, 2026 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-77499-9

## Hidden Brain Wiring May Help Preserve Language as Gray Matter Shrinks

**What happened:** USC's Stevens Neuroimaging and Informatics Institute analyzed brain scans and cognitive testing from 459 community-dwelling adults age 60 and older in India, focusing on "superficial white matter" — short nerve-fiber bundles just beneath the cortex that connect nearby brain regions, distinct from the deep white-matter tracts usually studied in aging research. Gray-matter loss was the strongest overall predictor of cognitive decline, but healthier superficial white matter was specifically tied to better-preserved language ability, and appeared to weaken the link between gray-matter loss and cognitive impairment when it was in good condition.
**Why it matters:** It identifies a distinct, understudied brain structure that may buffer the cognitive effects of gray-matter aging — a potential new target for preserving thinking skills, especially in under-researched, lower-resource populations.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary — a reasonably sized human cohort study, but cross-sectional, so it can't show whether healthy white matter causes the protective effect.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — 459 adults, brain imaging and cognitive testing.
**How promising:** A plausible and novel structural finding, but it needs replication in other populations and longitudinal follow-up to confirm the buffering effect is real and not just a snapshot correlation.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether interventions could improve superficial white matter health, and whether the same pattern holds in higher-income countries and other age groups.
**Availability:** Basic research only — no diagnostic or clinical application yet.

**Source:** Alzheimer's & Dementia: The Journal of the Alzheimer's Association, ScienceDaily coverage September 13, 2026 — https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/09/260913081921.htm
