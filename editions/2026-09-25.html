Donor Immune Cells Can Transfer Healthy Mitochondria to Failing Cells

A Stanford Medicine team led by pediatrics researcher Natalia Gomez-Ospina found that bone-marrow-derived immune cells, after transplant, don't just replace damaged tissue — they physically donate their own healthy mitochondria to neighboring cells whose mitochondria are failing. Testing the approach in mice engineered to model Friedreich's ataxia (a genetic disease that cripples cellular energy production and damages the heart and nervous system), the team developed a conditioning regimen that boosted how efficiently donor cells took up residence throughout the body. Treated mice lived longer, grew better, and — in females — showed improved movement, strength, coordination, and heart function compared with untreated mice with the disease. Friedreich's ataxia and related mitochondrial diseases currently have no way to fix the underlying energy-production defect in already-damaged tissue; a transplant approach that lets healthy donor cells directly resupply failing cells with working mitochondria is a different repair strategy than most current gene or enzyme approaches.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
No — a mouse model (human-frataxin-gene mouse strain mimicking the disease); no human trial yet.
How promising
A clean mechanistic story with measurable multi-marker improvement, but sex-specific results and the need for a conditioning regimen raise translation questions.
Unknowns
Whether mitochondrial transfer is efficient enough in humans, what conditioning would be safe in fragile patients, and why benefits differed by sex.
Availability
Experimental only — preclinical, not in any human pipeline yet.

Stanford Medicine research, published in Nature Communications, September 2026 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13574779/

First Direct Human Evidence Links Gut Bacteria to Brain Chemical Levels

Researchers from the University of Surrey and the University of Roehampton studied 61 healthy young women aged 17–25, using proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy to measure levels of GABA and glutamate — the brain's primary inhibitory and excitatory chemical messengers — in three brain regions. They paired this with shotgun metagenomic sequencing of stool samples to map each person's gut microbial genes for producing or breaking down GABA, glutamate, short-chain fatty acids, and other neuroactive compounds. Specific microbial metabolic pathways tracked with GABA and glutamate levels, and different pathways mattered in different brain regions rather than one microbial signature explaining brain chemistry everywhere. The gut-brain connection is widely discussed, but most human evidence has been indirect; this is described as the first study to directly link what gut bacteria are genetically capable of doing chemically to measured levels of specific brain neurotransmitters in living people.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — a direct human study, though observational and limited to young women.
How promising
Combining brain imaging with metagenomic sequencing in the same people is methodologically stronger than prior indirect studies, but it shows association, not causation.
Unknowns
Whether altering these microbial pathways would shift GABA/glutamate balance, and whether the pattern holds in men, older adults, or clinical populations.
Availability
Not a clinical intervention — a mechanism finding, not a therapy or test currently offered.

Molecular Psychiatry, published online August 25, 2026 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42642466/

A Year of Supervised Weightlifting Slowed a Biological "Brain Age" Clock in Older Adults

A randomized controlled trial (Gonzalez-Gomez, Demnitz, Coronel, and colleagues) assigned 309 healthy adults aged 62–70 to different exercise arms, with one group doing supervised heavy resistance training three times a week for a full year at a training center. Using an established "brain age" algorithm derived from MRI scans, the researchers found the resistance-training group's estimated brain age advanced more slowly over the year than control groups doing lighter or no structured exercise. Cardio exercise has long been linked to brain health, but this is a fairly large, year-long randomized trial isolating heavy resistance training specifically, using an objective brain-aging biomarker rather than just cognitive test scores.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — a randomized controlled trial in 309 adults, one of the larger, longer resistance-training-and-brain trials to date.
How promising
Size, randomization and duration make it more convincing than typical pilot studies, but "brain age" clocks remain an imperfect, evolving measure, and real-world cognitive benefit isn't yet shown.
Unknowns
Whether the slower brain-aging trajectory persists beyond one year, and how much depends on the specific heavy-resistance protocol used.
Availability
Not a clinical treatment — supports an already widely available practice (supervised strength training).

GeroScience, published online February 10, 2026 — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11357-026-02141-x

Off-the-Shelf CAR T-Cell Therapy for Lupus Gets $15M State Grant and Moves to Phase 2

Fate Therapeutics announced on September 25, 2026 that the California Institute for Regenerative Medicine (CIRM) awarded it a $15 million grant to advance RECLAIM-LN, a Phase 2 trial of its off-the-shelf (donor-derived, not custom-made per patient) CAR T-cell therapy FT819 in lupus nephritis, a serious kidney complication of lupus. The Phase 2 trial plans to enroll about 53 patients, giving a single infusion of 900 million engineered cells after a chemotherapy conditioning regimen, with the primary goal being complete kidney response at six months. That follows Phase 1 safety data in 16 lupus patients showing no dose-limiting toxicities and no severe cases of the immune side effects (cytokine release syndrome, neurotoxicity) that often complicate CAR T-cell therapies. CAR T-cell therapy has transformed treatment of some blood cancers, and researchers are now testing whether the same cell-engineering approach can reset the immune system in severe autoimmune disease; an "off-the-shelf" version made in advance from donor cells could make the approach far more practical to deliver if it works.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — Phase 1 data exist in 16 patients; Phase 2 enrollment is just beginning.
How promising
The favorable early safety profile is a meaningful hurdle cleared, but Phase 1 tests safety, not efficacy — larger controlled data isn't in yet.
Unknowns
Whether FT819 produces durable kidney-disease remission at a rate beating existing treatments, and its longer-term safety profile.
Availability
Experimental only — available solely through the clinical trial.

IV NAD+ Causes Noticeably Worse Side Effects Than IV Nicotinamide Riboside, Clinic Data Shows

Researchers reviewed electronic medical records from a commercial IV-therapy clinic (Restore Hyper Wellness, Austin, TX) for clients who received four consecutive days of either 500 mg IV NAD+ or IV nicotinamide riboside (NR, a related NAD+ precursor sold under brand names like Niagen), then followed them for 30 days. Every NAD+ recipient reported moderate-to-severe side effects during infusion — including cramping, nausea, vomiting, elevated heart rate, and chest pressure — and infusions took about 97 minutes on average. NR recipients reported only mild, transient effects, and their infusions finished in about 37 minutes, roughly 60% faster. IV NAD+ is marketed at wellness clinics nationwide as an anti-aging and energy-boosting therapy, often at significant cost, but this is one of the few real-world looks at how it's actually tolerated compared with a related but different compound sold for similar claims.

Evidence
Little or no credible evidence for anti-aging benefit; notable tolerability concern (this study addressed side effects, not benefit)
In humans
Yes — a retrospective review of real clinic patients, not a placebo-controlled trial.
How promising
Useful as a safety/comfort comparison between two similar-sounding IV products, but says nothing about whether either delivers advertised longevity benefits.
Unknowns
Why NAD+ causes more acute discomfort than NR at these doses, and whether either compound produces measurable long-term benefit.
Availability
Sold commercially now at wellness clinics — not FDA-approved for any anti-aging indication.

Frontiers in Aging, published online February 2, 2026 — frontiersin.org
