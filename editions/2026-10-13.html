A Finger Ring That Reads Sweat Could Make Continuous Biomarker Tracking Less Invasive

Researchers in Joseph Wang's lab at UC San Diego, led by postdoctoral researcher Tamoghna Saha, built a prototype ring called CHARM (Continuous Health Analyzing Ring Module) that uses a built-in osmotic hydrogel to passively draw sweat from the finger — no exercise or stimulation needed — and an electrochemical sensor array to measure it. The device can track glucose, ketones, vitamin C, uric acid, lactate, and alcohol, though it reads at most four of those at once, and runs about 12 hours on a flexible battery before recharging. The work was published in Nature Communications in late July 2026. In testing that included people with type 1 diabetes, the ring's glucose readings tracked closely with a standard continuous glucose monitor, and its ketone readings matched a blood ketone meter. Most wearables that measure body chemistry, like continuous glucose monitors, require a sensor inserted under the skin — a ring that reads sweat passively could make ongoing biomarker tracking far less invasive, with potential use for diabetes management and broader metabolic monitoring.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — validated against standard CGMs and blood ketone meters in human testing that included people with type 1 diabetes, though this is early-stage prototype testing, not a large clinical trial.
How promising
Matching an established CGM for glucose and a blood meter for ketones in real users is a meaningful validation step, but the ring reads only a handful of markers at once and needs practical upgrades before daily long-term wear.
Unknowns
How accuracy holds up over weeks of continuous wear, how it performs across a larger population, and whether it will reach commercial production.
Availability
Experimental only — a research prototype, not an approved or commercially sold device.

A Head-to-Head Look at NAD+ vs. NR IV Infusions Finds the Pricier One Causes More Side Effects

A retrospective review of electronic medical records from clients of Restore Hyper Wellness, a commercial IV-therapy chain, compared people who received four consecutive days of a 500 mg NAD+ IV infusion against others who received an equivalent nicotinamide riboside (NR) IV infusion, with 30 days of follow-up. The study, published in Frontiers in Aging in 2026 and funded by Restore Hyper Wellness and Niagen Biosciences (which makes NR supplements), found the NAD+ group reported more side effects — nausea, headache, diarrhea, and muscle tightness — than the NR group, which mostly reported mild tingling, slight nausea, and coldness. NAD+ infusions also took roughly 97 minutes on average versus about 37 minutes for NR. Standard safety labs didn't meaningfully change in either group; NR IV, but not NAD+ IV, was linked to a drop in HbA1c at 30 days. NAD+ IV infusions are heavily marketed at wellness clinics as an anti-aging and energy treatment, often at significant out-of-pocket cost — this is a rare head-to-head comparison of tolerability for the two most common infusion options, rather than just a safety claim from a clinic's marketing material.

Evidence
Little or no credible evidence (for any health or anti-aging benefit — this study measured tolerability, not efficacy)
In humans
Yes — a retrospective records review of real clinic clients, not a randomized, placebo-controlled trial.
How promising
Useful as a real-world tolerability comparison, but the retrospective design and funding from the two companies that sell these infusions make it a weak basis for any health-benefit claim.
Unknowns
Whether either infusion provides any measurable benefit to otherwise healthy people, and how the findings would hold up in an independent, blinded trial.
Availability
Already widely available and commercially sold at wellness clinics, despite the lack of efficacy evidence.

New Data From a Small Creatine Trial in Alzheimer's Finds a Cellular Energy Boost — Mostly in Women

Researchers at the University of Kansas Medical Center, led by Matthew Taylor, published a 2026 follow-up analysis of their CABA pilot trial (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05383833), an 8-week, open-label, single-arm study that gave 20 grams per day of creatine monohydrate to 20 people with Alzheimer's disease (35% female). The new paper, in Alzheimer's & Dementia: Diagnosis, Assessment & Disease Monitoring, reports that mitochondrial respiration in blood cells rose in female participants but not male participants, while a separate marker of cellular energy, lymphocyte ATP, rose in both sexes. Brain levels of two other metabolic markers, NAA and glutathione, didn't change. An earlier companion analysis of the same trial had found brain creatine levels rose about 11% and reported a moderate improvement on working-memory tests. Impaired brain energy metabolism is a well-established feature of Alzheimer's disease, and creatine is already a cheap, widely available supplement — this adds early evidence it may have a measurable effect on cellular energy production in people with the disease, with a hint the effect could differ by sex.

Evidence
Mixed / uncertain evidence
In humans
Yes — 20 people with Alzheimer's disease, but in a single-arm pilot with no placebo comparison group.
How promising
The rise in a cellular energy marker and in brain creatine levels suggests the supplement is doing something measurable, but without blinding or a control group the reported cognitive improvement could reflect practice effects rather than a true drug effect; outside neurologists caution the data isn't yet strong enough to recommend creatine for cognition.
Unknowns
Why the mitochondrial effect appeared only in women, whether blood-cell energy changes reflect what's happening in the brain, and whether this translates into slower cognitive decline in a larger trial.
Availability
Creatine monohydrate is sold over the counter, but is not an approved or established Alzheimer's treatment; people with Alzheimer's should talk to a clinician before starting high-dose supplementation.

Estrogen-Only Hormone Therapy Linked to Lower Alzheimer's Risk in New Stanford Analysis

Stanford researchers analyzed data from 21,462 participants, comparing 258 women who reported using estrogen-only menopausal hormone therapy against roughly 2,701 women who reported no hormone therapy use. Women who used estrogen-only therapy had 35% lower odds of developing Alzheimer's disease and 39% lower odds of a dementia diagnosis overall. The study was published in Neurology on August 12, 2026. Estrogen-only therapy is typically prescribed to women who have had a hysterectomy, since adding estrogen without progesterone raises uterine cancer risk in women who still have a uterus. The finding runs counter to the 2003 Women's Health Initiative Memory Study, which found hormone therapy linked to higher dementia risk — a result that shaped two decades of caution. The new study's senior author notes the women in the older trial started therapy later in life, which may have obscured a benefit from earlier use.

Evidence
Mixed / uncertain evidence
In humans
Yes — an observational analysis of 21,462 participants, not a randomized trial.
How promising
The size of the association and its fit with a plausible biological mechanism make it worth taking seriously, but it's an association from observational data, not proof the therapy itself lowers Alzheimer's risk.
Unknowns
Whether the relationship is causal, how it differs for women on combined estrogen-progesterone therapy, and what timing or duration of use matters most.
Availability
Estrogen-only hormone therapy is already FDA-approved for menopause symptom management; using it specifically to reduce Alzheimer's risk is not an approved or established indication.

A Nearly 877,000-Woman Danish Study Finds No Mortality Increase From Menopausal Hormone Therapy

A nationwide Danish registry study led by Anders Pretzmann Mikkelsen of Copenhagen University Hospital Herlev followed 876,805 women born in Denmark between 1950 and 1977, from age 45 through mid-2023 — a median of 14.3 years. About 11.9% of the women had redeemed a prescription for menopausal hormone therapy, and 5.4% died during follow-up. Although crude death rates were higher among hormone-therapy users (54.9 versus 35.5 per 10,000 person-years), after adjusting for other factors the hazard ratio was 0.96 — no increase in all-cause mortality among users — and cause-specific mortality from cardiovascular disease and cancer didn't differ either. Among a small subgroup of 703 women who'd had both ovaries removed between ages 45 and 54, hormone therapy use was linked to 27–34% lower mortality. The study was published in The BMJ on February 18, 2026. Menopausal hormone therapy use has dropped sharply since the early 2000s over mortality and cancer fears; this is one of the largest and longest-running look-backs at whether that use is actually tied to higher real-world death rates.

Evidence
Mixed / uncertain evidence
In humans
Yes — a registry-based cohort of 876,805 women, one of the largest studies of its kind.
How promising
The size and length of follow-up make this a credible, well-powered look at real-world outcomes, but as an observational study it can't rule out "healthy-user bias," and the dataset lacked data on smoking and body mass index.
Unknowns
Whether the reassuring numbers hold up after fully accounting for lifestyle differences between users and non-users, and whether the result applies to women with higher-risk health profiles.
Availability
Menopausal hormone therapy is already FDA-approved and widely prescribed; this study adds to the evidence clinicians and patients can weigh, but doesn't change its approval status.

The BMJ, February 18, 2026 — https://www.bmj.com/content/392/bmj-2025-085998
