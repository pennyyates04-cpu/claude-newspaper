## Transplanted Neural Progenitor Cells Survive a Full Year in Retinitis Pigmentosa Patients

**What happened:** In a phase 1/2a trial funded by the California Institute for Regenerative Medicine (CIRM), researchers gave 13 patients with retinitis pigmentosa a single subretinal injection of CNS10-NPC — a human neural progenitor cell product derived from fetal brain cortex tissue — at one of two doses. New 12-month data were presented at the ISSCR 2026 Annual Meeting.
**Why it matters:** Retinitis pigmentosa is an inherited, progressive retinal disease with no treatment for most patients, and because thousands of different mutations can cause it, gene-specific therapies don't scale. A cell-based approach that doesn't depend on a patient's specific mutation could in principle help a much broader group of patients.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** Yes — phase 1/2a trial, 13 patients, open-label, no placebo arm; designed primarily to test safety and feasibility, not efficacy.
**How promising:** Visual acuity stayed stable throughout the study, and optical coherence tomography imaging confirmed the transplanted cells remained present in the subretinal space for at least a year — a meaningful bar for long-term cell engraftment. Cell-related adverse events included three cases of epiretinal membrane and one persistent subretinal bleb; investigators judged the treatment well tolerated overall.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether long-term engraftment actually slows vision loss or improves function, the ideal cell dose, and how the therapy performs in a larger, controlled trial.
**Availability:** Experimental only — the therapy remains in early-stage clinical trials, with patients now moving into long-term follow-on monitoring.

## Stem-Cell Strategy for Chronic Spinal Cord Injury Moves Toward a Human Trial

**What happened:** At the ISSCR 2026 Annual Meeting, Hideyuki Okano of Keio University presented preclinical efficacy data for a new stem-cell approach to chronic, incomplete spinal cord injury, using clinical-grade gliogenic neural stem/progenitor cells (gNS/PCs). Unlike earlier work that transplanted neuron-generating cells for injuries caught early, this strategy targets patients whose nerve fibers survived but lost their protective myelin coating, aiming to remyelinate them. A physician-initiated clinical trial is now planned to begin recruiting in 2027.
**Why it matters:** There is currently no restorative treatment for chronic spinal cord injury — most patients are told their deficits are permanent once the injury stabilizes. A remyelination-focused strategy specifically designed for the chronic stage, rather than the injury's first weeks, would open treatment to a much larger population of longer-term patients.

**Evidence:** Little or no credible evidence yet in this specific chronic-injury application — so far demonstrated only in preclinical (animal/lab) models.
**Studied in humans:** Not yet for chronic injury; the trial is still in the planning stage. A related earlier trial of neuron-generating cells for subacute (early-stage) spinal cord injury enrolled four patients and reported one-year follow-up in 2025, with no serious treatment-related adverse events and neurological improvement in two of the four, including one patient who regained the ability to stand independently.
**How promising:** A logical, mechanistically distinct next step from the team's earlier subacute-injury work, but efficacy in chronic injury is still unproven outside animal models.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether remyelination in this model translates to functional recovery in chronic human patients, optimal cell dose and delivery, and long-term safety in this new patient population.
**Availability:** Experimental only — human trial recruitment is not expected to begin until 2027.

**Source:** International Society for Stem Cell Research, July 2026 — https://www.isscr.org/isscr-news/stem-cell-strategy-for-chronic-spinal-cord-injury-advances

## Feinstein Institutes Publishes First Comprehensive Map of the Human Vagus Nerve

**What happened:** Researchers at Northwell Health's Feinstein Institutes, led by Dr. Stavros Zanos and funded by a $6.7 million NIH grant, published the REVA (Reconstructing Vagal Anatomy) atlas — the first comprehensive anatomical map of the human vagus nerve, tracing roughly 200,000 individual nerve fibers across 60 nerves from 30 human donors. The team combined physical dissection, ultrasound imaging, fluorescent protein labeling, and micro-CT tracing, and released the dataset publicly through SPARC Science.
**Why it matters:** The vagus nerve is already used therapeutically for epilepsy, depression, and inflammatory conditions, but current stimulation devices activate the whole nerve bundle indiscriminately, causing side effects. A fiber-by-fiber map showing which bundles carry which kinds of signals is the missing reference needed to design electrodes that stimulate only the fibers relevant to a given condition.

**Evidence:** Strong evidence for the anatomical mapping itself (a large, methodologically rigorous, peer-reviewed anatomical dataset); not a treatment claim.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — the atlas is built entirely from human donor tissue (30 donors), not animal models.
**How promising:** A foundational resource rather than a therapy — it doesn't treat anything by itself, but it directly addresses the main technical barrier (imprecise, broad stimulation) limiting current vagus nerve stimulation devices.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether device makers can translate the fiber map into electrodes that reliably target specific fiber populations in living patients, and how much that would actually reduce side effects in practice.
**Availability:** Not a clinical product — it's a reference dataset now available to researchers; any resulting more-precise stimulation devices are still years from clinical use.

**Source:** Northwell Health / Feinstein Institutes, July 27, 2026 — https://feinstein.northwell.edu/news/the-latest/feinstein-institutes-unveils-worlds-first-comprehensive-vagus-nerve-map

## Common Medications May Reshape the Gut Microbiome for Years After You Stop Taking Them

**What happened:** Researchers at the University of Tartu's Institute of Genomics analyzed stool samples and prescription histories from more than 2,500 participants in the Estonian Biobank's microbiome cohort, testing 186 different drugs for lasting associations with gut bacteria composition. Nearly half showed effects that persisted for years after people stopped taking them — most notably antibiotics, beta-blockers, benzodiazepine derivatives, proton-pump inhibitors (common acid-reducers), and antidepressants.
**Why it matters:** Most microbiome research assumes current medication use is what matters, but this suggests a person's drug history — not just what they're taking now — could be quietly shaping their gut bacteria and confounding studies that link the microbiome to disease.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary — a large, well-powered observational cohort study, not an interventional trial.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — over 2,500 people in a real-world biobank cohort, using stool samples and linked prescription records.
**How promising:** A large sample size and a broad drug panel make this a credible signal, and the researchers propose a plausible mechanism (several of these drug classes affect intestinal motility, which shapes bacterial transit time and composition).
**What we don't know yet:** Whether these lasting microbiome shifts actually cause any health effects, how long "years later" really extends, and whether the changes are reversible with intervention.
**Availability:** Not applicable — this is an observational research finding, not a treatment or diagnostic.

**Source:** mSystems (American Society for Microbiology), 2026 — https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/msystems.00541-25

## Antibody Preserves Muscle During Tirzepatide-Driven Weight Loss in Phase 2 Trial

**What happened:** Scholar Rock's phase 2 EMBRAZE trial tested apitegromab, an antibody that blocks myostatin activation, in about 100 adults with overweight or obesity who were also taking the GLP-1/GIP drug tirzepatide. Over 24 weeks, participants got tirzepatide plus either apitegromab (10 mg/kg) or placebo. Results were published in Nature Medicine.
**Why it matters:** GLP-1-class weight-loss drugs cause substantial fat loss but also strip away a meaningful amount of lean muscle mass, which is a growing clinical concern, especially for older patients. A drug that lets people keep more muscle while still losing fat could change how these medications are used.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary — positive phase 2 result, not yet phase 3-confirmed.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — phase 2, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in roughly 100 adults without diabetes.
**How promising:** Apitegromab preserved about 1.9 kg (4.2 lb) more lean mass than placebo — a 54.9% relative reduction in lean-mass loss — while total body weight loss was similar between groups, meaning the extra preservation came specifically from sparing muscle rather than blunting fat loss.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether preserving lean mass this way translates into meaningfully better strength, function, or long-term health outcomes, and how the combination performs in a larger phase 3 trial.
**Availability:** Experimental only — not FDA-approved; adverse events were balanced between groups (76% vs. 71%, with serious adverse events at 2% in each arm and no related deaths), a reassuring but early safety picture.

**Source:** Nature Medicine, June 8, 2026 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04440-4
