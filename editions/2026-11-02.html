First human data on a "cell rejuvenation" gene therapy shows early signs in glaucoma patients

Life Biosciences, co-founded by longevity researcher David Sinclair, announced interim Phase 1 results for ER-100, a gene therapy using a single intravitreal (eye) injection of three "Yamanaka factors" plus daily oral doxycycline to partially reprogram aging retinal cells. In the first three open-angle glaucoma patients, dosed at the lowest level (2×10¹¹ vg), the therapy was well tolerated through day 56 with no dose-limiting toxicities, serious adverse events, or events of special interest. Two of the three patients showed improvement on a standard visual-field test, with at least 28% of test points improving by more than 4 decibels. A safety board has since cleared the trial to escalate to a higher dose. This is the first time a "partial epigenetic reprogramming" therapy — an approach that tries to reverse cellular aging markers without erasing a cell's identity — has been dosed in humans at all, after years of promising but preclinical mouse and primate data.

Promising but preliminary

Yes — a first-in-human, open-label Phase 1 trial with no control group; results so far cover only 3 of up to 18 planned participants

The clean safety readout at eight weeks is the headline result; the visual-field improvement in 2 of 3 patients is encouraging but far too small a sample to call a treatment effect
Unknowns

A rapamycin-plus-exercise trial undercuts a popular longevity claim

RAPA-EX-01, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial published in the Journal of Cachexia, Sarcopenia and Muscle, gave 40 sedentary adults aged 65–85 either weekly 6 mg sirolimus (rapamycin) or placebo alongside an identical 13-week home exercise program (resistance "chair stands" plus stationary cycling). Both groups improved on the primary measure, but sirolimus did not add benefit: the intention-to-treat difference was -2.13 chair-stand repetitions favoring placebo (95% CI -4.61 to 0.34; p=0.089), and some pre-specified sensitivity analyses found placebo significantly outperformed rapamycin. The rapamycin group also had more total adverse events (99 vs. 63), including one possibly drug-related serious infection. Rapamycin is widely promoted in longevity circles as a way to amplify the benefits of exercise through mTOR inhibition; this is one of the first controlled human trials to test that specific combination directly, and it found no benefit — with a hint of possible interference.

Little or no credible evidence, specifically for the claim that rapamycin boosts exercise-driven strength and endurance gains in older adults

Not promising as currently used for this purpose; the authors call the trial exploratory given its size, single dose, and 13-week duration

A topical "senolytic" cream shows early, company-reported promise for psoriasis and eczema

Rubedo Life Sciences reported preliminary Phase 1 results for RLS-1496, a topical cream that modulates GPX4 to push disease-driving senescent ("zombie") skin cells toward ferroptosis, a form of programmed cell death. In a single-center, randomized, double-blind, vehicle-controlled EU trial of roughly 44 patients across three dose strengths, the company says it met its primary safety and tolerability endpoint. Psoriasis cohorts showed a dose-dependent average 20% reduction in epidermal thickness after one month, and in a separate atopic-dermatitis cohort, 25% of RLS-1496 patients had a clinically meaningful drop in itch score versus none on vehicle. Senescent cells are thought to drive both chronic inflammatory skin disease and visible skin aging, so a topical drug built around clearing them would be a concrete bridge between aging biology and everyday dermatology, rather than a systemic anti-aging pill.

Promising but preliminary

NAD+ IV drips cause more side effects than a cheaper alternative — and neither has proven anti-aging benefits

A retrospective review of wellness-clinic records, published in Frontiers in Aging in February 2026, compared patients who received four consecutive days of 500 mg NAD+ IV infusions against patients who received the same regimen with nicotinamide riboside (NR) instead. The NAD+ group reported more side effects during infusion — including gastrointestinal symptoms, faster heart rate, and chest pressure — and their infusions took roughly 97 minutes on average, versus about 37 minutes for NR. Neither group showed significant changes in liver, kidney, or thyroid markers over 30 days of follow-up. The study was funded in part by Restore Hyper Wellness and Niagen Biosciences, which sells the NR product used in the comparison. NAD+ IV therapy is marketed heavily as an anti-aging and energy treatment, often costing hundreds of dollars per session, despite lacking FDA approval for any indication and despite this study — like most NAD+ IV research to date — measuring tolerability rather than any actual health benefit.

Evidence
Little or no credible evidence for anti-aging or longevity benefit; mixed/uncertain evidence even on which IV formulation is better tolerated, given the funding conflicts
In humans
Yes, but only as a small retrospective chart review of real-world patients, not a trial designed to test whether either infusion actually works
How promising
Not promising as an anti-aging therapy based on current data; the main finding is that NAD+ IV is harder on the body to deliver than NR IV, not that either extends healthspan
Unknowns
Whether IV infusion meaningfully raises NAD+ levels inside cells at all, and whether any clinical outcome beyond lab safety markers improves
Availability
Widely available now at wellness clinics nationwide, used off-label with no FDA-approved indication

Largest study yet on strength training and lifespan finds a clear, modest dose works best

Harvard researchers followed more than 147,000 adults across three long-running cohort studies for up to 30 years, publishing their analysis in the British Journal of Sports Medicine in June 2026. Compared with doing no resistance training, people who did 90–119 minutes of resistance training per week had a 13% lower risk of death from any cause, a 19% lower risk of cardiovascular death, and a 27% lower risk of death from neurological disease. The benefit leveled off above roughly 120 minutes per week. People who also did substantial aerobic activity (45+ MET-hours weekly) had 53–58% lower mortality risk regardless of how much resistance training they did. Strength training is one of the cheapest, most accessible interventions in the "exercise as medicine" category, and this is one of the largest and longest-running looks yet at how much of it is actually associated with a longer life.

Evidence
Strong evidence for the association, drawn from a large, long-running dataset; it's observational, so it cannot prove the training itself caused the lower mortality
In humans
Yes — a large observational cohort study, not a randomized trial
How promising
Reinforces a pattern seen across many smaller studies, with a useful new detail: benefit appears to plateau around two hours per week, so more volume isn't clearly better
Unknowns
The exact mechanism, whether intensity or type of resistance training matters, and the standard observational caveat that healthier or wealthier people may simply be more likely to lift weights regularly
Availability
Not a medical treatment requiring approval — a lifestyle recommendation already accessible to most people
