## Stem-Cell Heart Therapy Misses Its Goal in Pivotal Infant Trial, but Safety Signal Holds

**What happened:** Longeveron announced topline results from ELPIS II, a Phase 2b trial testing its stem-cell product laromestrocel as an add-on to standard heart surgery in 40 infants with hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS), a fatal birth defect in which the heart's left side is critically underdeveloped. Infants received a single intramyocardial dose of laromestrocel or surgery alone during a routine palliative procedure (the Glenn or hemi-Fontan operation) and were followed for 12 months. The trial did not meet its primary endpoint — change in right ventricular ejection fraction at month 12 showed almost no difference between groups (−0.7 percentage points; 95% CI −7.3 to 5.9; p=0.83).
**Why it matters:** HLHS is one of the deadliest congenital heart defects, and current surgical palliation alone leaves many children facing heart failure or transplant. A stem-cell adjunct that improved heart function could change how these infants are managed long-term — but this trial shows that promise from smaller, earlier studies didn't translate into a clear functional benefit here.

**Evidence:** Mixed / uncertain evidence — this is a randomized, controlled Phase 2b trial, but it missed its primary efficacy endpoint.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — 40 infants with HLHS, randomized 1:1 to treatment or standard-of-care surgery.
**How promising:** The efficacy signal on heart function was essentially flat, but there were no deaths among treated infants over 12 months versus one death in the control group, which the company says warrants a closer look at the full dataset with the FDA rather than an outright program stop.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether secondary and exploratory endpoints (still being analyzed) show any benefit, and what the FDA will conclude from a meeting on next steps.
**Availability:** Experimental only — this was a pivotal trial toward potential approval, now complicated by the missed primary endpoint.

## A Gene-Activation Tool Half the Size of CRISPR Could Widen What In Vivo Gene Therapy Can Reach

**What happened:** Stanford Medicine researchers, led by ophthalmology professor Yang Sun, developed TIGRa, an ultra-compact programmable gene activator built to fit inside a single AAV (adeno-associated virus) — the workhorse delivery vehicle for in vivo gene therapy. At under half the size of dSpCas9-based CRISPR activators, TIGRa can switch on up to 12 genes at once from one compact construct. In a mouse model of retinal injury, a single AAV-delivered dose of TIGRa activated a neuroprotective gene (CaMKII) directly inside the eye, helping retinal ganglion cells survive and preserving visual function.
**Why it matters:** Many CRISPR-based gene-activation tools are too bulky to package into a single AAV, the main way gene therapies are currently delivered into the body, forcing researchers toward split-vector workarounds that are less efficient. A smaller, multiplexed tool like TIGRa could make single-shot, in vivo gene therapies feasible for a wider range of diseases, including ones needing multiple genes turned on at once. It's a platform advance, not a treatment for one disease — its real test will be whether it works as well in other tissues and eventually in humans.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary — peer-reviewed and mechanistically clean, but the therapeutic result (protecting vision) has so far only been shown in mice.
**Studied in humans:** No — mouse retinal-injury model only.
**How promising:** The compactness and multiplexing capability address a real bottleneck in gene therapy delivery, making this a notable tools paper, but efficacy in a disease-relevant human context hasn't been tested.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether TIGRa performs as well in other organs and larger animals, its long-term safety profile, and how it compares head-to-head with existing CRISPR activators in efficacy and off-target effects.
**Availability:** Basic/preclinical research only — a laboratory tool, not a clinical product.

**Source:** Cell Stem Cell, published August 10, 2026 (Stanford Medicine coverage) — https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2026/08/tigra-compact-gene-editing-tool

## NAD+ Levels in Blood Don't Actually Track With Age, Undercutting a Popular IV-Drip Rationale

**What happened:** A narrative review published in Frontiers in Aging examined the clinical evidence behind intravenous NAD+ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) and its precursors, a fast-growing wellness-clinic offering marketed for energy, recovery, and anti-aging. The review highlights a large cross-sectional analysis (Trętowicz et al.) showing that whole-blood NAD+ concentrations were not independently associated with chronological age once disease burden and comorbidity were factored in — echoing a separate seven-cohort, 303-person study that found no consistent NAD+ decline with age once blood-handling artifacts were corrected for.
**Why it matters:** "Boosting declining NAD+ levels" is the core marketing pitch for NAD+ IV drips, which can cost hundreds of dollars per session at wellness clinics. If NAD+ doesn't reliably decline with age in the way assumed, that specific rationale for IV NAD+ as an anti-aging intervention weakens considerably, even though NAD+ still plays a real role in cellular energy metabolism.

**Evidence:** Mixed / uncertain evidence for the anti-aging marketing claim — solid underlying data on the NAD+/age relationship, but the review itself synthesizes existing (mostly small, non-placebo-controlled) studies rather than new clinical trial data.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — the underlying NAD+/age data comes from human cross-sectional cohorts; the review also covers small human IV-NAD+ tolerability studies.
**How promising:** Not promising for the anti-aging rationale specifically, though the review notes NAD+ IV therapy may still have narrower, unproven uses under investigation; overall clinical evidence for wellness use remains thin.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether IV NAD+ produces any measurable functional or longevity benefit in properly controlled human trials, independent of the age question, and what (if anything) predicts who might respond.
**Availability:** Not FDA-approved for anti-aging or wellness use; widely sold off-label at IV therapy clinics.

**Source:** Frontiers in Aging, published September 10, 2026 — https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/aging/articles/10.3389/fragi.2026.1887175/full

## A Single Workout Measurably Changes How the Brain Handles Mental Tasks

**What happened:** Researchers from Japan's AIST, working with UT Southwestern Medical Center, used repeated fMRI scans to track brain activity in healthy young and older adults before and after a single session of aerobic exercise, while participants repeated the same cognitive task multiple times. By separating the effect of exercise itself from the effect of simply practicing the task, the team found aerobic exercise measurably altered regional brain activity patterns during the cognitive task, and that in older adults, a bigger drop in brain activity with repeated practice tracked with better task performance — suggesting more efficient neural processing rather than decline.
**Why it matters:** Much of the "exercise is good for your brain" research relies on activity trackers or long-term outcomes; this adds direct, controlled neuroimaging evidence of an acute brain-activity change from a single workout, and a method for telling exercise effects apart from simple task-practice effects — a distinction earlier studies often blurred.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary — a controlled, well-designed crossover neuroimaging study, though likely a modest sample typical of fMRI research.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — both young and older healthy adults, using live fMRI before and after acute exercise.
**How promising:** Mechanistically informative and methodologically careful, but it's a single-session, lab-based effect; it doesn't yet show whether repeated exercise sessions produce lasting cognitive benefits.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether the acute brain-activity changes seen here translate into durable improvements in memory or executive function, and how exercise intensity or duration affects the size of the effect.
**Availability:** Not a clinical intervention — basic research; exercise itself is of course freely available today.

**Source:** Journal of Magnetic Resonance Imaging, published September 2, 2026 (EurekAlert coverage) — https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1140933

## First NHS-Run Trial Finds Single-Dose Psilocybin Eases Treatment-Resistant Depression

**What happened:** King's College London researchers ran the first publicly funded, placebo-controlled UK trial testing psilocybin-assisted therapy inside Britain's National Health Service. In this randomized, double-blind Phase 2 feasibility trial, 60 adults with treatment-resistant major depression (an inadequate response to at least two prior antidepressants, or one antidepressant plus therapy) received a single 25-mg dose of psilocybin or placebo, each paired with structured psychological support before, during, and after dosing. The single dose was feasible to deliver within routine NHS care and produced large preliminary reductions in depressive symptoms among the psilocybin group.
**Why it matters:** Most psilocybin depression trials to date have run in well-resourced private research settings. Showing the treatment can be delivered feasibly inside a public health system, with a genuine placebo arm, is a practical step toward psilocybin therapy someday being offered as routine care rather than remaining confined to specialty trial centers.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary — a real randomized, placebo-controlled trial, but explicitly a small feasibility study, not designed to be definitive on efficacy.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — 60 adults with treatment-resistant depression in an NHS clinical setting.
**How promising:** The symptom reduction was described as large, and delivering it within standard public healthcare infrastructure is itself a meaningful feasibility result, but the trial was not sized or designed to confirm durability or cost-effectiveness.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether the benefit holds up in larger, longer-term efficacy trials, how durable the effect is beyond the initial follow-up window, and what it would cost to deliver at scale within a public health system.
**Availability:** Experimental only — psilocybin is not an approved depression treatment in the UK or US outside of clinical trials.

**Source:** Nature Medicine, published 2026 (King's College London coverage) — https://www.kcl.ac.uk/news/promising-results-from-first-publicly-funded-uk-randomised-trial-of-psilocybin-for-depression
