A Gene Therapy Designed to Make Aging Eye Cells Younger Shows an Early Safety Signal in Glaucoma Patients

Life Biosciences announced on October 8, 2026 interim Phase 1 data for ER-100, an experimental therapy built on partial cellular reprogramming — activating a small set of genes associated with restoring a more youthful state to aged cells. ER-100 is injected into one eye, then switched on by a course of oral doxycycline taken for about eight weeks, so the reprogramming genes stay active only while the drug is being taken. Three participants with open-angle glaucoma had been dosed and followed through day 56. The company reported no dose-limiting toxicities, serious adverse events, or adverse events of special interest; it attributed all reported side effects to the injection procedure, a steroid, or participants' pre-existing conditions rather than to ER-100 itself. Two of the three participants showed improvement in portions of their visual field by day 56. Based on these early safety results, an independent data safety monitoring board recommended escalating to a higher dose in the ongoing trial, which plans to enroll up to 18 people with glaucoma or NAION (a form of optic nerve damage) across four U.S. sites. This is among the first attempts to test partial cellular reprogramming directly in people rather than only in mice. Glaucoma and related optic neuropathies damage the optic nerve in ways current treatments can't reverse, so any therapy that restores lost visual function — rather than just slowing further loss — would be a notable departure if the signal holds up.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — three people with open-angle glaucoma in an open-label Phase 1 safety trial; the trial is not designed or powered to establish efficacy.
How promising
Tolerable so far, with a visual field improvement in two of three early participants, but outside experts note ER-100 doesn't lower eye pressure — the main driver of glaucoma damage — so any benefit may not address the disease's root cause or prove durable.
Unknowns
Whether the visual improvements persist and replicate in more patients, and the therapy's long-term cancer risk, a general concern for approaches that reactivate embryonic-type genes.
Availability
Experimental only — an ongoing, dose-escalating Phase 1 trial.

Blocking a Single Receptor Calms Inflammation in Living Human Brain Tissue — and the Drugs to Do It Already Exist

Researchers at the University of Birmingham, led by Professor Nicholas Barnes, tested P2X7 receptor-blocking compounds on living human brain tissue obtained during neurosurgery, as well as on human microglia-like immune cells grown from blood monocytes. Blocking the P2X7 receptor significantly reduced the release of inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, from both tissue types. The findings were published in the journal Brain in 2026. Because several P2X7-blocking compounds have already gone through human safety testing for other conditions, the authors argue they could be repurposed and moved toward brain-disease trials faster than an entirely new drug could. Chronic neuroinflammation is a feature of a wide range of brain conditions — including Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, traumatic brain injury, and some psychiatric disorders — but most anti-inflammatory drugs tested in the brain so far have failed in clinical trials. Demonstrating the effect directly in living human brain tissue, rather than only in mouse models, is a more direct way to show a mechanism is relevant to human biology.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Partially — real human brain tissue and immune cells were tested in the lab (ex vivo), not in living patients; no clinical trial has been run yet.
How promising
The human-tissue result strengthens the case the mechanism translates to people, and existing, already-tested P2X7 blockers could shorten the path to a trial — but it remains a laboratory-tissue finding, not a demonstrated patient benefit.
Unknowns
Whether blocking P2X7 in living patients actually helps any of the targeted conditions, which existing compound to repurpose, and at what dose.
Availability
Experimental only — laboratory research; no P2X7 blocker is approved for any brain condition.

Aging Cells Use a Cancer-Style Immune Checkpoint to Hide From the Body's Defenses, Cedars-Sinai Finds

Researchers at Cedars-Sinai, led by Selim Chaib, found that senescent cells — aging cells that stop dividing but don't die, and accumulate with age — raise levels of a protein called PD-L2 on their surface, which appears to help them evade clearance by the immune system. PD-L2 is already known from cancer immunotherapy, where a related checkpoint protein helps tumor cells hide from immune attack. In older mice, genetically removing or blocking PD-L2 reduced the buildup of senescent cells and lowered the inflammatory signals those cells produce, and was linked to better insulin sensitivity and grip strength compared with untreated older mice. The study was published in Cell Metabolism, with Cedars-Sinai's release dated September 10, 2026. Senescent cells are a major target of the "senolytic" anti-aging field because their buildup is linked to inflammation, frailty, and age-related disease. Most current senolytic drugs try to kill these cells directly; this finding points to a different approach — blocking the signal that lets them hide — and raises the possibility PD-L2 could double as a blood marker for tracking senescent-cell burden.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
No — findings are from mice; no human trial has tested blocking PD-L2 for aging-related purposes.
How promising
The parallel to a validated cancer-immunotherapy mechanism gives it real plausibility, and pairing fewer senescent cells with functional gains in mice is more convincing than a marker change alone — but the leap to people, especially given checkpoint-blockade's known immune side effects in cancer care, is unproven.
Unknowns
Whether blocking PD-L2 in humans would meaningfully cut senescent-cell burden or improve healthspan, and whether it carries the same autoimmune-type risks seen with checkpoint cancer drugs.
Availability
Experimental only — a preclinical mouse finding; no PD-L2-targeting therapy is in human development.

Cedars-Sinai, September 10, 2026 — cedars-sinai.org

In Older Adults, High-Intensity Intervals — Not Moderate Exercise — Preserved Muscle While Cutting Fat

Researchers at the University of the Sunshine Coast in Australia randomly assigned 123 healthy older adults (averaging about 72 years old) to six months of supervised treadmill-based moderate-intensity training, high-intensity interval training (HIIT), or a low-intensity active control, with three 45-minute sessions per week. Both the moderate- and high-intensity groups lost more fat mass than the control group, by similar amounts. But only the moderate-intensity group also lost lean (muscle) mass along with the fat; the HIIT group lost fat without losing lean mass. The findings, from a sub-study of a larger randomized trial, were published in the journal Maturitas, with ScienceDaily covering the results on October 7, 2026. Older adults who lose weight through diet or moderate exercise often lose muscle alongside fat, which can worsen age-related frailty even as the scale number drops. If HIIT reliably avoids that tradeoff, it would be a meaningful, practical distinction for how older adults should exercise for body composition, not just general fitness.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — a randomized, supervised six-month trial in 123 healthy older adults.
How promising
The randomized design and reasonably large sample make this a credible signal, and it fits a plausible mechanism, but it's one trial in healthy volunteers, and the researchers say it needs confirmation.
Unknowns
Whether the pattern holds in less healthy or less fit older adults, what the lean-mass difference actually reflects, and how durable the effect is beyond six months.
Availability
Not a medical treatment — HIIT is a widely accessible form of exercise.

ScienceDaily (University of the Sunshine Coast), October 7, 2026 — sciencedaily.com

A Wearable Sensor That Tracks Cortisol Around the Clock Moves Toward FDA Review

Adaptyx Biosciences, a Stanford spinout, presented first-in-human data for a wearable sensor that continuously measures free cortisol from interstitial fluid just beneath the skin, using programmable DNA-based molecular switches that bind the hormone and generate a continuous electrical signal. In one study, the wearable's readings tracked paired venous blood cortisol during a controlled oral hydrocortisone challenge; in a second, overnight study, the sensor captured a person's natural cortisol rhythm — including the typical morning cortisol-awakening rise and overnight low point — during normal daily activity. The company says its human testing program has now collected more than 400 hours of in-body monitoring data and that it is pursuing FDA submission as a Class II device. Cortisol is central to stress physiology, sleep, and metabolic health, but it's currently measured mainly through single-point blood, saliva, or urine tests that miss its natural rise and fall over the day. A validated continuous cortisol sensor could do for stress-hormone monitoring something like what continuous glucose monitors did for diabetes.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — two small first-in-human studies: a controlled hormone-challenge comparison against blood draws, and an overnight natural-rhythm monitoring study.
How promising
Tracking blood cortisol during a controlled challenge and picking up the expected daily rhythm are meaningful early steps, but detailed accuracy data hasn't been published in a peer-reviewed journal, and the device has no FDA clearance yet.
Unknowns
Accuracy across a broader range of cortisol levels and real-world conditions, performance over weeks of continuous wear, and the FDA review timeline.
Availability
Experimental only — in development, not yet FDA-cleared or commercially available.
