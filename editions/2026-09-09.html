Skipping Exercise at Work Doesn't Count the Same as Exercising for Fun, Large Dementia Study Finds

A team led by David Raichlen at the USC Dornsife College of Letters, Arts and Sciences pooled 74 cohort and case-control studies covering more than 4.2 million people ages 45–93 across 30 countries, with a median follow-up of about 10 years, comparing dementia risk between people who got their physical activity through leisure versus through demanding jobs. Public health advice generally treats "physical activity" as one interchangeable bucket, but this is the first systematic test of whether the well-known "physical activity paradox" — where occupational activity doesn't bring the same heart-health benefits as leisure activity — also applies to dementia risk.

Evidence
Mixed / uncertain evidence — a very large pooled analysis, but built from observational studies, not a randomized trial.
In humans
Yes — entirely human cohort and case-control data, no animal models involved.
How promising
Leisure-time activity was linked to a 24% lower dementia risk; occupational activity to a 20% higher risk, plausibly explained by low autonomy, stress, and pollution exposure bundled into many physical jobs.
Unknowns
Whether the link is causal or reflects confounding differences (education, income, healthcare access) between the two groups, and the exact biological mechanism.
Availability
Not applicable — an epidemiological finding, not a treatment.

The Lancet Public Health / USC, August 2026 — https://today.usc.edu/why-you-move-not-just-how-often-could-affect-dementia-risk/

Semaglutide Slowed Molecular Aging Clocks in a Randomized HIV Trial

A UC San Diego–led team ran a post hoc epigenetic analysis of a 32-week, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 2b trial (NCT04019197) of semaglutide in adults with HIV-associated lipohypertrophy (semaglutide n=45, placebo n=39; 84 participants had usable paired blood samples), measuring several validated "epigenetic clocks" that estimate biological aging pace from DNA methylation. GLP-1 drugs like semaglutide (Ozempic/Wegovy) are known for weight loss and metabolic benefits; this is early randomized evidence they might also measurably slow biological aging itself.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary — a real randomized trial, but this analysis was exploratory, not the pre-specified primary endpoint.
In humans
Yes — 84 adults with HIV-associated lipohypertrophy, a narrow population, not the general public.
How promising
Semaglutide slowed the DunedinPACE pace-of-aging clock by about 9% versus placebo, with significant decreases across several other clocks including PCGrimAge (−3.1 years) and PhenoAge (−4.9 years) — a consistent pattern across independent measures.
Unknowns
Whether these clock changes translate into real reductions in disease or mortality, whether the effect holds in people without HIV, and durability beyond 32 weeks.
Availability
Semaglutide is FDA-approved for diabetes and obesity, not "anti-aging" — this remains a research finding.

Nature Communications, May 19, 2026 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-72861-3

Your Gut "Species" May Actually Be Several Different Bacteria — and Some Might Be Contagious

Researchers at the University of Vienna's Centre for Microbiology and Environmental Systems Science, led by Martin Polz with lead author Xiaoqian Annie Yu, applied a genomic technique called "reverse ecology" to large gut microbiome datasets and found that many bacterial groups long classified as single species actually contain several evolutionarily distinct sub-populations, each adapted to different gut conditions. Microbiome research usually treats a "species" as one unit, but if it's really several health-relevant sub-populations lumped together, current microbiome-disease links could be missing the real signal.

Evidence
Strong evidence for the underlying genomic finding (large dataset, peer-reviewed in Nature); promising but preliminary for the disease-association and transmission claims.
In humans
Yes — built from human gut metagenomic data, but a computational/observational analysis, not an intervention.
How promising
Several sub-populations correlated with advanced age, inflammatory bowel disease, colorectal cancer, and type 2 diabetes; some competitive sub-populations spread across large geographic distances within decades, suggesting person-to-person transmission shapes the gut microbiome more than assumed.
Unknowns
Whether these sub-populations cause the diseases they correlate with or merely mark them, and how much transmission versus shared environment explains the geographic spread.
Availability
Not a clinical product — a foundational research finding; sharper biomarkers or targeted treatments are still years away.

Nature / University of Vienna, May 2026 — https://www.univie.ac.at/en/news/press-room/press-releases/detail/evolutionary-processes-shape-bacterial-populations-in-the-human-gut

NAD+ Anti-Aging Marketing Has Outrun the Evidence, NPR Review Finds

NPR examined the booming market for NAD+ pills, injectables, and IV infusions — marketed as able to reverse aging, sharpen skin, and deliver "clean energy" — against what human trials actually show. Aging researchers, including Christopher Martens of the Delaware Center for Cognitive Aging Research, described the marketing as having gotten ahead of the science. This falls squarely under the evidence-strength rule: NAD+ decline with age is real biology, but that doesn't mean current commercial products deliver the benefits being sold.

Evidence
Mixed / uncertain evidence overall.
In humans
Yes, but mostly small trials of oral NAD+ precursors (NR, NMN); IV and injectable NAD+ have less human data.
How promising
Some small trials found benefits in specific groups — women with prediabetes, newly diagnosed Parkinson's patients, people with peripheral artery disease — but other trials, including on general metabolic health, failed to reproduce effects seen in animals. One researcher: "The data in humans are pretty iffy right now."
Unknowns
Whether any benefit applies to healthy, average adults, the right dose and form, and effects of repeated long-term IV use.
Availability
Already widely available as unregulated supplements and clinic infusions — access has outpaced evidence. IV infusions appear safe in small studies but can cause abdominal cramping, diarrhea, nausea, and vomiting.

NPR, May 11, 2026 — https://www.npr.org/2026/05/11/nx-s1-5813664/nad-infusions-supplements-longevity-science

A Single Dose of Creatine Blunted Cognitive Decline From a Sleepless Night

Researchers led by Ali Gordji-Nejad at Forschungszentrum Jülich in Germany gave 29 healthy adults a single 0.2 g/kg dose of creatine monohydrate or a placebo, then tested their cognition repeatedly during 21 hours of sleep deprivation. Creatine is a cheap, widely available supplement mostly associated with muscle and strength; this adds evidence it may also support brain energy metabolism under acute metabolic stress, such as sleep loss.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary.
In humans
Yes — a small (29-person), randomized, controlled human study.
How promising
Creatine blunted sleep-deprivation-related decline in logical and numerical reasoning, language-related processing speed, and sustained attention, with women showing larger benefits than men — linked by the authors to women's lower baseline creatine stores.
Unknowns
Whether the effect holds with repeated dosing or chronic (rather than one-off) sleep deprivation, and the optimal dose for different people.
Availability
Creatine monohydrate is already available over the counter; using it specifically for sleep-deprivation cognitive support is not an approved medical indication.

Nutrients, April 10, 2026 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13119191/
