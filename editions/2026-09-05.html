Stem-Cell-Derived Dopamine Neurons Show Early Promise for Parkinson's Disease

Researchers published 12-month results from the STEM-PD trial, a phase 1/2 open-label study that transplanted a cryopreserved, off-the-shelf dopamine-producing cell product — grown from human embryonic stem cells — directly into the brains of eight Parkinson's patients. Parkinson's is caused by the loss of dopamine-producing neurons; this is among the first human tests of a mass-producible cell therapy meant to replace them, rather than just treating symptoms with medication.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — phase 1/2 trial, 8 patients, no placebo control
How promising
Six of seven surviving patients cut their medication substantially; PET scans showed signs cells survived at 6 and 12 months, no serious cell-related side effects
Unknowns
Durability beyond a year, best-responding patients, optimal dose, performance vs. placebo. One patient died of an unrelated pulmonary infection
Availability
Experimental only

World's First Neural Stem Cell Trial for Huntington's Disease Doses Its First Patient

Researchers dosed the first participant in REGEN4HD, a phase 1b/2a trial testing hNSC-01 — a neural stem cell therapy derived from pluripotent stem cells — as a treatment for Huntington's disease, with an update presented at the ISSCR 2026 Annual Meeting. Huntington's has no treatment that slows or reverses the underlying brain degeneration; this is the world's first attempt to replace or support lost brain cells with transplanted neural stem cells in Huntington's patients.

Evidence
Little or no credible evidence yet — only one patient treated so far
In humans
Yes, but just 1 of a planned 21 participants; tolerated the transplant and immunosuppression well, elected a second transplant in the opposite hemisphere
How promising
Historically significant first step, but this stage tests only safety and tolerability, not efficacy
Unknowns
Any clinical benefit, long-term safety, whether the safety board keeps clearing new participants

Lilly's Retatrutide Delivers Largest Sustained Drug-Driven Weight Loss on Record

Eli Lilly reported new 104-week extension data from its Phase 3 TRIUMPH-1 obesity trial of retatrutide, a once-weekly injectable that activates three separate hormone receptors (GLP-1, GIP, and glucagon). Participants with a starting BMI of 35 or higher who stayed on the 12 mg dose for the full 104 weeks lost an average of 85 pounds, or 30.3% of body weight — weight loss that kept increasing rather than plateauing, an encouraging sign of durability that pushes drug-based results closer to outcomes previously seen mainly with bariatric surgery.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary — strong Phase 3 data, not yet an approved use
In humans
Yes — Phase 3 trial in adults with obesity/overweight without diabetes; 104-week extension included 532 higher-BMI participants
How promising
Among the strongest weight-loss results reported for any pharmacologic agent in a large trial to date
Unknowns
Long-term cardiovascular outcomes, weight regain after stopping, cost/access, head-to-head performance vs. tirzepatide/semaglutide
Availability
Not yet FDA-approved

Pomegranate Compound Urolithin A Reverses Heart-Stiffening Damage — in Mice and Lab-Grown Tissue

Researchers at King's College London found that urolithin A — a compound gut bacteria produce from substances in pomegranates, walnuts, and berries — improved heart function by up to 80% in mice engineered to mimic heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), and improved contraction and relaxation speed in heart tissue grown from human stem cells. HFpEF is a common, hard-to-treat form of heart failure where the heart stiffens and can't relax properly; this identifies a new, non-nitric-oxide molecular pathway (activating a protein called PKGI) that could open a fresh drug target.

Evidence
Mixed / uncertain — promising mechanism, but preclinical
In humans
No — tested in male mice and heart tissue grown from human stem cells (single donor line), not in human patients
How promising
Notable effect size in animals and a plausible new mechanism, but the authors caution this is early-stage
Unknowns
Whether it works in humans or in female mice (HFpEF disproportionately affects women, only male mice tested), proper human dosing
Availability
Experimental only — not a reason to think eating pomegranates treats heart disease

FDA Authorizes First Wearable to Track Both Glucose and Ketones

The FDA granted Abbott's Libre Duo 10 Day system De Novo marketing authorization on August 25, 2026 — the first wearable biosensor to continuously monitor both glucose and ketone levels in a single device, for patients with diabetes aged 2 and up. Rising ketones combined with high blood sugar can signal diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), a life-threatening complication; combining both measurements in one wearable could give an earlier warning than glucose monitoring alone.

Vagus Nerve Stimulation After Practice Strengthens Long-Term Motor Learning — in Mice

Researchers at Tohoku University found that stimulating the vagus nerve in mice right after they practiced a visual-tracking eye-movement task didn't improve performance during training itself, but produced stronger long-term learning on later days — an effect linked to rhythmic changes in brain blood volume following stimulation. It suggests the vagus nerve, already used therapeutically for epilepsy and depression, may work partly by tuning blood flow rhythms that support memory consolidation after practice.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
No — mouse study using a cuff electrode on the vagus nerve
How promising
A plausible, well-controlled mechanistic finding, but a long way from human application
Unknowns
Whether the effect occurs in humans, whether it generalizes beyond this task, needed stimulation timing/dosing
Availability
Not applicable — basic animal research, not a treatment
