## Implanted Vagus-Nerve Stimulator Shows Durable Benefit and Less Bone Damage in Rheumatoid Arthritis

**What happened:** The pivotal RESET-RA trial of SetPoint Medical's implanted neuroimmune-modulation device — which stimulates the vagus nerve to dial down inflammatory signaling — randomized 242 people with moderate-to-severe rheumatoid arthritis who hadn't responded to or couldn't tolerate advanced drugs (biologics or JAK inhibitors) to active stimulation or a sham device for three months, then gave everyone open-label active stimulation and followed them to 12 months. The 3-month primary result favored active stimulation (35.2% vs. 24.2% achieving an ACR20 response), and that response grew to 50.0% at 6 months and 52.8% at 12 months. In a prespecified subgroup at high risk of joint damage, new or worsening bone erosion by 3 months occurred in 18.9% of stimulated patients versus 37.8% on sham. The full results were published in Nature Medicine; the device has since received FDA approval.
**Why it matters:** It's one of the strongest clinical demonstrations yet that directly manipulating the nervous system — rather than suppressing the immune system with another drug — can meaningfully treat an autoimmune disease, and it's now an approved option rather than just a research finding.
**Evidence:** Strong evidence
**Studied in humans:** Yes — a 242-person randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled pivotal trial with 12-month follow-up.
**How promising:** Genuinely promising for a hard-to-treat population: roughly half of patients who'd exhausted other advanced therapies had a meaningful response sustained to a year, and the erosion data suggest it may also slow joint damage, not just symptoms.
**What we don't know yet:** Long-term durability beyond 12 months, how it compares head-to-head against switching to a different biologic, and which patients respond best.
**Availability:** Clinically available — FDA-approved for adults with moderate-to-severe RA who've failed advanced DMARD therapy.

**Source:** Nature Medicine, January 5, 2026 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-04114-7

## Senolytic Drug Combo Reverses Liver Scarring Markers in Small Human Trial

**What happened:** Researchers ran a phase 2, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of the senolytic combination dasatinib plus quercetin (D+Q) — drugs designed to clear senescent "zombie" cells that accumulate with age and drive inflammation — in 31 adults with fibrotic MASH (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis, the more severe form of fatty liver disease). Participants took D+Q or placebo three days a week for three weeks, repeated over three 7-week cycles. Single-cell sequencing of liver tissue showed the D+Q group had fewer senescent and fibrotic cell signatures, and MASH resolved in 53% of the D+Q group versus 7% on placebo. The study was published in Nature Metabolism on October 1, 2026.
**Why it matters:** This is a proof-of-principle that clearing senescent cells with existing, repurposed drugs can produce a measurable improvement in a real human disease — not just a biomarker shift in a lab dish or mouse.
**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** Yes — a 31-person randomized controlled trial, small but placebo-controlled and biopsy-confirmed.
**How promising:** The resolution-rate gap (53% vs. 7%) is large for a trial this size, but adverse events were also much more common with D+Q (82% vs. 43%), though described as self-limiting.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether the benefit holds up in a larger trial, the right dose and duration, and whether the side-effect burden is acceptable outside a closely monitored study.
**Availability:** Experimental only — dasatinib and quercetin are each individually approved for other uses (leukemia; a dietary supplement, respectively), but this specific combination protocol for liver disease is not an approved treatment.

**Source:** Nature Metabolism, October 1, 2026 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s42255-026-01643-4

## UCLA Study Links Gut Bacteria and Blood Chemistry to Faster Brain Aging — Before Symptoms Appear

**What happened:** UCLA Health researchers analyzed resting-state brain scans from nearly 1,500 adults across three study groups, building a model that estimates "brain age" from how different brain regions communicate with each other. The gap between that estimated age and a person's actual age — the Brain Aging Index (BAI) — was higher in people with specific gut bacteria, certain fatty-molecule and cholesterol-related metabolites, and lower levels of the hormone estetrol. A higher BAI also tracked with worse working memory and executive function and more frequently reported depression symptoms. The study was published in eBioMedicine in September 2026.
**Why it matters:** It adds specific, measurable gut-chemistry signatures to the idea that brain aging is visible — and potentially gut-related — long before someone would notice symptoms, which is what makes early intervention plausible in principle.
**Evidence:** Mixed/uncertain evidence
**Studied in humans:** Yes — nearly 1,500 adults, but the design is cross-sectional (a snapshot), not a trial testing whether changing the microbiome changes brain aging.
**How promising:** The associations are consistent across several measures (bacteria, metabolites, memory, mood), which strengthens the pattern, but correlation here doesn't establish that the gut changes are a cause of brain aging rather than a downstream effect of it.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether altering these specific gut bacteria or metabolites would actually slow brain aging, and how much of the link is confounded by diet, weight, or other shared factors.
**Availability:** Not a clinical test or treatment — a research finding; no product or intervention stems from it yet.

**Source:** UCLA Health / eBioMedicine, September 2026 — https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261001214106.htm

## Smartwatch Data Plus Basic Blood Tests Predicts Insulin Resistance Better Than Blood Tests Alone

**What happened:** The WEAR-ME study combined wearable-device data (resting heart rate, daily step counts, sleep duration) with routine blood biomarkers (triglycerides, HDL cholesterol) and demographics in 1,165 adults (median age 45, median HbA1c 5.5%) who also had a gold-standard insulin-resistance measurement (HOMA-IR). Using a wearable "foundation model" pretrained on 40 million hours of sensor data, adding the wearable data to a model built on demographics, fasting glucose, and a lipid panel raised predictive accuracy (AUROC) from 0.76 to 0.88. The work was published in Nature in 2026.
**Why it matters:** Insulin resistance usually isn't caught until blood sugar is already abnormal; a model that flags it earlier using data many people already generate passively on a smartwatch could let people intervene — through diet or exercise — well before prediabetes.
**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** Yes — 1,165 adults with directly measured insulin resistance, not just a proxy.
**How promising:** The jump in predictive accuracy from adding wearable data is substantial and was validated against a real physiological measurement, which is a meaningfully higher bar than most consumer wearable-health claims.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether this holds up across more diverse populations and device types, and whether acting on the prediction (earlier lifestyle intervention) actually improves outcomes.
**Availability:** Not available as a consumer product — a research model, not a feature shipped in any wearable app yet.

**Source:** Nature, 2026 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10179-2

## A Lab Compound Extends Lifespan Over 25% in Three Species — and a Separate Study Pours Cold Water on IV NAD+

**What happened:** Two unrelated findings on the longevity-and-nutrient-therapy beat: First, researchers showed that compound 991, which directly switches on the energy-sensing enzyme AMPK, extended lifespan by more than 25% in fruit flies, roundworms, and fission yeast, and produced a "pro-longevity" protein signature — more mitochondria-building activity, less growth-signaling (mTOR) activity — in the livers of treated mice. It was published in Aging Cell on September 29, 2026. Separately, a retrospective study comparing IV infusions of NAD+ against NR (nicotinamide riboside, a precursor the body converts to NAD+) found NAD+ infusions caused more moderate-to-severe side effects — nausea, elevated heart rate, chest pressure — than NR infusions, which caused only mild, brief symptoms and could be given about 60% faster. It was published in Frontiers in Aging in February 2026.
**Why it matters:** The AMPK finding is an early but genetically validated lead on a druggable longevity target; the NAD+ finding is a reminder that IV "anti-aging" infusions — increasingly sold directly to consumers — haven't been shown in rigorous human trials to do anything for longevity, and the NAD+ form specifically may be harder on the body than alternatives.
**Evidence:** Little or no credible evidence (IV NAD+ anti-aging claims); Mixed/uncertain evidence (AMPK activation as a longevity strategy)
**Studied in humans:** AMPK/compound 991 — no, tested in flies, worms, yeast, and mice only. IV NAD+ vs. NR — yes, but a small retrospective tolerability study, not a trial testing any longevity or health benefit.
**How promising:** The AMPK effect size is large and backed by genetic knockout experiments confirming the mechanism, but it's unproven in any mammal beyond a 3-week proteomic snapshot. For IV NAD+, a recent systematic review found no eligible outcome trials at all for anti-aging or wellness claims in humans.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether AMPK activation would extend lifespan or healthspan in mammals, and whether any form of IV NAD+ or NR delivers a measurable longevity or energy benefit in healthy people beyond raising a blood marker.
**Availability:** Compound 991 is experimental only, not in human trials. IV NAD+ and NR infusions are both already sold commercially at wellness clinics, despite the lack of outcome data.

**Source:** Aging Cell, September 29, 2026 — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/acel.70721; Frontiers in Aging, February 2026 — https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/aging/articles/10.3389/fragi.2026.1652582/full
