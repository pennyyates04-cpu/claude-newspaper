## Daily Cortisol Rhythm, Not Just Its Average Level, Tracks Cognitive Decline in Older Adults

**What happened:** Researchers analyzed salivary cortisol samples taken at three points across the day (morning, afternoon, evening) in 3,895 older adults (2,503 Black, 1,392 White; average age 76.7) from the Chicago Health and Aging Project, following participants for up to 11 years. People with greater variation in cortisol across the day tended to show slower cognitive decline, while those with the highest sustained overall cortisol levels tended to decline faster. Black participants had flatter daily cortisol curves than White participants, but the pattern linking cortisol shape to cognitive decline looked similar across both groups. The associations with Alzheimer's disease risk specifically were less clear than the associations with general cognitive decline. The study was published in *JAMA Network Open* in September 2026.
**Why it matters:** Chronic stress and dysregulated cortisol have long been suspected contributors to cognitive aging, but this is one of the largest and most racially diverse cohorts to track the daily cortisol *rhythm* — not just a single blood draw — against long-term cognitive outcomes, suggesting the shape of the daily curve carries information beyond the average level.

**Evidence:** Mixed / uncertain evidence
**Studied in humans:** Yes — observational cohort of 3,895 older adults followed for up to 11 years
**How promising:** A well-powered, long-running cohort finding, but it's associational — it identifies a pattern linked to decline, not a cause, and doesn't establish that intervening on cortisol rhythm would change outcomes
**What we don't know yet:** Whether flattening or amplifying the daily cortisol curve through behavioral or medical intervention would actually slow decline, and why the pattern didn't clearly track Alzheimer's-specific risk
**Availability:** Not a treatment — salivary cortisol testing is already clinically available for other purposes, but using daily cortisol rhythm to predict or manage cognitive decline is not a validated clinical practice

## First Controlled Trial of Rapamycin Plus Exercise Finds No Added Benefit — and a Possible Modest Downside

**What happened:** In the first randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial to pair weekly rapamycin (sirolimus) with a structured exercise program, researchers randomized 40 sedentary adults aged 65–85 to 6 mg sirolimus or matched placebo once weekly for 13 weeks, while both groups followed the same home-based chair-stand and stationary-bike program three times a week. Both groups improved on chair-stand performance, but sirolimus did not outperform placebo; a sensitivity analysis found sirolimus modestly underperformed placebo (a complete-case difference of −2.46 repetitions, p=0.045). Sirolimus was also linked to more adverse events, including one serious case of pneumonia judged possibly related to the drug. The trial, led by Stanfield and colleagues, was published in the *Journal of Cachexia, Sarcopenia and Muscle* in April 2026.
**Why it matters:** Rapamycin is one of the most talked-about longevity drugs and is already used off-label by biohackers, partly on the theory that it amplifies exercise's benefits. This is the first controlled human test of that specific combination, and it found no added benefit — and a signal of possible harm to exercise gains — directly challenging a popular self-experimentation practice.

**Evidence:** Little or no credible evidence (for the exercise-enhancing claim specifically — the trial itself is a small but rigorous negative result)
**Studied in humans:** Yes — 40 adults aged 65–85, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, over 13 weeks
**How promising:** Not promising for this specific use; a rigorous small trial found no benefit and a possible modest downside, though the sample is small enough that larger trials could still refine the picture
**What we don't know yet:** Whether a different dose, different timing relative to exercise days, or longer duration would change the result, and whether rapamycin's other claimed longevity effects (unrelated to exercise) hold up on their own
**Availability:** Rapamycin is FDA-approved for organ-transplant rejection and certain other indications; it is not approved for aging or exercise enhancement and is used for that purpose only off-label

**Source:** Peter Attia MD, summarizing Stanfield et al., *Journal of Cachexia, Sarcopenia and Muscle*, April 2026 (DOI 10.1002/jcsm.70274) — https://peterattiamd.com/rapamycin-plus-exercise-trial/

## Large Yale Study Finds Nearly Half of Older Adults Improve Physically or Cognitively Over Time — and Positive Age Beliefs Predict Who

**What happened:** Using data from 11,314 community-dwelling adults aged 65 and older in the Health and Retirement Study, Yale researchers Becca Levy and Martin Slade tracked walking speed and cognitive performance over up to 12 years of follow-up. About 45% of participants improved in cognition, walking speed, or both (32% improved cognitively, 28% physically) rather than simply declining. People who held more positive beliefs about aging at baseline were significantly more likely to show these improvements, even after adjusting for age, sex, education, chronic disease, depression, and a genetic Alzheimer's risk marker (APOE). The study was published in the journal *Geriatrics* in March 2026.
**Why it matters:** Most aging research assumes decline is the default trajectory; this study's scale — over 11,000 people followed for up to 12 years — makes a harder case that meaningful improvement is common, and that a psychological factor like self-perception of aging is independently associated with it, a potentially modifiable target distinct from diet, exercise, or drugs.

**Evidence:** Mixed / uncertain evidence
**Studied in humans:** Yes — 11,314 adults 65+, followed for up to 12 years
**How promising:** A large, well-adjusted observational association, but the authors themselves note it shows correlation, not causation — people who are already aging well may simply develop more positive beliefs, rather than the beliefs causing the improvement
**What we don't know yet:** Whether deliberately shifting someone's age beliefs through intervention would causally produce physical or cognitive improvement, and what biological mechanism, if any, would explain such an effect
**Availability:** Not a treatment — there is no approved "age-belief" intervention; small trials of age-belief interventions exist but are not validated clinical care

**Source:** *Geriatrics* (MDPI), March 2026 — Levy & Slade, "Aging Redefined: Cognitive and Physical Improvement with Positive Age Beliefs," DOI 10.3390/geriatrics11020028 — https://doi.org/10.3390/geriatrics11020028

## UTMB Launches Trial Testing Whether a Weight-Loss Drug Can Slow Biological Aging Itself

**What happened:** University of Texas Medical Branch researchers, funded by the Moody Endowment, launched the "Moody Longevity Trial," testing whether tirzepatide (the GLP-1/GIP drug marketed as Zepbound) can slow biological aging. Participants aged 55 to 70 take the drug for 24 weeks, followed by a 12-week observation period after stopping, while researchers track biological age using DNA-methylation ("epigenetic") clocks alongside physical performance, mood, and inflammation markers. Lead investigators have said they hope to see biological aging slow by 10–15%, but the trial is still underway and has not reported results. UTMB announced the trial in February 2026.
**Why it matters:** GLP-1 drugs are already reshaping cardiology and metabolic medicine; this is one of the first trials designed specifically to test their effect on biological aging itself, rather than just weight or blood sugar — including whether any benefit persists after stopping the drug, a question with real-world importance given these drugs' cost and the fact that most patients don't stay on them indefinitely.

**Evidence:** Little or no credible evidence (no results yet — this is a trial in progress, not a finding)
**Studied in humans:** Trial underway in humans; no results reported yet
**How promising:** Too early to say — this evaluates a hoped-for effect, not a demonstrated one; the "10–15% slowing" figure is the researchers' stated goal, not a measured outcome
**What we don't know yet:** Essentially the entire outcome — whether tirzepatide affects biological-age markers at all, and whether any effect would persist after stopping the drug
**Availability:** Tirzepatide is already FDA-approved and clinically available for diabetes and weight loss; using it specifically to slow aging is not an approved use and remains unproven

**Source:** UTMB News, February 23, 2026 — https://www.utmb.edu/news/article/utmb-news/2026/02/23/aging-under-the-microscope-in-utmb-moody-longevity-trial

## Rheumatologist Argues GLP-1 Drugs May Fight Inflammation Directly, Not Just Through Weight Loss

**What happened:** At the Congress of Clinical Rheumatology West in September 2026, Dr. Leonard Calabrese of the Cleveland Clinic presented an argument, drawing on existing trial data, that GLP-1 receptor agonist drugs (such as semaglutide and tirzepatide) may reduce inflammation directly and independent of the weight loss they cause, potentially through a proposed "brain-immune axis" mechanism. He suggested this could eventually support using these drugs as broader anti-inflammatory or anti-aging treatments, beyond diabetes and obesity.
**Why it matters:** If GLP-1 drugs do have a genuine weight-independent anti-inflammatory effect, it would help explain reported benefits in conditions like heart failure and kidney disease that go beyond what weight loss alone would predict, and would strengthen the case for studying these drugs as general anti-inflammatory agents rather than purely metabolic ones.

**Evidence:** Little or no credible evidence (a conference presentation and interpretive argument, not a new primary trial)
**Studied in humans:** Not tested as a standalone claim here — it's a mechanistic hypothesis built on secondary signals from existing GLP-1 trials
**How promising:** Biologically plausible given a growing body of secondary findings (reduced inflammatory markers in cardiovascular and kidney trials of these drugs), but this specific presentation is expert interpretation, not new primary data
**What we don't know yet:** Whether the anti-inflammatory effect is truly independent of weight loss and calorie restriction, what the actual mechanism is, and whether it would justify prescribing these drugs for inflammation alone
**Availability:** GLP-1 drugs are already FDA-approved and widely available for diabetes and obesity; prescribing them specifically for anti-inflammatory or anti-aging effects is not an approved use
