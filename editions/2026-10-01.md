## Bone Marrow Transplant Lets Healthy Cells "Donate" Mitochondria to Rescue a Mitochondrial Disease

**What happened:** Researchers at Stanford Medicine gave bone marrow transplants to mice with a model of Friedreich's ataxia, an inherited disease in which a gene defect cripples mitochondria (the cell's energy-producing structures), causing progressive nerve damage and heart failure. Using a combination of chemotherapy conditioning and a drug that clears out the animals' own immune cells, the team replaced the mice's resident microglia (brain immune cells) and tissue macrophages with donor cells from healthy bone marrow. Those donor cells then physically transferred their own healthy mitochondria to neighboring neurons, other brain cells, and heart muscle cells. The work was published in Nature Communications in 2026.
**Why it matters:** Friedreich's ataxia currently has no treatment that addresses its root cause. If intercellular mitochondrial transfer — effectively using transplanted immune cells as mitochondria "couriers" — works in a mitochondrial disease, it could point to a broader strategy for other conditions where failing energy production drives tissue damage.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** No — mouse model only.
**How promising:** The treated mice showed better coordination, muscle strength, growth, survival, and heart function than untreated mice, with donor mitochondria measurably boosting energy production (ATP synthesis and oxidative phosphorylation) in both brain and heart tissue — a clear, multi-organ signal in animals.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether the same microglia/macrophage-replacement approach is safe and effective in humans, who would need to tolerate the conditioning regimen used to clear out their own immune cells, and whether a disease already present at birth can be meaningfully reversed once damage has accumulated.
**Availability:** Experimental only — not in human trials yet.

**Source:** Nature Communications, 2026 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-76775-y

## Study Pinpoints Why Exercise Reverses Muscle Aging: It's the Mitochondria

**What happened:** Researchers combined several mouse models — including a strain engineered to lack mitochondrial function specifically in muscle — with a cross-sectional study of muscle biopsies from 30 human donors ranging from 17 to 99 years old. The goal was to test whether exercise's well-known ability to preserve muscle strength and reduce frailty in aging actually depends on changes to mitochondria. The study was published in PNAS in March 2026.
**Why it matters:** Exercise is already recommended for healthy aging, but this helps explain the mechanism: when the researchers blocked mitochondrial function in muscle, old mice stopped getting the usual functional benefits from exercise, meaning the mitochondrial remodeling isn't just a side effect — it's apparently required for exercise to work.
**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** Yes, for the correlational piece — biopsies from 30 human donors linked mitochondrial function to age and physical capacity; the causal mitochondria-blocking experiment was in mice only.
**How promising:** Strong mechanistic evidence in animals (structural, enzymatic, and functional mitochondrial remodeling tracked with exercise-driven improvements, and removing mitochondrial function removed the benefit), corroborated by a consistent pattern in human tissue.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether a drug or intervention that mimics this mitochondrial remodeling without exercise could produce the same benefit, and how the finding translates to specific exercise types, doses, or clinical populations.
**Availability:** Not a clinical treatment — exercise itself is already available to anyone; this is new evidence for why it works.

**Source:** PNAS, March 2026 — https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2508286123

## Largest Trial Yet of a GLP-1 Drug for Alzheimer's Comes Up Empty

**What happened:** Novo Nordisk's EVOKE and EVOKE+ trials — phase 3, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled studies run across 566 sites in 40 countries — tested oral semaglutide (up to 14 mg daily) in roughly 3,800 adults aged 55–85 with amyloid-confirmed, early-stage Alzheimer's disease. At the two-year mark, change in the CDR-SB cognitive/functional scale was essentially identical between semaglutide and placebo in both trials (differences of −0.08 and 0.10 points, not statistically significant). The results were published in The Lancet on March 19, 2026.
**Why it matters:** GLP-1 drugs like semaglutide (Ozempic/Wegovy) have generated enormous interest for potential benefits beyond weight loss and diabetes, including neuroprotection. This is the largest, most rigorous test of that idea in Alzheimer's to date, and it was negative on the primary outcome — an important, if disappointing, result for a much-hyped drug class.
**Evidence:** Disproven for this specific use (oral semaglutide as an early-Alzheimer's treatment) — though the drug's safety profile was consistent with prior trials.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — two phase 3 RCTs totaling roughly 3,800 patients, the gold standard of clinical evidence.
**How promising:** Not promising as an Alzheimer's treatment. In a biomarker subgroup of about 200 patients, semaglutide did significantly reduce markers linked to neuroinflammation, but researchers say that biological signal didn't translate into any detectable slowing of cognitive or functional decline.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether GLP-1 drugs could help at an earlier disease stage, in combination with other therapies, or via a different formulation — but as tested here, the hypothesis did not hold up.
**Availability:** Semaglutide is clinically available for diabetes and weight loss, but not approved or shown effective for Alzheimer's.

**Source:** The Lancet, March 19, 2026 — https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(26)00459-9/fulltext

## Adding a Daily Probiotic to Antidepressants Modestly Eases Depression in Older Adults

**What happened:** In the PRODG trial, researchers randomized 58 adults aged 60 and older with moderate depression to receive either a daily probiotic (containing Lactobacillus helveticus and Bifidobacterium longum) or a placebo for 12 weeks, on top of their existing antidepressant treatment. The multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial was published in the Journal of the American Geriatrics Society in June 2026.
**Why it matters:** The gut-brain axis — the idea that gut bacteria influence mood via inflammation, the vagus nerve, and neurotransmitter production — is a major area of nervous-system research, but most human evidence in depression has come from smaller or lower-quality studies. This is a randomized, placebo-controlled test specifically in older adults, a population often underrepresented in psychiatric trials.
**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** Yes — 58 participants, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicenter.
**How promising:** Modest — both groups improved substantially on their existing antidepressants, and the probiotic group showed slightly greater additional improvement in depression and anxiety symptoms than placebo, a real but not dramatic effect.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether the effect holds up in larger samples, whether other probiotic strains or doses work better or worse, and how durable the benefit is after stopping the probiotic.
**Availability:** The probiotic strains used are commercially available, but this specific adjunctive use for depression in older adults is not an established clinical protocol.

**Source:** Journal of the American Geriatrics Society, June 2026 — https://www.medicaldaily.com/probiotics-depression-anxiety-older-adults-clinical-trial-jags-june-2026-475688

## Genetic Study Finds Testosterone Trades Longevity for Fertility — But Only in Men

**What happened:** Using Mendelian randomization — a method that uses naturally occurring genetic variation to approximate a randomized experiment — researchers analyzed genetic instruments for testosterone in 184,205 men and 199,569 women from the UK Biobank, testing testosterone's relationship to lifespan (proxied by parental attained age), fertility (number of children), and blood pressure. The study was published in The Aging Male in 2026.
**Why it matters:** It's long been debated whether testosterone is protective or harmful for long-term health, with wellness clinics increasingly marketing testosterone "optimization" to aging men. This study tests a specific evolutionary hypothesis — that testosterone trades off survival for reproductive success — using genetics rather than observational data, which helps sidestep the usual confounding (e.g., sicker men having lower testosterone to begin with).
**Evidence:** Mixed/uncertain (genetic association, not a drug trial — doesn't prove testosterone therapy itself shortens life)
**Studied in humans:** Yes — a genetic association study in nearly 384,000 UK Biobank participants.
**How promising:** The pattern was sex-specific and internally consistent: genetically higher testosterone predicted shorter lifespan and higher blood pressure in men, and also predicted having more children in men — but showed no association with either lifespan or fertility in women.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether this lifelong genetic effect applies to adults who start testosterone therapy later in life (a different biological scenario), and the exact mechanism linking testosterone to blood pressure and mortality risk.
**Availability:** Not a treatment — a genetic epidemiology finding relevant to how testosterone therapy and "optimization" are evaluated.

**Source:** The Aging Male, 2026 — https://doi.org/10.1080/13685538.2026.2717455
