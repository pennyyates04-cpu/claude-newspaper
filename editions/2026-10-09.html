Eli Lilly's Triple-Hormone Drug Retatrutide Posts the Largest Weight-Loss Numbers Yet in a Phase 3 Trial

Eli Lilly's phase 3 TRIUMPH-1 trial tested retatrutide — a drug that mimics three gut hormones (GLP-1, GIP, and glucagon) at once — in 2,339 adults with obesity or overweight who did not have type 2 diabetes. Participants were randomized 1:1:1:1 to 4mg, 9mg, or 12mg of retatrutide, or placebo, in a double-blind design over 80 weeks. At 80 weeks, the 12mg group lost an average of 28.3% of body weight versus 2.2% on placebo. In a prespecified extension to 104 weeks, limited to 532 participants with a baseline BMI of at least 35 kg/m² who tolerated the drug, the 12mg group's average reduction reached 30.3%. Lilly announced the results in company and conference disclosures starting in May 2026. It's the largest weight-loss figure reported for any drug trial in this class to date, exceeding earlier GLP-1 therapies and approaching the range typically seen with bariatric surgery.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — a randomized, placebo-controlled phase 3 trial of 2,339 adults over 80 weeks, with a 104-week extension in a smaller subgroup.
How promising
The scale, randomization, and consistent dose-response make this a credible signal, but the headline 104-week figure comes from a subgroup that had already shown it could tolerate the drug — which likely inflates the number relative to all comers — and the results have so far been disclosed by the sponsor rather than confirmed in a peer-reviewed paper we could independently verify.
Unknowns
Long-term safety beyond 104 weeks, whether weight returns after stopping the drug, and the full peer-reviewed data once published.
Availability
Experimental only — retatrutide is not yet FDA-approved for any indication.

A Muscle-Preserving Combo Could Fix GLP-1 Drugs' Biggest Side Effect

The phase 2 BELIEVE trial tested bimagrumab — an antibody that blocks the activin receptor, part of the myostatin pathway that normally limits muscle growth — added to semaglutide in 507 adults with obesity, across placebo, bimagrumab-alone, semaglutide-alone, and combination arms, over a 48-week primary period with an open-label extension to 72 weeks. Results were published in Nature Medicine in early March 2026. The combination group lost more total weight than semaglutide alone (roughly 17.8 kg versus 14.2 kg) while losing proportionally more of it as fat: muscle mass fell only modestly in the combination group versus a larger drop with semaglutide alone, and it actually increased with bimagrumab by itself. Rapid GLP-1-driven weight loss often strips away lean muscle along with fat — a particular concern for older or frail patients — and this is one of the first trials to show a way to blunt that side effect without giving up the weight-loss benefit.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — 507 adults in a randomized phase 2 trial over 48 weeks, with an open-label extension to 72 weeks.
How promising
Consistent, directionally aligned effects across weight, fat mass, muscle mass, and inflammatory markers support the underlying mechanism, but this is a mid-sized, 48-week phase 2 study — it wasn't designed or powered to show whether preserved muscle mass translates into preserved strength or function.
Unknowns
Whether the muscle-mass benefit holds up in a larger phase 3 trial, whether it translates into better physical function, and bimagrumab's long-term safety profile.
Availability
Experimental only — bimagrumab is investigational and not an approved obesity treatment.

A Small Trial Finds an 8-Hour Eating Window Eased Crohn's Disease Without Cutting Calories

Researchers at the University of Calgary and University of British Columbia (Natasha Haskey, Maitreyi Raman, and colleagues), funded by the Crohn's & Colitis Foundation, randomized 35 adults with Crohn's disease and overweight or obesity to either an 8-hour daily eating window or a standard eating schedule, with no instruction to cut calories, over 12 weeks. The results were published in Gastroenterology in February 2026. The time-restricted group showed roughly 40% lower disease activity and about 50% less abdominal discomfort than controls, lost an average of about 5.5 lbs (versus a roughly 3.7 lb gain in controls), and showed reduced blood markers tied to inflammation (leptin, PAI-1) along with improved gut-microbiome diversity. It's one of the first controlled trials to show that meal timing alone — without reducing how much people eat — can meaningfully ease an inflammatory bowel disease, pointing to a low-cost, drug-free lever clinicians could test further.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — 35 adults, randomized, over 12 weeks.
How promising
The randomized design and the size of the measured effect are encouraging, but the trial is small and enrolled only people who were already overweight or obese, so it's unclear how broadly the benefit generalizes.
Unknowns
Whether the improvement holds beyond 12 weeks, the exact mechanism, and whether the approach is safe for Crohn's patients who are underweight or have more severe disease — groups generally advised against prolonged fasting.
Availability
Not a clinical treatment — this is a dietary pattern, not a drug; people with IBD should talk to a clinician before changing their eating schedule.

An Implanted Vagus Nerve Stimulator Clears a Phase 3 Bar for Rheumatoid Arthritis That Drugs Had Missed

The RESET-RA trial, a double-blind, sham-controlled phase 3 study, tested an implanted vagus-nerve-stimulation device (the SetPoint System) in 242 patients with moderate-to-severe rheumatoid arthritis who had not adequately responded to, or could not tolerate, biologic or targeted synthetic drugs. At 3 months, 35.2% of the active-stimulation group reached the primary endpoint — ACR20, a standard measure of at least 20% symptom improvement — versus 24.2% of the sham-implant group. In the open-label follow-up, about half of patients reached ACR20 by 6 months, rising past 52% by 12 months, with a 4-year extension still under way. The trial was published in Nature Medicine in January 2026 (Tesser et al.). It's a rare phase 3 success for a device-based approach that works by electrically dialing down the body's own inflammatory signaling — the "inflammatory reflex" — rather than adding another drug, offering a potential option for patients who have run out of medication choices.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — 242 patients, randomized, sham-controlled, with a 3-month primary endpoint, 12-month open-label follow-up, and an ongoing 4-year extension.
How promising
A statistically significant result from a blinded, sham-controlled trial in a hard-to-treat population is a notably higher bar than most device studies clear, and the effect appeared to grow through open-label follow-up.
Unknowns
Long-term durability and safety beyond the ongoing 4-year extension, how it compares head-to-head with switching to a different biologic drug, and which patients are most likely to respond.
Availability
Not yet broadly available for this specific indication in routine rheumatology care; patients should ask a rheumatologist about current access and trial status.

A 104,000-Person Study Finds Men and Women's Bodies Age on Different Schedules

A team led by the China National Center for Bioinformation, the Institute of Zoology at the Chinese Academy of Sciences, and Xuanwu Hospital of Capital Medical University analyzed routine clinical data — 172 common measurements such as weight, cholesterol, and blood sugar — from 104,208 adults ages 18 to 98 across three medical centers in China, building separate "aging clock" models for men and women. Men's biggest metabolic shift (rising BMI, triglycerides, and cholesterol) clustered around ages 35 to 45, while women's major shift came later, around menopause (roughly ages 55 to 65), tied to hormonal change; physical differences between the sexes tended to shrink as both aged further. Markers including glucose, lipids, uric acid, and two tumor-associated proteins (CEA, HE4) tracked with faster biological aging in both sexes. The study was published in Nature Aging on August 25, 2026. It suggests aging doesn't hit everyone on the same timeline, and that sex-specific checkpoints — the mid-30s to 40s for men, perimenopause for women — might be more useful moments for preventive screening than a one-size-fits-all age cutoff.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — over 104,000 adults, among the largest such analyses to date, though the design is observational.
How promising
The sample size and reliance on routine, widely available lab panels make the approach easy to replicate and apply broadly, but the findings are correlational — they show when biomarkers shift, not what causes the shift or whether intervening at those windows changes outcomes.
Unknowns
Whether acting on these checkpoints (earlier screening or intervention around ages 35–45 for men, around menopause for women) actually improves long-term health outcomes, and how well the clock generalizes outside Chinese clinical populations.
Availability
Not a clinical test — it's a research model built from existing lab panels, not a commercially available product.
