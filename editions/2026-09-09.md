## Skipping Exercise at Work Doesn't Count the Same as Exercising for Fun, Large Dementia Study Finds

**What happened:** A team led by David Raichlen at the USC Dornsife College of Letters, Arts and Sciences pooled 74 cohort and case-control studies covering more than 4.2 million people ages 45–93 across 30 countries, with a median follow-up of about 10 years. They compared dementia risk between people who got their physical activity through leisure (exercise, sports, recreation) versus through demanding jobs (manual labor, repetitive physical work).
**Why it matters:** Public health advice generally treats "physical activity" as one interchangeable bucket, but this is the first systematic test of whether the well-known "physical activity paradox" — where occupational activity doesn't bring the same heart-health benefits as leisure activity — also applies to dementia risk.

**Evidence:** Mixed / uncertain evidence — a very large pooled analysis, but built from observational studies, not a randomized trial.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — entirely human cohort and case-control data, no animal models involved.
**How promising:** Leisure-time physical activity was associated with a 24% lower dementia risk, while occupational physical activity was associated with a 20% higher dementia risk. The authors suggest occupational activity is often repetitive and low-autonomy, and comes bundled with psychosocial stress and pollution exposure — all independently linked to dementia risk.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether the association is causal or reflects underlying differences between people in these two groups (education, income, healthcare access, job stress) that weren't fully accounted for, and the exact biological mechanism connecting job-based activity to brain risk.
**Availability:** Not applicable — this is an epidemiological finding, not a treatment, but it reframes "get more exercise" advice around context, not just volume.

**Source:** The Lancet Public Health / USC, August 2026 — https://today.usc.edu/why-you-move-not-just-how-often-could-affect-dementia-risk/

## Semaglutide Slowed Molecular Aging Clocks in a Randomized HIV Trial

**What happened:** A UC San Diego–led team ran a post hoc epigenetic analysis of a 32-week, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 2b trial (NCT04019197) of semaglutide in adults with HIV-associated lipohypertrophy (semaglutide n=45, placebo n=39, with 84 participants having usable paired blood samples). They measured several validated "epigenetic clocks" that estimate biological aging pace from DNA methylation patterns.
**Why it matters:** GLP-1 drugs like semaglutide (Ozempic/Wegovy) are known for weight loss and metabolic benefits; this is early randomized evidence that they might also measurably slow biological aging itself, not just its downstream risk factors.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary — a real randomized trial, but this specific analysis was exploratory and not the trial's pre-specified primary endpoint.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — 84 adults with HIV-associated lipohypertrophy, a narrow patient population, not the general public.
**How promising:** Semaglutide slowed the DunedinPACE pace-of-aging clock by about 9% relative to placebo, and produced significant decreases across several other clocks, including PCGrimAge (−3.1 years) and PhenoAge (−4.9 years) — a consistent pattern across multiple independent measures, which strengthens the signal.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether these clock changes translate into real reductions in disease or mortality, whether the effect holds in people without HIV, and how durable it is beyond 32 weeks.
**Availability:** Semaglutide is FDA-approved for diabetes and obesity, not for "anti-aging" — this remains a research finding, not a new clinical use.

**Source:** Nature Communications, May 19, 2026 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-72861-3

## Your Gut "Species" May Actually Be Several Different Bacteria — and Some Might Be Contagious

**What happened:** Researchers at the University of Vienna's Centre for Microbiology and Environmental Systems Science, led by Martin Polz with lead author Xiaoqian Annie Yu, applied a genomic technique called "reverse ecology" to large gut microbiome datasets. They found that many bacterial groups long classified as single species are actually composed of several evolutionarily distinct sub-populations, each adapted to different conditions inside the gut.
**Why it matters:** Microbiome research and diagnostics usually treat a bacterial "species" as one unit, but if it's really several health-relevant sub-populations lumped together, current microbiome-disease links could be missing the real signal — and the way these sub-populations spread between people may matter more than assumed.

**Evidence:** Strong evidence for the underlying genomic finding (large dataset, peer-reviewed in Nature); Promising but preliminary for the disease-association and transmission claims.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — built from human gut metagenomic data, though this was computational/observational analysis, not an intervention.
**How promising:** Several of the identified sub-populations correlated with advanced age, inflammatory bowel disease, colorectal cancer, and type 2 diabetes, and some highly competitive sub-populations appeared to spread across large geographic distances within just a few decades — evidence that person-to-person transmission, not just diet and lifestyle, may shape which bacteria establish themselves in the gut.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether these sub-populations cause the diseases they correlate with or are just markers of them, and how much transmission versus shared environment explains the geographic spread.
**Availability:** Not a clinical product — a foundational research finding that could eventually sharpen microbiome biomarkers or targeted treatments, but that's still years away.

**Source:** Nature / University of Vienna, May 2026 — https://www.univie.ac.at/en/news/press-room/press-releases/detail/evolutionary-processes-shape-bacterial-populations-in-the-human-gut

## NAD+ Anti-Aging Marketing Has Outrun the Evidence, NPR Review Finds

**What happened:** NPR examined the booming market for NAD+ pills, injectables, and IV infusions — now popular with celebrities and biohackers and marketed as able to reverse aging, sharpen skin, and deliver "clean energy" — against what human trials actually show. Aging researchers, including Christopher Martens of the Delaware Center for Cognitive Aging Research, described the marketing as having gotten ahead of the science.
**Why it matters:** This falls squarely under the guidelines' evidence-strength rule: NAD+ decline with age is real biology, but that doesn't mean current commercial products deliver the benefits being sold.

**Evidence:** Mixed / uncertain evidence overall.
**Studied in humans:** Yes, but mostly in small trials of oral NAD+ precursors (NR, NMN); IV and injectable NAD+ have less human data.
**How promising:** Some small trials found benefits in specific groups — women with prediabetes, newly diagnosed Parkinson's patients, and people with peripheral artery disease — but other trials, including ones focused on general metabolic health, failed to reproduce the impressive effects seen in animal studies. One researcher summarized: "The data in humans are pretty iffy right now."
**What we don't know yet:** Whether any benefit applies to healthy, average adults (as opposed to the specific patient groups where signals appeared), the right dose and form, and the effects of repeated long-term IV use.
**Availability:** Already widely available now as unregulated supplements and clinic infusions — a case where access has far outpaced supporting evidence. IV infusions appear safe in small studies but can cause moderate-to-severe abdominal cramping, diarrhea, nausea, and vomiting.

**Source:** NPR, May 11, 2026 — https://www.npr.org/2026/05/11/nx-s1-5813664/nad-infusions-supplements-longevity-science

## A Single Dose of Creatine Blunted Cognitive Decline From a Sleepless Night

**What happened:** Researchers led by Ali Gordji-Nejad at Forschungszentrum Jülich in Germany gave 29 healthy adults a single 0.2 g/kg dose of creatine monohydrate or a placebo, then tested their cognition repeatedly during 21 hours of sleep deprivation.
**Why it matters:** Creatine is a cheap, widely available supplement mostly associated with muscle and strength; this adds evidence that it may also support brain energy metabolism when the brain is under acute metabolic stress, such as from sleep loss.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — a small (29-person), randomized, controlled human study.
**How promising:** Creatine blunted sleep-deprivation-related decline in logical and numerical reasoning, language-related processing speed, and sustained attention (measured via the Psychomotor Vigilance Test), with women showing larger benefits than men, which the authors linked to women's lower baseline creatine stores.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether the effect holds with repeated dosing or in chronic (rather than one-off) sleep deprivation, and the optimal dose for different people.
**Availability:** Creatine monohydrate is already available over the counter as a supplement; using it specifically for sleep-deprivation cognitive support is not an approved medical indication.

**Source:** Nutrients, April 10, 2026 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13119191/
