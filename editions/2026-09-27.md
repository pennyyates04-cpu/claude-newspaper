## CRISPR-Edited Stem Cells Let Doctors Attack Leukemia Without Destroying the New Blood System

**What happened:** In a first-in-human phase 1/2 trial run across 15 sites in the US and Canada, researchers (led by teams including Washington University School of Medicine, with the CD33-deleted cell product — trem-cel — made by Vor Biopharma) used CRISPR-Cas9 to delete the CD33 gene from healthy donor blood stem cells before transplanting them into adults with high-risk acute myeloid leukemia (AML) or myelodysplastic syndrome. Because CD33 is present on most AML cells but is not required for normal blood-cell function, deleting it from the donor graft let doctors follow the transplant with gemtuzumab ozogamicin (GO), an antibody drug that kills CD33-positive cells — normally too toxic to give after a transplant because it would also destroy the new blood system. All transplanted patients achieved normal engraftment, and the edited cells tolerated GO maintenance without the cytopenias (dangerous blood-cell drops) that GO usually causes.
**Why it matters:** Relapse after stem-cell transplant is the leading cause of death in high-risk AML, and oncologists have lacked a way to keep attacking leftover leukemia cells post-transplant without also harming the newly rebuilt blood system. This is a genuine proof-of-concept that gene-editing a *donor graft itself* — not the patient's own cells — can create a lasting, drug-resistant blood system to enable more aggressive follow-up therapy.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** Yes — a first-in-human phase 1/2 trial in adult AML/MDS patients, published in *Nature Medicine*.
**How promising:** All patients engrafted normally and tolerated the follow-up drug without the expected toxicity — a clean early safety and feasibility result, and one patient with especially aggressive AML reportedly remained in remission over a year out.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether this translates into meaningfully lower long-term relapse rates or better survival versus standard transplant — this trial was sized and designed to test safety and engraftment, not yet a definitive survival benefit, and only 30 patients have been treated so far.
**Availability:** Experimental only — an investigational product (trem-cel) in early-phase trials, not yet FDA-approved or in routine clinical use.

**Source:** *Nature Medicine* 32, 1763–1772 (2026) — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04362-1

## Ozempic-Class Drug Tirzepatide May Burn Fat by Switching On Brown Fat, Not Just Curbing Appetite

**What happened:** Researchers at the University of Barcelona tested tirzepatide (the active ingredient in Zepbound and Mounjaro) in obese mice and compared its effects on white versus brown adipose tissue. They found tirzepatide didn't just shrink white fat stores — it directly and selectively boosted thermogenic activity in brown fat, upregulating genes tied to heat production and fat-burning (substrate oxidation), effectively reprogramming brown fat's gene-expression pattern toward more active calorie burning.
**Why it matters:** Tirzepatide and similar GLP-1/GIP drugs are widely assumed to work mainly by suppressing appetite. If they also directly switch on the body's calorie-burning brown fat, that would be a second, independent mechanism behind their unusually large weight-loss effects — and a clue for designing next-generation obesity and diabetes drugs that target energy expenditure, not just hunger.
**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** No — this specific brown-fat mechanism was shown only in obese mice; tirzepatide itself is already FDA-approved and widely used in humans, but this particular fat-activation effect hasn't been confirmed in people.
**How promising:** A clean, mechanistically specific finding (brown fat responded differently than white fat, with a clear gene-expression signature), but the authors themselves caution that mice and humans differ substantially in fat distribution and drug metabolism.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether tirzepatide activates brown fat the same way in humans, and how much this contributes to the weight loss already seen in human trials versus appetite suppression alone.
**Availability:** Not applicable to a new product — tirzepatide is already FDA-approved and in clinical use for obesity and type 2 diabetes; this is new mechanistic evidence about a drug already on the market.

**Source:** *Biomedicine & Pharmacotherapy* (ScienceDirect), 2026 — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0753332226000892

## Your Gut Bacteria May Predict Future Disease Risk Better Than Your Age Does

**What happened:** Researchers characterized the gut microbiome composition of 1,199 adults and compared how well it predicted inflammation and physiological measurements against chronological age. Gut microbiota composition explained more variance than age for 97% of inflammatory markers (cytokines) and 84% of physiological variables tested. One microbial community type, dubbed the "Bacteroides 2" enterotype, was linked to higher inflammation and a worse metabolic profile — young adults with this pattern showed inflammatory and metabolic markers that other groups didn't reach until decades later ("inflammaging"). In a separate prospective analysis of 2,259 participants, having this Bacteroides 2 pattern was associated with a 21% higher relative hazard of a subsequent hospital-recorded diagnosis.
**Why it matters:** Most preventive-health risk scores lean heavily on age and genetics. This suggests gut microbiome composition may be a more sensitive, and potentially modifiable, early-warning signal for inflammation-driven disease — years before age alone would flag someone as high-risk.
**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** Yes — cross-sectional characterization of 1,199 adults plus a prospective cohort of 2,259 participants, published in *Nature Communications*.
**How promising:** Large sample sizes and a clear dose-response pattern (specific enterotype, specific hazard increase) make this a solid association; the authors themselves note it does not establish that changing your microbiome would lower your disease risk.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether deliberately shifting someone out of the higher-risk "Bacteroides 2" pattern (via diet, probiotics, or other interventions) actually reduces their inflammation or disease risk — that requires interventional trials, not just observation.
**Availability:** Not a clinical test yet — a research finding from cohort studies, not currently offered as a standard doctor-ordered risk score.

**Source:** *Nature Communications* (2026) — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-77499-9

## Dreaming Brain Shows an "Energy Paradox": Blood Flow Rises, but Fuel on Hand Actually Falls

**What happened:** Researchers at Tohoku University in Sendai, Japan, imaged the brains of 15 mice through their intact skulls during natural sleep, tracking blood flow and energy metabolites in real time across sleep stages. They found that blood supply to the brain begins increasing even before REM sleep (the dreaming stage) starts — but neurons' immediately available energy currency, ATP, actually drops during REM itself, even as more fuel is being delivered.
**Why it matters:** It suggests dreaming demands such intense internal neural processing that neurons burn through available energy faster than the increased blood flow can replenish it — a dynamic reorganization of the brain's metabolic resources that hadn't been directly observed before, and a new physiological detail in the long-running mystery of why the brain stays this active during sleep.
**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** No — this was measured directly only in mice; equivalent real-time metabolite imaging hasn't been done in the human brain during sleep.
**How promising:** A well-instrumented, direct physiological measurement (not inference) of blood flow and ATP together during natural sleep, but it's a single study in 15 mice.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether the same supply-versus-consumption mismatch occurs in human brains during REM sleep, and what happens to this balance when REM sleep or dreaming is disrupted, as in insomnia or sleep apnea.
**Availability:** Not applicable — basic neuroscience research, not a treatment or diagnostic.

**Source:** *Communications Biology* (2026) — https://www.nature.com/articles/s42003-026-10646-6

## Resistance Training Slowed Brain Aging by Up to 2.3 Years — and the Effect Outlasted the Workouts

**What happened:** In the Live Active Successful Aging (LISA) randomized controlled trial, 309 adults aged 62–70 were assigned to heavy-resistance training, moderate-intensity training, or a non-exercise control group for one year, with brain scans and fitness tests at baseline and at one and two years. Researchers applied a brain-age prediction model (trained on resting-state fMRI data from 2,433 healthy adults) to the participants' scans. Both training groups showed measurably younger brain-age scores than controls — moderate lifters by about 2.3 years, heavy lifters by about 1.8 years — and the gap was still present a full year after supervised training had ended.
**Why it matters:** Brain-age gaps measured this way have been linked to future cognitive decline risk. A one-year exercise program producing a multi-year shift in a brain-age score, and one that persists after training stops, is a notably durable result for a low-cost, widely accessible intervention.
**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** Yes — a two-year randomized controlled trial in adults aged 62–70, published in *GeroScience*.
**How promising:** A randomized design with an inactive control group and objective MRI-based scoring strengthens the finding, but "brain age" is a modeled proxy, not a direct measure of cognitive function or disease outcomes.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether this brain-age improvement translates into measurably better real-world cognition or lower dementia risk over the long term, and whether the effect eventually fades without any continued training.
**Availability:** Not a clinical intervention requiring approval — resistance training is a widely available, unsupervised-or-supervised exercise practice; no special device or protocol is being marketed off this specific study.

**Source:** *GeroScience* (2026) — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11357-026-02141-x
