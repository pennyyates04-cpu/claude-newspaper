## Donor Immune Cells Can Transfer Healthy Mitochondria to Failing Cells

**What happened:** A Stanford Medicine team led by pediatrics researcher Natalia Gomez-Ospina found that bone-marrow-derived immune cells, after transplant, don't just replace damaged tissue — they physically donate their own healthy mitochondria to neighboring cells whose mitochondria are failing. Testing the approach in mice engineered to model Friedreich's ataxia (a genetic disease that cripples cellular energy production and damages the heart and nervous system), the team developed a conditioning regimen that boosted how efficiently donor cells took up residence throughout the body. Treated mice lived longer, grew better, and — in females — showed improved movement, strength, coordination, and heart function compared with untreated mice with the disease.
**Why it matters:** Friedreich's ataxia and related mitochondrial diseases currently have no way to fix the underlying energy-production defect in already-damaged tissue. A transplant approach that lets healthy donor cells directly resupply failing cells with working mitochondria — rather than only replacing the damaged cells themselves — is a different repair strategy than most current gene or enzyme approaches.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** No — this is a mouse model (using a human-frataxin-gene mouse strain that mimics the disease); no human trial has been run.
**How promising:** A clean mechanistic story — donor mitochondria measurably improved multiple disease markers in an established disease model — but the sex-specific results (benefits appeared clearer in female mice) and the need for a conditioning regimen before transplant both raise questions about how directly it would translate to a human treatment.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether the same mitochondrial transfer happens efficiently enough in humans, what conditioning regimen would be safe in patients who are often already medically fragile, and why the functional benefits differed by sex in mice.
**Availability:** Experimental only — a preclinical finding, not in any human clinical pipeline yet.

**Source:** Stanford Medicine research, published in *Nature Communications*, September 2026 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13574779/

## First Direct Human Evidence Links Gut Bacteria to Brain Chemical Levels

**What happened:** Researchers from the University of Surrey and the University of Roehampton studied 61 healthy young women aged 17–25, using proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (a non-invasive brain-imaging technique) to measure levels of GABA and glutamate — the brain's primary inhibitory and excitatory chemical messengers — in three brain regions. They paired this with shotgun metagenomic sequencing of stool samples to map each person's gut microbial genes for producing or breaking down GABA, glutamate, short-chain fatty acids, and other neuroactive compounds. Specific microbial metabolic pathways tracked with GABA and glutamate levels, and — notably — different pathways mattered in different brain regions rather than one microbial signature explaining brain chemistry everywhere.
**Why it matters:** The gut-brain connection is widely discussed, but most human evidence has been indirect (correlating gut bacteria composition with mood or behavior). This is described as the first study to directly link what gut bacteria are genetically capable of doing chemically to measured levels of specific brain neurotransmitters in living people.
**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** Yes — a direct human study, though observational (not an intervention) and limited to young women.
**How promising:** The combination of brain imaging and metagenomic sequencing in the same people is methodologically stronger than prior indirect studies, but it shows association, not that changing gut bacteria would change brain chemistry.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether deliberately altering these microbial pathways (via diet, probiotics, or other means) would shift GABA/glutamate balance, and whether the same regional patterns hold in men, older adults, or people with mental health conditions.
**Availability:** Not a clinical intervention — a research finding about mechanism, not a therapy or test currently offered.

**Source:** *Molecular Psychiatry*, published online August 25, 2026 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42642466/

## A Year of Supervised Weightlifting Slowed a Biological "Brain Age" Clock in Older Adults

**What happened:** A randomized controlled trial (Gonzalez-Gomez, Demnitz, Coronel, and colleagues) assigned 309 healthy adults aged 62–70 to different exercise arms, with one group doing supervised heavy resistance training three times a week for a full year at a training center. Using an established "brain age" algorithm derived from MRI scans, the researchers found the resistance-training group's estimated brain age advanced more slowly over the year than control groups doing lighter or no structured exercise.
**Why it matters:** Cardio exercise has long been linked to brain health, but this is a fairly large, year-long randomized trial isolating heavy resistance training specifically, using an objective brain-aging biomarker rather than just cognitive test scores.
**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** Yes — a randomized controlled trial in 309 adults, one of the larger and longer resistance-training-and-brain trials to date.
**How promising:** The size, randomization, and year-long duration make this more convincing than typical small pilot studies, but "brain age" clocks are themselves an evolving, imperfect measurement tool, and the trial doesn't yet show whether the effect translates into meaningfully better real-world cognitive outcomes.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether the slower brain-aging trajectory persists beyond one year, and how much of the effect depends on the specific heavy-resistance protocol used versus resistance training in general.
**Availability:** Not a clinical treatment — supports an already widely available practice (supervised strength training), not a new product or protocol requiring approval.

**Source:** *GeroScience*, published online February 10, 2026 — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11357-026-02141-x

## Off-the-Shelf CAR T-Cell Therapy for Lupus Gets $15M State Grant and Moves to Phase 2

**What happened:** Fate Therapeutics announced on September 25, 2026 that the California Institute for Regenerative Medicine (CIRM) awarded it a $15 million grant to advance RECLAIM-LN, a Phase 2 trial of its off-the-shelf (donor-derived, not custom-made per patient) CAR T-cell therapy FT819 in lupus nephritis, a serious kidney complication of lupus. The Phase 2 trial plans to enroll about 53 patients, giving a single infusion of 900 million engineered cells after a chemotherapy conditioning regimen, with the primary goal being complete kidney response at six months. That follows Phase 1 safety data in 16 lupus patients showing no dose-limiting toxicities and no severe cases of the immune side effects (cytokine release syndrome, neurotoxicity) that often complicate CAR T-cell therapies.
**Why it matters:** CAR T-cell therapy has transformed treatment of some blood cancers, and researchers are now testing whether the same cell-engineering approach can reset the immune system in severe autoimmune disease. An "off-the-shelf" version — made in advance from donor cells rather than custom-manufactured from each patient's own cells over several weeks — could make the approach far more practical to deliver if it works.
**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** Yes — Phase 1 data exist in 16 patients; Phase 2 (the registrational-track trial) is just beginning enrollment.
**How promising:** The favorable early safety profile is a meaningful hurdle cleared for this cell type in autoimmune disease, but Phase 1 trials are designed to test safety, not prove the therapy actually works — efficacy data from a larger, controlled Phase 2 population isn't in yet.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether FT819 actually produces durable kidney-disease remission in lupus nephritis at a rate that beats existing treatments, and what its longer-term safety profile looks like beyond the first months.
**Availability:** Experimental only — available solely through the clinical trial, not as an approved treatment.

**Source:** Fate Therapeutics press release (CIRM grant announcement) and company clinical-trial disclosures, September 25, 2026 — https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/09/25/3369036/24675/en/fate-therapeutics-announces-receipt-of-california-institute-for-regenerative-medicine-cirm-clin2-grant-to-advance-reclaim-ln.html

## IV NAD+ Causes Noticeably Worse Side Effects Than IV Nicotinamide Riboside, Clinic Data Shows

**What happened:** Researchers reviewed electronic medical records from a commercial IV-therapy clinic (Restore Hyper Wellness, Austin, TX) for clients who received four consecutive days of either 500 mg IV NAD+ or IV nicotinamide riboside (NR, a related NAD+ precursor sold under brand names like Niagen), then followed them for 30 days. Every NAD+ recipient reported moderate-to-severe side effects during infusion — including cramping, nausea, vomiting, elevated heart rate, and chest pressure — and infusions took about 97 minutes on average. NR recipients reported only mild, transient effects, and their infusions finished in about 37 minutes, roughly 60% faster.
**Why it matters:** IV NAD+ is marketed at wellness clinics nationwide as an anti-aging and energy-boosting therapy, often at significant cost, but this is one of the few real-world looks at how it's actually tolerated compared with a related but different compound sold for similar claims.
**Evidence:** Little or no credible evidence for anti-aging or wellness benefit, plus a notable tolerability concern — this study addressed side effects, not whether either drip does anything beneficial.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — a retrospective review of real clinic patients, not a controlled clinical trial with a placebo arm.
**How promising:** Useful as a safety/comfort comparison between two similar-sounding IV products, but it says nothing about whether either one delivers the longevity or energy benefits clinics advertise; as of 2026, IV NAD+ has no rigorous randomized-trial support for anti-aging claims.
**What we don't know yet:** Why NAD+ infusions cause more acute discomfort than NR infusions at these doses, and whether either compound produces any measurable long-term health benefit that would justify the cost or the side effects.
**Availability:** Sold commercially now at wellness and IV-therapy clinics — not FDA-approved for any anti-aging indication, and not covered by insurance.

**Source:** *Frontiers in Aging*, published online February 2, 2026 — https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/aging/articles/10.3389/fragi.2026.1652582/full
