## Three Months of Exercise Measurably Shifted Mood and Neuron-Formation Markers in Middle Age

**What happened:** In King's College London's NeuroFit randomized controlled trial, healthy middle-aged adults who completed a three-month structured exercise program showed significant improvements in depressive-symptom scores (measured by the PHQ-9) compared with a control group. Researchers also tested each participant's blood serum on human neural stem cells in the lab and found a shift in how that serum affected the cells' behavior over time, consistent with exercise remodeling circulating factors that influence neurogenesis.
**Why it matters:** It's a rare human trial directly linking a structured, time-limited exercise dose to both a mood outcome and a laboratory proxy for how the brain makes new neurons — most neurogenesis evidence in humans is indirect.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** Yes — a randomized controlled trial in middle-aged adults, published in *npj Aging*.
**How promising:** The mood effect was clear and statistically significant; the neurogenesis-proxy finding was more surprising than expected — serum from exercisers actually reduced the proliferation of neural stem cells in the lab dish, which the researchers interpret as the brain possibly conserving a finite stem-cell pool rather than spending it, but that interpretation is speculative.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether the blood-serum lab assay reflects what's actually happening inside a living human brain, and whether the mood benefit persists beyond three months.
**Availability:** Not a clinical product — this describes general aerobic exercise, already widely accessible; the neurogenesis assay is a research tool only.

## FDA Advisory Panel Recommends the Wellness Peptide BPC-157 for Compounding, Over Staff Objections

**What happened:** On July 23-24, 2026, the FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee voted 8-6 to recommend adding BPC-157 to the Section 503A Bulks List, which would let compounding pharmacies legally prepare it. The vote came despite FDA staff's own briefing documents recommending against inclusion, citing sparse human trial data and potential safety risks, including immune reactions and product-purity concerns.
**Why it matters:** BPC-157 is one of the most widely sold "healing peptides" in wellness and biohacking circles, marketed for tendon, gut, and injury repair — but it has never been FDA-approved, and this vote is a real regulatory flashpoint over how much evidence should be required before a popular but unproven peptide gets an easier path to legal compounding.

**Evidence:** Little or no credible evidence
**Studied in humans:** Barely — a 2025 literature review found only a handful of small or incomplete human studies, including a 12-patient retrospective case series and one Phase I safety trial whose results were never publicly submitted. Most supporting evidence is from animal studies.
**How promising:** Uncertain. The committee's vote reflects demand and clinical interest, not new efficacy data — FDA staff's underlying safety and evidence concerns were not resolved, just outvoted.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether BPC-157 is actually effective for the injuries it's marketed for in humans, and its long-term safety profile.
**Availability:** Not FDA-approved. The committee's recommendation is non-binding; the FDA must still run a formal rulemaking process, typically 8-12 months, before compounding pharmacies would have clear legal authority to prepare it.

## Off-the-Shelf Stem-Cell Therapy for Parkinson's Clears 12-Month Safety Milestone

**What happened:** At the ISSCR 2026 Annual Meeting, Lund University researchers presented 12-month results from the STEM-PD Phase I/II trial, which transplants a cryopreserved, off-the-shelf dopaminergic progenitor cell product — derived from human pluripotent stem cells — into the brains of patients with moderately advanced Parkinson's disease who no longer respond well to medication. The trial met its primary endpoint of demonstrating feasibility, and the transplanted cells showed signs of surviving and functioning as dopamine-producing neurons.
**Why it matters:** Parkinson's disease progressively kills the dopamine-producing neurons that control movement, and current drugs only manage symptoms. An off-the-shelf (not patient-specific) cell product that can be manufactured in advance and safely transplanted would be a major manufacturing and access advantage over custom cell therapies if it eventually proves effective.
**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** Yes — an open-label Phase I/II trial in patients with moderately advanced Parkinson's disease.
**How promising:** Encouraging on safety and biological activity, but this trial phase wasn't designed or powered to prove the therapy improves symptoms.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether the biological dopamine activity translates into meaningful, lasting symptom improvement, and how the therapy performs in a larger, controlled trial.
**Availability:** Experimental only — an investigational cell therapy in early-phase clinical trials.

**Source:** International Society for Stem Cell Research (ISSCR), July 2026 — https://www.isscr.org/isscr-news/new-clinical-data-presented-at-isscr-2026-advance-stem-cell-based-cell-therapy-for-parkinsons-disease

## FDA Panel Votes Against Capricor's Duchenne Heart-Damage Cell Therapy; Decision Pushed to November

**What happened:** On July 29, 2026, an FDA advisory committee voted 9-3 that the available evidence does not demonstrate substantial effectiveness for deramiocel, Capricor Therapeutics' cardiac stem-cell-derived therapy for the heart damage (cardiomyopathy) caused by Duchenne muscular dystrophy. Reviewers found that neither of the therapy's two pivotal trials (HOPE-2, HOPE-3) met its prespecified primary or key secondary endpoints, and that Capricor's post-hoc analyses didn't fill the gap. The FDA's original August 22 decision deadline has since been pushed to November 22, 2026, after Capricor submitted new 24-month HOPE-3 data and asked the agency to consider a narrower approval focused on upper-limb function, an endpoint the company says HOPE-3 met with statistical significance.
**Why it matters:** Duchenne muscular dystrophy is a fatal, progressive genetic disease, and heart failure is a leading cause of death in patients; there's real pressure to approve promising therapies for a small, underserved patient population, colliding here with a trial record that an independent panel found didn't clear the effectiveness bar.
**Evidence:** Mixed / uncertain evidence
**Studied in humans:** Yes — two randomized pivotal trials (HOPE-2, HOPE-3), both missing their primary endpoints on an intention-to-treat basis; a post-hoc upper-limb-function subgroup analysis was more favorable.
**How promising:** Genuinely uncertain — the advisory committee's own discussion of the narrower upper-limb endpoint was "directionally supportive," even as it voted against overall effectiveness on the primary measures.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether the FDA will accept the narrower, post-hoc upper-limb-function claim as a basis for approval, or require another controlled trial.
**Availability:** Not yet approved; still under FDA review, with a decision due November 22, 2026.

## Mayo Clinic Finds a Blood Marker for "Zombie" Cells — and a Tomato Compound That Calms Them in Mice

**What happened:** A series of seven Mayo Clinic studies, part of the NIH-funded Cellular Senescence Network (SenNet) Consortium, reported new findings on senescent ("zombie") cells — aging cells that stop dividing but stay metabolically active and pump out inflammatory molecules linked to age-related disease. Researchers identified IL-23R, a plasma protein that rises with age in human blood samples, as a candidate biomarker of senescence burden. In separate work using aged mice, a naturally occurring tomato-derived compound, along with existing senolytic drugs and genetic approaches that selectively clear senescent cells, reduced inflammation and improved measures of physical frailty, brain inflammation, and cognitive performance.
**Why it matters:** Senescent-cell buildup is one of the leading biological theories of aging, but tracking it in living people has been difficult; a blood biomarker like IL-23R could let researchers measure biological aging and test whether anti-senescence treatments actually work, in people rather than just mice.
**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** Partially — the IL-23R biomarker discovery used human blood plasma samples; the interventions that reduced inflammation and improved frailty were tested only in aged mice, not humans.
**How promising:** The biomarker angle is a meaningful tool-building step; the tomato-compound and senolytic results are encouraging animal data but a long way from a human treatment.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether lowering IL-23R or the senescent-cell burden it reflects actually slows aging or disease in humans, and whether the tomato-derived compound is safe or effective in people.
**Availability:** Experimental only — biomarker and mouse-study findings, not a clinical test or treatment.

**Source:** Mayo Clinic News Network, late July 2026 — https://newsnetwork.mayoclinic.org/discussion/mayo-clinic-research-advances-understanding-of-senescent-zombie-cells-healthy-aging/
