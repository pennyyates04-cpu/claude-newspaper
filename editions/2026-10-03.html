First Human Patient Dosed With a Partial Epigenetic Reprogramming Therapy

Life Biosciences dosed the first participant in a Phase 1 trial of ER-100, a gene therapy delivered by injection directly into the eye that uses a controlled, switchable version of three Yamanaka factors (OCT4, SOX2, KLF4 — "OSK") to reset the epigenetic code of retinal ganglion cells toward a more youthful state, without altering the underlying DNA. The FDA cleared the IND application in January 2026, and the first dose was announced in June 2026. The trial will enroll up to 18 people with open-angle glaucoma or non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION) — a form of vision loss with no approved treatment — across four U.S. sites. This is the first time a partial epigenetic reprogramming therapy — a technology built on the Nobel Prize-winning discovery that cells can be reset to a younger state — has been given to a human being. The eye was chosen partly because it's an accessible, contained place to test safety first.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes, as of June 2026 — but only for safety so far; this is a first-in-human Phase 1 trial, not an efficacy study.
How promising
Reprogramming has restored vision-related function in animal models of optic nerve injury, which is why the eye was chosen as the first human target; whether it's safe and effective in people is what this trial will determine.
Unknowns
Long-term safety (reprogramming carries a theoretical tumor risk) and whether any visual benefit shows up — efficacy readouts aren't expected for some time.
Availability
Experimental only — enrolling at four U.S. trial sites (Boston, New York, Los Angeles, Charleston).

Stanford Study: A Gut Bacterium Blocks a Memory Circuit in Aging Mice — and Reversing It Restores Memory

Stanford researchers found that as mice age, a gut bacterium called Parabacteroides goldsteinii becomes more abundant and triggers immune cells in the gut to release fatty acids that spark inflammation. That inflammation impairs the vagus nerve's ability to signal the hippocampus, the brain region responsible for memory formation and spatial navigation. The study was published in Nature in March 2026. It offers a specific, mechanistic explanation for why gut health and age-related memory decline are linked, rather than just a correlation — and points to two very different ways to intervene (the microbiome, or the nerve itself).

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
No — mouse model only.
How promising
Notably strong within the animal model: stimulating the vagus nerve or resetting the gut microbiome restored old mice's memory and spatial navigation to roughly the level of 2-month-old mice — a full reversal, not just a slowed decline.
Unknowns
Whether the same bacterium-inflammation-vagus pathway works the same way in humans, and whether a device or microbiome treatment could reproduce the reversal in people.
Availability
Experimental only — not tested in humans.

AI-Designed Lung Drug Shows a "Biological Age Reversal" Signal Across Six Independent Aging Clocks

Researchers reanalyzed blood samples from a previously published 12-week Phase 2a trial of rentosertib, an AI-discovered anti-fibrotic drug being tested in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), applying six different proteomic "aging clocks" to the same samples. All six clocks independently indicated a reduction in predicted biological age in patients who received the drug. The work was published in Nature Biotechnology on September 7, 2026. It's the first time multiple independent aging-biomarker algorithms have been compared head-to-head inside an actual drug trial, and the clocks agreed with each other — a step toward measuring "biological age reversal" alongside a drug's intended effect.

Evidence
Mixed/uncertain evidence
In humans
Yes — but it's a secondary biomarker analysis of an already-completed 12-week IPF trial, not a trial designed to test aging itself.
How promising
Intriguing but still a surrogate signal: the dose with the strongest age-reversal reading wasn't the dose with the best lung-function improvement, suggesting the two effects are at least partly independent.
Unknowns
Whether a shift in these proteomic clocks predicts any real-world health or longevity benefit, and whether it would replicate in a trial designed to test it from the start.
Availability
Rentosertib remains experimental, only in trials for pulmonary fibrosis — not available as a longevity treatment.

Nature Biotechnology, September 7, 2026 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41587-026-03286-y

Small Pilot Finds Time-Restricted Eating Eases a Blood Marker of Nerve Damage in Huntington's Disease

Oregon Health & Science University researchers ran a 12-week, open-label pilot trial of time-restricted eating — a self-selected 6- to 8-hour daily eating window — in 20 adults with early-stage Huntington's disease (average age 45). It is the first time this dietary approach has been tested in this patient group. The study was published in Nature Metabolism on September 29, 2026. Huntington's disease has very limited treatment options and no approved way to slow its progression, so any intervention — especially one as low-risk and accessible as changing eating timing — that moves a biomarker of neurodegeneration is notable.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — 20 patients, but open-label with no placebo/control group.
How promising
Modest but real: blood neurofilament light chain (a nerve-damage marker) fell by about 12.6% on average, and a standard severity scale improved by about 0.5 points, versus the roughly 1-point annual decline typical in early-stage patients.
Unknowns
Whether the effect holds up in a larger, placebo-controlled trial, and whether it reflects genuine slowing of disease progression or a shorter-term metabolic effect.
Availability
Time-restricted eating is freely available to try; using it specifically to slow Huntington's is not an established clinical protocol.

Large Real-World Study Links Continuous Glucose Monitors to Lower Death Risk in Type 2 Diabetes

Abbott presented data at the European Association for the Study of Diabetes (EASD) annual meeting, which opened September 28, 2026 in Milan, from a roughly 28,000-person study of adults with type 2 diabetes on basal insulin. Using "target trial emulation" — a statistical method designed to mimic a randomized trial using real-world data — the analysis found continuous glucose monitor (CGM) users, including those on Abbott's Libre devices, had a 45% lower risk of death at 12 months and 36% lower risk at 24 months than non-users. A separate analysis of more than 253,000 people found CGM use nearly halved acute diabetes-related hospitalizations. CGMs are increasingly marketed to the general wellness market, but this is a large-scale signal that, specifically in insulin-treated type 2 diabetes, continuous glucose data may meaningfully affect hard outcomes like death and hospitalization.

Evidence
Mixed/uncertain evidence
In humans
Yes — roughly 28,000 and 253,000 people respectively, but both are observational analyses, not randomized trials, and the research was conducted and presented by Abbott, which sells the Libre CGM.
How promising
The effect size is large and consistent across two analyses, but target trial emulation can't fully rule out that healthier, more engaged patients are simply more likely to use a CGM in the first place.
Unknowns
Whether a randomized trial would show the same magnitude of benefit, and how much reflects the sensor itself versus the closer follow-up that comes with using one.
Availability
CGMs are already widely available and commonly prescribed for insulin-treated type 2 diabetes.
