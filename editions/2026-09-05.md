## Stem-Cell-Derived Dopamine Neurons Show Early Promise for Parkinson's Disease

**What happened:** Researchers published 12-month results from the STEM-PD trial, a phase 1/2 open-label study that transplanted a cryopreserved, off-the-shelf dopamine-producing cell product — grown from human embryonic stem cells — directly into the brains of eight Parkinson's patients.
**Why it matters:** Parkinson's is caused by the loss of dopamine-producing neurons; this is among the first human tests of a mass-producible cell therapy meant to replace them, rather than just treating symptoms with medication.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** Yes — phase 1/2 trial, 8 patients, no placebo control
**How promising:** Six of the seven surviving participants substantially reduced their Parkinson's medication, and PET brain scans showed signs the transplanted cells survived at 6 and 12 months, with no serious side effects tied to the cells themselves.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether the benefit lasts beyond a year, which patients respond best, the optimal cell dose, and how it would perform against a placebo group. One participant died of a pulmonary infection unrelated to the cell product.
**Availability:** Experimental only — still in early-stage clinical trials.

**Source:** Nature Medicine, July 9, 2026 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04525-0

## World's First Neural Stem Cell Trial for Huntington's Disease Doses Its First Patient

**What happened:** Researchers dosed the first participant in REGEN4HD, a phase 1b/2a trial testing hNSC-01 — a neural stem cell therapy derived from pluripotent stem cells — as a treatment for Huntington's disease. The update was presented at the ISSCR 2026 Annual Meeting.
**Why it matters:** Huntington's disease has no treatment that slows or reverses the underlying brain degeneration; this is the world's first attempt to replace or support lost brain cells with transplanted neural stem cells in Huntington's patients.

**Evidence:** Little or no credible evidence yet — a single patient has been treated so far
**Studied in humans:** Yes, but only one of a planned 21 participants has been dosed; that patient tolerated the transplant and immunosuppressive therapy well and elected to receive a second transplant in the opposite brain hemisphere.
**How promising:** Historically significant as a first step, but this stage of the trial is testing only safety and tolerability, not whether the therapy works.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether the cells provide any clinical benefit, long-term safety, and whether the trial's safety monitoring board will keep clearing new participants.
**Availability:** Experimental only — trial is actively recruiting, with estimated completion between 2028 and 2031.

**Source:** UCI Health, August 2026 — https://www.ucihealth.org/about-us/news/2026/08/huntingtons-disease

## Lilly's Retatrutide Delivers Largest Sustained Drug-Driven Weight Loss on Record

**What happened:** Eli Lilly reported new 104-week extension data from its Phase 3 TRIUMPH-1 obesity trial of retatrutide, a once-weekly injectable that activates three separate hormone receptors (GLP-1, GIP, and glucagon). Participants with a starting BMI of 35 or higher who stayed on the 12 mg dose for the full 104 weeks lost an average of 85 pounds, or 30.3% of body weight.
**Why it matters:** Weight loss kept increasing between 80 and 104 weeks rather than plateauing — an unusual, encouraging sign of durability that pushes drug-based weight loss closer to outcomes previously seen mainly with bariatric surgery.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary — strong Phase 3 data, not yet an approved use
**Studied in humans:** Yes — Phase 3 trial in adults with obesity or overweight without diabetes; the 104-week extension included 532 higher-BMI participants.
**How promising:** Among the strongest weight-loss results reported for any pharmacologic agent in a large trial to date.
**What we don't know yet:** Long-term cardiovascular outcomes, whether weight returns after stopping the drug, eventual cost and access, and how it compares head-to-head with tirzepatide or semaglutide.
**Availability:** Not yet FDA-approved; this is trial data that could support a future approval application.

**Source:** Eli Lilly and Company, 2026 — https://investor.lilly.com/news-releases/news-release-details/lillys-triple-agonist-retatrutide-delivered-powerful-weight-loss

## Pomegranate Compound Urolithin A Reverses Heart-Stiffening Damage — in Mice and Lab-Grown Tissue

**What happened:** Researchers at King's College London found that urolithin A — a compound gut bacteria produce from substances in pomegranates, walnuts, and berries — improved heart function by up to 80% in mice engineered to mimic heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), and improved contraction and relaxation speed in heart tissue grown from human stem cells.
**Why it matters:** HFpEF is a common, hard-to-treat form of heart failure where the heart stiffens and can't relax properly; this identifies a new, non-nitric-oxide molecular pathway (activating a protein called PKGI) that could open a fresh drug target.

**Evidence:** Mixed / uncertain evidence — promising mechanism, but preclinical
**Studied in humans:** No — tested in male mice and in heart tissue grown from human stem cells, not in human patients. The human-tissue experiments used cells from a single donor line.
**How promising:** Notable effect size in animals and a plausible new mechanism, but the authors themselves caution this is early-stage.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether it works in human patients, whether it works in female mice or people (HFpEF disproportionately affects women, and only male mice were tested), and proper human dosing.
**Availability:** Experimental only. The authors explicitly note this does not mean eating pomegranates can treat or prevent heart disease.

**Source:** Science Advances, August 19, 2026 — https://www.sci.news/medicine/urolithin-a-heart-failure-with-preserved-ejection-fraction-15008.html

## FDA Authorizes First Wearable to Track Both Glucose and Ketones

**What happened:** The FDA granted Abbott's Libre Duo 10 Day system De Novo marketing authorization on August 25, 2026 — the first wearable biosensor to continuously monitor both glucose and ketone levels in a single device, for patients with diabetes aged 2 and up.
**Why it matters:** Rising ketones combined with high blood sugar can signal diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), a life-threatening complication; combining both measurements in one wearable could give an earlier warning than glucose monitoring alone.

**Evidence:** Strong evidence for the underlying sensing technology (FDA-authorized medical device)
**Studied in humans:** Yes — sufficient clinical and analytical data to earn FDA authorization, though the device is not yet on the market.
**How promising:** A meaningful incremental safety upgrade for a well-established monitoring category, rather than a speculative new science claim.
**What we don't know yet:** Real-world accuracy and adoption once it ships, and how it will be priced and covered by insurance.
**Availability:** Not yet available — expected to launch later this year.

**Source:** Abbott, August 25, 2026 — https://abbott.mediaroom.com/2026-08-25-Abbott-receives-FDA-authorization-for-worlds-first-dual-glucose-ketone-sensing-technology-for-people-with-diabetes

## Vagus Nerve Stimulation After Practice Strengthens Long-Term Motor Learning — in Mice

**What happened:** Researchers at Tohoku University found that stimulating the vagus nerve in mice right after they practiced a visual-tracking eye-movement task didn't improve performance during training itself, but produced stronger long-term learning on later days. The effect was linked to rhythmic changes in blood volume in the brain following stimulation.
**Why it matters:** It suggests the vagus nerve — already used therapeutically for epilepsy and depression — may work partly by tuning blood flow rhythms that support memory consolidation after practice, a mechanism that could eventually inform rehabilitation or learning therapies.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** No — this was a mouse study using a cuff electrode on the vagus nerve.
**How promising:** A plausible, well-controlled mechanistic finding, but a long way from a human application.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether the same effect occurs in humans, whether it applies beyond this specific eye-movement learning task, and what stimulation timing/dosing would be needed.
**Availability:** Not applicable — basic animal research, not a treatment.

**Source:** iScience, August 25, 2026 — https://www.news-medical.net/news/20260826/Vagus-nerve-stimulation-may-enhance-long-term-motor-learning.aspx
