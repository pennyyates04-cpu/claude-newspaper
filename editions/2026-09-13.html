Exercise at Work Doesn't Protect the Brain the Way Exercise at Play Does

A team led by Natan Feter and David Raichlen (University of Southern California) pooled 74 cohort and case-control studies covering more than 4.2 million adults ages 45–93 across roughly 30 countries, following them for a median of 10 years. People with higher leisure-time physical activity — walking, cycling, running, swimming, sports — had a 24% lower risk of dementia than the least active group. But higher occupational physical activity (physically demanding jobs) was linked to a 20% higher dementia risk. Cardiovascular research has long documented this "physical activity paradox" — job-related exertion doesn't bring the same health benefits as voluntary exercise — and this is the first large-scale look at whether the same split applies to dementia risk.

Evidence
Strong evidence — the largest meta-analysis of its kind — but it's observational, so it shows association, not proof that hard physical jobs directly cause cognitive decline.
In humans
Yes — entirely human data, 4.2 million participants.
How promising
The leisure-exercise finding is robust and actionable; the occupational-risk finding is striking but could partly reflect confounding factors tied to certain jobs rather than the physical exertion itself.
Unknowns
Why occupational activity would raise risk, and whether changing conditions at physically demanding jobs could offset it.
Availability
Not a treatment — this is public-health evidence favoring leisure-time exercise, which is already freely available to anyone.

A Single Protein May Explain Why Ovaries Stiffen With Age

Researchers found that interleukin-11 (IL-11), a signaling protein, rises in the aging ovaries of mice, rats, and humans, and drives fibroblasts to pump out extracellular matrix — physically stiffening ovarian tissue. In mice and rats, delivering short interfering RNA against IL-11 (via nanoparticles) reduced that stiffness, improved ovarian function, restored hormone balance, and boosted fertility markers in aged animals. It identifies a specific, targetable molecular mechanism behind reproductive and hormonal aging, rather than just describing ovarian decline — a root-cause angle that could eventually lead to therapies that delay it.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary — a well-designed mechanistic study with functional rescue in two rodent species, but no human treatment data.
In humans
Partially — elevated IL-11 was confirmed in aging human ovarian tissue, but the blocking treatment itself has only been tested in mice and rats.
How promising
Reversing a specific, measurable driver of tissue stiffness in two species is a meaningfully strong preclinical signal, though far from a human therapy.
Unknowns
Whether blocking IL-11 can reverse already-established ovarian fibrosis, and how to deliver the treatment specifically to the ovaries rather than throughout the body.
Availability
Experimental only — no human trials of IL-11 blockade for ovarian aging exist yet.

AI "Tissue Clocks" Show Your Organs Can Age at Very Different Speeds

A team analyzed 25,712 whole-slide histopathology images spanning 40 tissue types from 983 people in the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) cohort. Using deep learning, they built organ-specific "tissue clocks" that estimate biological age from structural changes in tissue, with a mean error of about 4.9 years. The clocks correlated with telomere attrition and subclinical disease, and blood-based versions of the signatures tracked with conditions including Alzheimer's disease, Crohn's disease, cystic fibrosis, vasculitis, diabetes, and stroke. Most popular "biological age" tests give one whole-body number; this shows organs can age on very different timelines within the same person, offering a way to potentially flag which specific organ is aging fastest.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary — a large, well-built measurement study, but a research tool, not a validated clinical test.
In humans
Yes — entirely human tissue and blood data from 983 people.
How promising
The scale of the dataset and the disease correlations are encouraging, but this measures aging — it doesn't yet change what a doctor or patient would do differently.
Unknowns
Whether acting on a tissue clock's readout actually changes health outcomes, and how well the blood-based proxy generalizes beyond the GTEx cohort.
Availability
Research tool only — not a commercially available clinical test.

Nature Medicine, published online August 14, 2026 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04566-5

FDA Clears Trial of a Dual-Targeted CAR T-Cell Therapy for Colorectal Cancer

The FDA cleared a University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus clinical trial of an experimental CAR T-cell therapy engineered to attack two targets at once: B7-H3, a protein found on most colorectal tumors, and IL-8, a protein that promotes tumor growth, inflammation, and spread. The trial, led by Christopher Lieu, MD (with the therapy co-developed by Michael Verneris, MD, and manufactured at CU Anschutz's Gates Biomanufacturing Facility), will enroll adults with advanced colorectal cancer and pediatric patients with solid tumors who have exhausted standard treatment, and is expected to begin in December 2026. CAR T-cell therapy has transformed treatment for some blood cancers, but has largely failed against solid tumors like colorectal cancer, which are harder for engineered immune cells to infiltrate and survive in; targeting IL-8 alongside the tumor marker is meant to help the T-cells actually reach and persist in the tumor.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary — strong rationale from earlier preclinical work on IL-8-directed CAR-T homing, but this is now moving into its first human trial.
In humans
No — the trial has not yet started; this is FDA clearance to proceed, not efficacy data.
How promising
A logical, mechanism-driven next step for a therapy class that has repeatedly struggled in solid tumors, but entirely unproven in colorectal cancer patients.
Unknowns
Whether dual-targeting will produce durable tumor responses or come with manageable side effects once tested in people.
Availability
Experimental only — the trial is not yet enrolling (start expected December 2026).

FDA Decision Due September 19 on What Could Be the First Approved Sanfilippo Syndrome Treatment

The FDA has set a September 19, 2026 target date (PDUFA date) to decide whether to approve Ultragenyx's UX111 (rebisufligene etisparvovec), an AAV9 gene therapy for Sanfilippo syndrome type A (MPS IIIA), a fatal, progressive childhood neurodegenerative disease. The agency accepted Ultragenyx's resubmitted application after issuing a complete response letter in July 2025 over manufacturing and inspection issues. Updated data from the ongoing trial (33 children dosed, up to 8.5 years of follow-up) show a median 63.98% reduction in a key cerebrospinal fluid biomarker (CSF-HS) versus natural history, with sustained functional benefits and a safety profile dominated by mild-to-moderate, reversible liver-enzyme elevations. Sanfilippo type A currently has no approved treatment and is typically fatal in childhood or adolescence; if approved, UX111 would be the first.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary at the population level — an open-label trial with an unusually long follow-up and a clear divergence from natural history, but without a randomized control group.
In humans
Yes — 33 children treated across a Phase 1/2 trial, with follow-up ranging up to 8.5 years.
How promising
Multi-year retention of skills that untreated Sanfilippo patients typically lose is a strong signal in a disease with no other options, though the lack of a concurrent control group leaves some uncertainty about the exact size of the effect.
Unknowns
Whether the FDA will accept this evidence as sufficient (it did not the first time), and how durable the benefit will be beyond the roughly 8-year follow-up seen so far.
Availability
Not yet approved — FDA decision expected September 19, 2026.
