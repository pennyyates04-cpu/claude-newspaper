AI-Designed Lung Drug Shows a Biological-Age Signal in Six Independent "Aging Clocks"

Insilico Medicine, a Hong Kong-based AI drug discovery company, published a secondary analysis in Nature Biotechnology of blood samples from 42 patients who took part in the Phase IIa trial of rentosertib, an AI-designed drug for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (a scarring lung disease). Researchers applied six independently developed proteomic aging clocks (including ProtAge and two OrganAge variants) to blood drawn at baseline and at weeks 2, 4, and 12. All six clocks predicted lower biological age in the treated groups relative to placebo, with the biggest signal at week four in the 30 mg twice-daily arm — some clocks suggested roughly three to four years of apparent reduction, one suggested as much as six. The paper was published around September 7, 2026, with results also presented at a Nature conference in Paris. Rentosertib itself was designed end-to-end by Insilico's AI platform, and this is among the first times a drug's effect on multiple independent molecular "aging clocks" has been reported from an actual human trial rather than animal data — a proof of concept that AI-designed drugs could be evaluated for broader aging effects, not just their original disease target.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — secondary analysis of 42 patients from a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase IIa trial
How promising
Intriguing but far from conclusive — the authors say they cannot distinguish a true systemic anti-aging effect from changes that simply reflect treating the lung disease, and only about 21 of 54 comparisons per arm reached significance
Unknowns
Whether this signal would show up in people without pulmonary fibrosis, whether it predicts any real-world benefit, and how durable it is — the trial's primary focus was safety, and seven participants discontinued due to liver toxicity
Availability
Investigational drug for pulmonary fibrosis, not approved for any aging-related use

Experimental Anti-Inflammatory Pill Cuts a Key Blood Marker by ~85% in Early Human Trial

BioAge Labs announced Phase 1 results for BGE-102, an oral, brain-penetrant small-molecule inhibitor of NLRP3 — a protein complex that drives inflammation. In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled multiple-ascending-dose study in healthy volunteers and people with obesity (BMI 32–42) and elevated inflammation (hsCRP above 3 mg/L), the 120 mg and a newly tested 60 mg once-daily dose each produced median reductions in high-sensitivity CRP of roughly 85% by three weeks, with most active-treatment participants dropping below the 2 mg/L threshold associated with lower cardiovascular risk. The company said BGE-102 was well tolerated at all doses tested. The release was issued April 21, 2026. NLRP3-driven inflammation has been implicated in heart disease, diabetes, and neurodegeneration, and an oral, brain-penetrant inhibitor that lowers hsCRP this consistently — if the effect holds up in larger trials — could become a general-purpose anti-inflammatory tool well beyond its original target.

Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — Phase 1, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, roughly 15 active-treatment participants per dose cohort
How promising
A strong, consistent biomarker effect for a brand-new drug, but Phase 1 trials are designed for safety and dosing, not efficacy, and the cohorts are small
Unknowns
Whether lowering hsCRP this way actually reduces heart attacks, strokes, or other hard clinical outcomes — the question BioAge's planned Phase 2 cardiovascular-risk trial is designed to test
Availability
Experimental only — not approved or available outside clinical trials

Stem-Cell Therapy Shows Reduced Brain Inflammation on MRI in Small Alzheimer's Trial — But It's Still a Preprint

Longeveron released a post-hoc, exploratory analysis of its Phase 2a CLEAR MIND trial (NCT05233774; 49 participants with mild Alzheimer's disease), testing its stem-cell product laromestrocel (formerly Lomecel-B). Using "free-water" MRI imaging, a technique for detecting neuroinflammation, the analysis found that laromestrocel appeared to slow the progression of neuroinflammation compared with placebo, with the clearest effect in brain regions central to Alzheimer's pathology, including the left hippocampus. The preprint was posted to medRxiv and announced September 30, 2026; it has not yet completed peer review. The trial's original efficacy and safety results were published separately in Nature Medicine in March 2025. Neuroinflammation is increasingly seen as a driver of Alzheimer's progression, not just a side effect of it, and this is among the first human imaging evidence that a cell-based regenerative therapy might directly dampen that process in a region of the brain central to memory.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — 49 participants with mild Alzheimer's disease in a randomized Phase 2a trial, though this MRI analysis is a secondary, post-hoc look at the data
How promising
A real human imaging signal rather than a surrogate blood marker, which is notable, but the sample is small, this wasn't the trial's pre-specified primary endpoint, and the write-up has not yet been peer-reviewed
Unknowns
Whether the MRI changes translate into meaningfully slower cognitive decline, and whether the finding holds up once the preprint is reviewed and replicated
Availability
Investigational — laromestrocel has not been approved by the FDA or any other regulator

Large Study Finds Blood NAD+ Doesn't Decline With Age — Undercutting a Popular Supplement's Main Pitch

Researchers at Amsterdam UMC, led by Riekelt Houtkooper, measured whole-blood NAD+ (a molecule central to cellular energy metabolism, and the target of popular NMN/NR supplements) across seven independent human cohorts totaling more than 300 people, using high-resolution mass spectrometry. They found whole-blood NAD+ levels stayed remarkably stable with age and were essentially unchanged by lifestyle factors — elite athletes showed the same levels as sedentary people. NAD+ did rise in response to taking nicotinamide riboside (NR) supplements, but that supplement-driven bump is distinct from any age-related decline, which the data didn't show. The study was published in Nature Metabolism in June 2026 (DOI 10.1038/s42255-026-01537-5); a separate unreviewed preprint from February 2026 reached a similar conclusion. The idea that NAD+ naturally declines with age — largely based on animal and tissue studies — is the central marketing claim behind a multi-billion-dollar NAD+/NMN/NR supplement industry; this is one of the largest human blood datasets to directly test that premise in people, and it didn't hold up.

Evidence
Mixed / uncertain evidence
In humans
Yes — pooled analysis of over 300 people across seven independent cohorts, including elite athletes and sedentary controls
How promising
Not promising for the "declining NAD+ causes aging" narrative specifically — solid, sizable human data contradicting that premise, though whole blood may not reflect what's happening inside individual tissues
Unknowns
Whether NAD+ inside specific tissues (muscle, liver, brain) declines with age even though whole-blood levels don't, and whether that would matter for a different reason than supplements are currently marketed on
Availability
NAD+ precursor supplements (NMN, NR) remain widely available over the counter; this only challenges the evidence behind the age-decline rationale for taking them

30-Year Harvard Study: Variety of Exercise, Not Just Volume, Linked to Lower Risk of Early Death

Harvard researchers (Han Han, Jinbo Hu, Yang Hu, and colleagues) analyzed activity data from roughly 111,000 people in the Nurses' Health Study (1986–2018) and Health Professionals Follow-Up Study (1986–2020), tracking both the total amount and the variety of physical activity reported over up to 30 years of follow-up. People who did the widest mix of different activity types had about 19% lower mortality risk than those doing the least varied activity — and this association held up independent of total exercise volume, with a pattern suggesting a possible threshold rather than a straight-line benefit. The study was published in BMJ Medicine on January 20, 2026 (DOI 10.1136/bmjmed-2025-001513). Most exercise guidance focuses on hitting a weekly minutes target; this large, long-running cohort suggests that spreading effort across several different kinds of activity — rather than repeating one — may carry an independent longevity benefit, a simple, low-cost behavioral lever distinct from any drug or supplement.

Evidence
Strong evidence (for a well-powered, long-term observational association; not a randomized trial)
In humans
Yes — roughly 111,000 people followed for up to 30 years
How promising
One of the largest and longest cohort studies on this specific question, with a clear dose-independent signal, but it remains observational and cannot prove causation
Unknowns
Whether deliberately diversifying exercise would causally lower mortality in a randomized trial, and what mechanism would explain a variety-specific benefit
Availability
Not a treatment — behavioral evidence anyone can act on now, not a clinical intervention requiring approval
