Transplanted Neural Progenitor Cells Survive a Full Year in Retinitis Pigmentosa Patients

In a phase 1/2a trial funded by the California Institute for Regenerative Medicine (CIRM), researchers gave 13 patients with retinitis pigmentosa a single subretinal injection of CNS10-NPC — a human neural progenitor cell product derived from fetal brain cortex tissue — at one of two doses. New 12-month data were presented at the ISSCR 2026 Annual Meeting. Retinitis pigmentosa is an inherited, progressive retinal disease with no treatment for most patients, and because thousands of different mutations can cause it, gene-specific therapies don't scale; a cell-based approach that doesn't depend on a patient's specific mutation could in principle help a much broader group of patients.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — phase 1/2a trial, 13 patients, open-label, no placebo arm; designed primarily for safety and feasibility, not efficacy.
How promising
Visual acuity stayed stable throughout, and OCT imaging confirmed transplanted cells remained present at 1 year; cell-related adverse events (3 epiretinal membranes, 1 subretinal bleb) were judged well tolerated overall.
Unknowns
Whether long-term engraftment actually slows vision loss or improves function, the ideal dose, and how it performs in a larger controlled trial.
Availability
Experimental only — early-stage clinical trials, now in long-term follow-on monitoring.

Stem-Cell Strategy for Chronic Spinal Cord Injury Moves Toward a Human Trial

At the ISSCR 2026 Annual Meeting, Hideyuki Okano of Keio University presented preclinical efficacy data for a new stem-cell approach to chronic, incomplete spinal cord injury, using clinical-grade gliogenic neural stem/progenitor cells (gNS/PCs). Unlike earlier work that transplanted neuron-generating cells for injuries caught early, this strategy targets patients whose nerve fibers survived but lost their protective myelin coating, aiming to remyelinate them. A physician-initiated clinical trial is now planned to begin recruiting in 2027. There is currently no restorative treatment for chronic spinal cord injury — most patients are told their deficits are permanent once the injury stabilizes — so a strategy designed specifically for the chronic stage would open treatment to a much larger population of longer-term patients.

Evidence
Little or no credible evidence yet in this specific chronic-injury application — demonstrated only in preclinical models so far.
In humans
Not yet for chronic injury; trial still in planning. A related earlier trial of neuron-generating cells for subacute injury (4 patients) reported 1-year follow-up in 2025 with no serious treatment-related adverse events and improvement in 2 of 4 patients.
How promising
A logical, mechanistically distinct next step from the team's earlier subacute-injury work, but efficacy in chronic injury remains unproven outside animal models.
Unknowns
Whether remyelination translates to functional recovery in chronic human patients, optimal dose and delivery, and long-term safety in this new patient population.
Availability
Experimental only — human trial recruitment not expected until 2027.

Feinstein Institutes Publishes First Comprehensive Map of the Human Vagus Nerve

Researchers at Northwell Health's Feinstein Institutes, led by Dr. Stavros Zanos and funded by a $6.7 million NIH grant, published the REVA (Reconstructing Vagal Anatomy) atlas — the first comprehensive anatomical map of the human vagus nerve, tracing roughly 200,000 individual nerve fibers across 60 nerves from 30 human donors. The team combined physical dissection, ultrasound imaging, fluorescent protein labeling, and micro-CT tracing, and released the dataset publicly through SPARC Science. The vagus nerve is already used therapeutically for epilepsy, depression, and inflammatory conditions, but current stimulation devices activate the whole nerve bundle indiscriminately, causing side effects — a fiber-by-fiber map is the missing reference needed to design electrodes that stimulate only the fibers relevant to a given condition.

Evidence
Strong evidence for the anatomical mapping itself — a large, methodologically rigorous, peer-reviewed dataset; not a treatment claim.
In humans
Yes — the atlas is built entirely from human donor tissue (30 donors), not animal models.
How promising
A foundational resource rather than a therapy, but it directly addresses the main technical barrier — imprecise, broad stimulation — limiting current vagus nerve stimulation devices.
Unknowns
Whether device makers can translate the map into electrodes that reliably target specific fiber populations in living patients, and how much that would reduce side effects in practice.
Availability
Not a clinical product — a reference dataset now available to researchers; more-precise stimulation devices are still years from clinical use.

Common Medications May Reshape the Gut Microbiome for Years After You Stop Taking Them

Researchers at the University of Tartu's Institute of Genomics analyzed stool samples and prescription histories from more than 2,500 participants in the Estonian Biobank's microbiome cohort, testing 186 different drugs for lasting associations with gut bacteria composition. Nearly half showed effects that persisted for years after people stopped taking them — most notably antibiotics, beta-blockers, benzodiazepine derivatives, proton-pump inhibitors (common acid-reducers), and antidepressants. Most microbiome research assumes current medication use is what matters, but this suggests a person's drug history — not just what they're taking now — could be quietly shaping their gut bacteria and confounding studies that link the microbiome to disease.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary — a large, well-powered observational cohort study, not an interventional trial.
In humans
Yes — over 2,500 people in a real-world biobank cohort, using stool samples and linked prescription records.
How promising
A large sample size and broad drug panel make this a credible signal, with a plausible mechanism (several drug classes affect intestinal motility, shaping bacterial transit time and composition).
Unknowns
Whether these lasting shifts actually cause health effects, how long "years later" really extends, and whether the changes are reversible.
Availability
Not applicable — an observational research finding, not a treatment or diagnostic.

mSystems (American Society for Microbiology), 2026 — https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/msystems.00541-25

Antibody Preserves Muscle During Tirzepatide-Driven Weight Loss in Phase 2 Trial

Scholar Rock's phase 2 EMBRAZE trial tested apitegromab, an antibody that blocks myostatin activation, in about 100 adults with overweight or obesity who were also taking the GLP-1/GIP drug tirzepatide. Over 24 weeks, participants received tirzepatide plus either apitegromab (10 mg/kg) or placebo. Results were published in Nature Medicine. GLP-1-class weight-loss drugs cause substantial fat loss but also strip away a meaningful amount of lean muscle mass, a growing clinical concern especially for older patients — a drug that lets people keep more muscle while still losing fat could change how these medications are used.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary — positive phase 2 result, not yet phase 3-confirmed.
In humans
Yes — phase 2, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in roughly 100 adults without diabetes.
How promising
Apitegromab preserved about 1.9 kg (4.2 lb) more lean mass than placebo — a 54.9% relative reduction in lean-mass loss — while total weight loss was similar between groups.
Unknowns
Whether preserving lean mass this way improves strength, function, or long-term health outcomes, and how it performs in a larger phase 3 trial.
Availability
Experimental only — not FDA-approved; adverse events were balanced between groups (76% vs. 71%, serious events at 2% in each arm, no related deaths).

Nature Medicine, June 8, 2026 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04440-4
