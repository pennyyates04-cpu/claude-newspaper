CRISPR-Edited Stem Cells Let Doctors Attack Leukemia Without Destroying the New Blood System

In a first-in-human phase 1/2 trial run across 15 sites in the US and Canada, researchers (including teams at Washington University School of Medicine, with the CD33-deleted cell product — trem-cel — made by Vor Biopharma) used CRISPR-Cas9 to delete the CD33 gene from healthy donor blood stem cells before transplanting them into adults with high-risk acute myeloid leukemia (AML) or myelodysplastic syndrome. Because CD33 sits on most AML cells but isn't required for normal blood-cell function, deleting it from the donor graft let doctors follow the transplant with gemtuzumab ozogamicin (GO), an antibody drug that kills CD33-positive cells — normally too toxic to give after a transplant because it would also destroy the new blood system. All transplanted patients achieved normal engraftment, and the edited cells tolerated GO maintenance without the cytopenias GO usually causes. Relapse after stem-cell transplant is the leading cause of death in high-risk AML, and this is a genuine proof-of-concept that gene-editing the donor graft itself — not the patient's own cells — can create a lasting, drug-resistant blood system enabling more aggressive follow-up therapy.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — first-in-human phase 1/2 trial in adult AML/MDS patients, published in Nature Medicine.
How promising
All patients engrafted normally and tolerated the follow-up drug without expected toxicity; one patient with especially aggressive AML reportedly stayed in remission over a year out.
Unknowns
Whether this lowers long-term relapse or improves survival — only 30 patients treated so far, trial sized for safety/feasibility, not a definitive survival benefit.
Availability
Experimental only — investigational product in early-phase trials, not FDA-approved.

Ozempic-Class Drug Tirzepatide May Burn Fat by Switching On Brown Fat, Not Just Curbing Appetite

Researchers at the University of Barcelona tested tirzepatide (the active ingredient in Zepbound and Mounjaro) in obese mice, comparing its effects on white versus brown adipose tissue. Tirzepatide didn't just shrink white fat stores — it directly and selectively boosted thermogenic activity in brown fat, upregulating genes tied to heat production and fat-burning, reprogramming brown fat's gene-expression pattern toward more active calorie burning. GLP-1/GIP drugs are widely assumed to work mainly by suppressing appetite; if they also directly switch on the body's calorie-burning brown fat, that's a second, independent mechanism behind their outsized weight-loss effects, and a clue for designing next-generation obesity and diabetes drugs.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
No — shown only in obese mice; tirzepatide itself is FDA-approved in humans, but this brown-fat effect isn't confirmed in people.
How promising
A clean, mechanistically specific finding, but the authors caution mice and humans differ substantially in fat distribution and drug metabolism.
Unknowns
Whether tirzepatide activates brown fat the same way in humans, and how much this contributes to weight loss versus appetite suppression alone.
Availability
Not applicable to a new product — tirzepatide is already approved and in clinical use; this is new mechanistic evidence about a drug already on the market.

Biomedicine & Pharmacotherapy (ScienceDirect), 2026 — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0753332226000892

Your Gut Bacteria May Predict Future Disease Risk Better Than Your Age Does

Researchers characterized the gut microbiome of 1,199 adults and compared how well it predicted inflammation and physiological measurements against chronological age. Gut microbiota composition explained more variance than age for 97% of inflammatory markers and 84% of physiological variables tested. One microbial community type, dubbed the "Bacteroides 2" enterotype, was linked to higher inflammation and a worse metabolic profile — young adults with this pattern showed markers other groups didn't reach until decades later ("inflammaging"). In a separate prospective analysis of 2,259 participants, this pattern was associated with a 21% higher relative hazard of a subsequent hospital-recorded diagnosis. Most preventive-health risk scores lean heavily on age and genetics; this suggests gut microbiome composition may be a more sensitive, potentially modifiable early-warning signal.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — cross-sectional characterization of 1,199 adults plus a prospective cohort of 2,259 participants, in Nature Communications.
How promising
Large samples and a clear dose-response pattern strengthen the finding; the authors note it doesn't establish that changing your microbiome lowers disease risk.
Unknowns
Whether deliberately shifting out of the higher-risk pattern (diet, probiotics) actually reduces inflammation or disease risk — needs interventional trials.
Availability
Not a clinical test yet — a research finding, not currently a standard doctor-ordered risk score.

Nature Communications, 2026 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-77499-9

Dreaming Brain Shows an "Energy Paradox": Blood Flow Rises, but Fuel on Hand Actually Falls

Researchers at Tohoku University in Sendai, Japan, imaged the brains of 15 mice through their intact skulls during natural sleep, tracking blood flow and energy metabolites in real time across sleep stages. Blood supply to the brain began increasing even before REM sleep (the dreaming stage) started — but neurons' immediately available energy currency, ATP, actually dropped during REM itself, even as more fuel was being delivered. This suggests dreaming demands such intense internal neural processing that neurons burn through available energy faster than increased blood flow can replenish it — a dynamic reorganization of the brain's metabolic resources not directly observed before.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
No — measured directly only in mice; equivalent real-time metabolite imaging hasn't been done in human brains during sleep.
How promising
A well-instrumented, direct physiological measurement of blood flow and ATP together during natural sleep, but a single study in 15 mice.
Unknowns
Whether the same supply-versus-consumption mismatch occurs in human brains during REM, and what happens when REM sleep is disrupted (insomnia, sleep apnea).
Availability
Not applicable — basic neuroscience research, not a treatment or diagnostic.

Communications Biology, 2026 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s42003-026-10646-6

Resistance Training Slowed Brain Aging by Up to 2.3 Years — and the Effect Outlasted the Workouts

In the Live Active Successful Aging (LISA) randomized controlled trial, 309 adults aged 62–70 were assigned to heavy-resistance training, moderate-intensity training, or a non-exercise control group for one year, with brain scans and fitness tests at baseline and at one and two years. Researchers applied a brain-age prediction model (trained on resting-state fMRI data from 2,433 healthy adults) to participants' scans. Both training groups showed measurably younger brain-age scores than controls — moderate lifters by about 2.3 years, heavy lifters by about 1.8 years — and the gap was still present a full year after supervised training had ended. Brain-age gaps measured this way have been linked to future cognitive decline risk, so a one-year, low-cost exercise program producing a multi-year shift that outlasts the training itself is a notably durable result.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — a two-year randomized controlled trial in adults aged 62–70, published in GeroScience.
How promising
A randomized design with an inactive control group and objective MRI-based scoring strengthens the finding, but "brain age" is a modeled proxy, not a direct measure of cognition or disease.
Unknowns
Whether the brain-age improvement translates into measurably better real-world cognition or lower dementia risk long-term, and whether the effect eventually fades without continued training.
Availability
Not a clinical intervention requiring approval — resistance training is widely available; no special device or protocol is being marketed off this study.

GeroScience, 2026 — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11357-026-02141-x
