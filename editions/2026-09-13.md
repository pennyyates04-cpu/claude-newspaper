## Exercise at Work Doesn't Protect the Brain the Way Exercise at Play Does

**What happened:** A team led by Natan Feter and David Raichlen (University of Southern California) pooled 74 cohort and case-control studies covering more than 4.2 million adults ages 45–93 across roughly 30 countries, following them for a median of 10 years. People with higher leisure-time physical activity — walking, cycling, running, swimming, sports — had a 24% lower risk of dementia than the least active group. But higher occupational physical activity (physically demanding jobs) was linked to a 20% *higher* dementia risk.
**Why it matters:** Cardiovascular research has long documented this "physical activity paradox" — job-related exertion doesn't bring the same health benefits as voluntary exercise. This is the first large-scale look at whether the same split applies to dementia risk.

**Evidence:** Strong evidence — the largest meta-analysis of its kind — but it's observational, so it shows association, not proof that hard physical jobs directly cause cognitive decline.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — entirely human data, 4.2 million participants.
**How promising:** The leisure-exercise finding is robust and actionable; the occupational-risk finding is striking but could partly reflect confounding factors tied to certain jobs (stress, sleep, income, cognitive stimulation) rather than the physical exertion itself.
**What we don't know yet:** Why occupational activity would raise risk — whether it's the movement itself, recovery time, stress, or something about the jobs where it occurs — and whether changing conditions at physically demanding jobs could offset the risk.
**Availability:** Not a treatment — this is public-health evidence favoring leisure-time exercise, which is already freely available to anyone.

**Source:** The Lancet Public Health, Vol. 11, Issue 9, September 2026 (DOI: 10.1016/S2468-2667(26)00142-8) — https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanpub/article/PIIS2468-2667(26)00142-8/fulltext

## A Single Protein May Explain Why Ovaries Stiffen With Age

**What happened:** Researchers found that interleukin-11 (IL-11), a signaling protein, rises in the aging ovaries of mice, rats, and humans, and drives fibroblasts to pump out extracellular matrix — physically stiffening ovarian tissue. In mice and rats, delivering short interfering RNA against IL-11 (via nanoparticles) reduced that stiffness, improved ovarian function, restored hormone balance, and boosted fertility markers in aged animals.
**Why it matters:** It identifies a specific, targetable molecular mechanism behind reproductive and hormonal aging, rather than just describing ovarian decline — a root-cause angle that could eventually lead to therapies that delay it.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary — a well-designed mechanistic study with functional rescue in two rodent species, but no human treatment data.
**Studied in humans:** Partially — elevated IL-11 was confirmed in aging human ovarian tissue, but the IL-11-blocking treatment itself has only been tested in mice and rats.
**How promising:** Reversing a specific, measurable driver of tissue stiffness in two species is a meaningfully strong preclinical signal, though far from a human therapy.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether blocking IL-11 can reverse *already established* ovarian fibrosis (the studies focused on younger, less-damaged tissue), and how to deliver the treatment specifically to the ovaries rather than throughout the body.
**Availability:** Experimental only — no human trials of IL-11 blockade for ovarian aging exist yet.

**Source:** Nature Aging, 2026 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s43587-026-01137-8

## AI "Tissue Clocks" Show Your Organs Can Age at Very Different Speeds

**What happened:** A team analyzed 25,712 whole-slide histopathology images spanning 40 tissue types from 983 people in the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) cohort. Using deep learning, they built organ-specific "tissue clocks" that estimate biological age from structural changes in tissue, with a mean error of about 4.9 years. The clocks correlated with telomere attrition and subclinical disease, and blood-based versions of the signatures tracked with conditions including Alzheimer's disease, Crohn's disease, cystic fibrosis, vasculitis, diabetes, and stroke. Published online August 14, 2026, in Nature Medicine.
**Why it matters:** Most popular "biological age" tests give one whole-body number. This shows organs can age on very different timelines within the same person, and offers a way to potentially flag which specific organ is aging fastest — a step toward more personalized prevention.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary — a large, well-built measurement study, but a research tool, not a validated clinical test.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — entirely human tissue and blood data from 983 people.
**How promising:** The scale of the dataset and the disease correlations are encouraging, but this measures aging — it doesn't yet change what a doctor or patient would do differently.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether acting on a tissue clock's readout (e.g., an "old" pancreas or kidney) actually changes health outcomes, and how well the blood-based proxy generalizes beyond the GTEx cohort.
**Availability:** Research tool only — not a commercially available clinical test.

**Source:** Nature Medicine, published online August 14, 2026 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-026-04566-5

## FDA Clears Trial of a Dual-Targeted CAR T-Cell Therapy for Colorectal Cancer

**What happened:** The FDA cleared a University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus clinical trial of an experimental CAR T-cell therapy engineered to attack two targets at once: B7-H3, a protein found on most colorectal tumors, and IL-8, a protein that promotes tumor growth, inflammation, and spread. The trial, led by Christopher Lieu, MD (with the therapy co-developed by Michael Verneris, MD, and manufactured at CU Anschutz's Gates Biomanufacturing Facility), will enroll adults with advanced colorectal cancer and pediatric patients with solid tumors who have exhausted standard treatment, and is expected to begin in December 2026.
**Why it matters:** CAR T-cell therapy has transformed treatment for some blood cancers, but has largely failed against solid tumors like colorectal cancer, which are harder for engineered immune cells to infiltrate and survive in. Targeting IL-8 alongside the tumor marker is meant to help the T-cells actually reach and persist in the tumor, building on earlier preclinical work showing IL-8-directed homing boosted CAR T activity against pediatric sarcoma.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary — strong rationale from earlier preclinical (animal/lab) work on IL-8-directed CAR-T homing, but this is now moving into its first human trial.
**Studied in humans:** No — the trial has not yet started; this is FDA clearance to proceed, not efficacy data.
**How promising:** A logical, mechanism-driven next step for a therapy class that has repeatedly struggled in solid tumors, but entirely unproven in colorectal cancer patients.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether dual-targeting will produce durable tumor responses or come with manageable side effects once tested in people.
**Availability:** Experimental only — the trial is not yet enrolling (start expected December 2026).

**Source:** News-Medical.net (reporting CU Anschutz Medical Campus announcement), September 11, 2026 — https://www.news-medical.net/news/20260911/FDA-clears-trial-of-dual-targeted-CAR-T-cell-therapy-for-cancer.aspx

## FDA Decision Due September 19 on What Could Be the First Approved Sanfilippo Syndrome Treatment

**What happened:** The FDA has set a September 19, 2026 target date (PDUFA date) to decide whether to approve Ultragenyx's UX111 (rebisufligene etisparvovec), an AAV9 gene therapy for Sanfilippo syndrome type A (MPS IIIA), a fatal, progressive childhood neurodegenerative disease. The agency accepted Ultragenyx's resubmitted application after issuing a complete response letter in July 2025 over manufacturing and inspection issues. Updated data from the ongoing trial (33 children dosed, up to 8.5 years of follow-up) show a median 63.98% reduction in a key cerebrospinal fluid biomarker (CSF-HS) versus natural history, with sustained functional benefits and a safety profile dominated by mild-to-moderate, reversible liver-enzyme elevations.
**Why it matters:** Sanfilippo type A currently has no approved treatment and is typically fatal in childhood or adolescence. If approved, UX111 would be the first. The decision is also a test case for how much long-term, single-arm (non-placebo-controlled) data the FDA will accept for a one-time gene therapy in an ultra-rare, devastating pediatric disease.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary at the population level — an open-label trial with an unusually long follow-up and a clear divergence from the disease's natural history, but without a randomized control group.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — 33 children treated across a Phase 1/2 trial, with follow-up ranging up to 8.5 years.
**How promising:** Multi-year retention of skills that untreated Sanfilippo patients typically lose is a strong signal in a disease with no other options, though the lack of a concurrent control group leaves some uncertainty about the exact size of the effect.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether the FDA will accept this evidence as sufficient (it did not the first time), and how durable the benefit will be beyond the roughly 8-year follow-up seen so far.
**Availability:** Not yet approved — FDA decision expected September 19, 2026.

**Source:** Ultragenyx Pharmaceutical Inc. investor release, April 2026 (PDUFA date confirmed) — https://ir.ultragenyx.com/news-releases/news-release-details/ultragenyx-announces-us-fda-acceptance-bla-resubmission-ux111
