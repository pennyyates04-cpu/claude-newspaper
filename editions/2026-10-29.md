## One-time CRISPR gene edit cut cholesterol in half — and it held for a year

**What happened:** Cleveland Clinic researchers published one-year results from a first-in-human trial of CTX310, a CRISPR-Cas9 gene-editing therapy delivered as a single IV infusion that permanently switches off the ANGPTL3 gene in the liver. In the four patients who received the highest dose, LDL ("bad") cholesterol fell 52.5% and triglycerides fell 47.8%, with the effect still holding a full year after treatment. The results were published as a letter in the New England Journal of Medicine and presented at the European Society of Cardiology Congress 2026.
**Why it matters:** Current cholesterol drugs require lifelong daily dosing. A one-time gene edit with a durable effect could change how doctors manage cardiovascular risk, especially for people who don't respond to or can't tolerate statins.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** Yes — but only 15 patients total, and the headline 52.5%/47.8% figures come from just the 4 patients on the highest dose.
**How promising:** One of the more compelling early gene-editing results in cardiovascular medicine, but the standout subgroup is tiny.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether the effect persists beyond one year, whether it holds up in a larger and more diverse group, and — critically — whether it actually reduces heart attacks and strokes, since the trial measured cholesterol levels, not hard cardiac outcomes.
**Availability:** Experimental only. A phase 1b trial is now underway, and Cleveland Clinic plans roughly 15 years of follow-up, per FDA recommendations for gene-editing therapies.

## Harvard: 90 minutes of weekly strength training tied to a 27% lower risk of dying from neurological disease

**What happened:** A Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health analysis drew on roughly 30 years of data from three large cohorts — the Health Professionals Follow-up Study and the two Nurses' Health Studies — and found that 90–119 minutes of resistance training per week was associated with a 13% lower risk of death from any cause, a 19% lower risk of cardiovascular death, and a 27% lower risk of death from neurological disease, including dementia, compared with no resistance training. Benefits plateaued beyond about 120 minutes per week. People who combined resistance training with regular aerobic exercise had up to a 45% lower mortality risk than those who did neither.
**Why it matters:** Most exercise guidance centers on cardio. This gives a specific, modest, and achievable weekly strength-training target tied to lower death risk — including a dementia link that has less prior long-term human data behind it.

**Evidence:** Strong, for an observational study — large sample, decades of follow-up, and a clear dose-response pattern.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — roughly 140,000 health professionals tracked for up to 30 years.
**How promising:** One of the more robust long-term datasets tying a specific, realistic weekly strength-training dose to lower mortality.
**What we don't know yet:** It's observational, not a randomized trial, so it can't prove the exercise itself causes the lower risk; the cohort is mostly white health professionals, which limits how broadly the findings apply; and exercise habits were self-reported.
**Availability:** Not a clinical treatment — this is exercise guidance anyone can act on today.

**Source:** BMJ Group press release on the British Journal of Sports Medicine study (DOI 10.1136/bjsports-2025-110503), June 2, 2026 — https://bmjgroup.com/90-120-weekly-minutes-of-strength-training-may-be-optimal-for-lowering-death-risk/

## Yale study finds most "biological age" tests don't reliably track whether anti-aging treatments work

**What happened:** Yale researchers led by Raghav Sehgal, in Albert Higgins-Chen's lab, systematically evaluated 111 DNA-methylation "epigenetic clocks" — including 16 of the most widely used — against 51 real human longevity-intervention studies, to see which clocks actually detect change when an intervention works. The findings were published in Nature Medicine, and the full dataset was released as an open platform called TranslAGE.
**Why it matters:** The booming longevity industry leans heavily on epigenetic "age" tests to claim a supplement, protocol, or clinic is working. This study is a reality check: many popular clocks are poor at detecting real intervention effects, while a handful of newer, reliability-optimized clocks (including DunedinPACE and PCGrimAge) responded far more consistently.

**Evidence:** Mixed/uncertain — specifically regarding what today's epigenetic clocks can actually tell us.
**Studied in humans:** Yes — the analysis was built entirely from existing human intervention-study data, though the study itself is a measurement-tool evaluation, not a treatment trial.
**How promising:** Useful going forward as a way to evaluate longevity claims, but it doesn't validate any specific treatment.
**What we don't know yet:** Lead author Sehgal noted there is still no direct evidence that a change in an epigenetic clock predicts a real future health outcome.
**Availability:** Not a treatment. TranslAGE.io is a research tool for scientists, and indirectly, a reference point for consumers evaluating longevity-clock marketing claims.

**Source:** Yale School of Medicine / Nature Medicine, August 21, 2026 — https://news.yale.edu/2026/08/21/can-you-slow-aging-process-study-reveals-which-interventions-might-help

## No evidence glucose monitors help healthy people, major review finds

**What happened:** A JAMA Internal Medicine narrative review by Dower, Johansson, Camp, Montori, and Lipska examined the evidence behind continuous glucose monitors (CGMs) — increasingly marketed to healthy people for "metabolic optimization" — and concluded there is no scientific evidence that CGM use improves health or prevents disease in people without diabetes.
**Why it matters:** CGMs have become a flagship wellness and biohacking product worn by people with no diabetes risk to chase flat glucose curves. This review, co-authored by a senior Yale endocrinologist, says that popularity has run well ahead of the evidence.

**Evidence:** Little or no credible evidence, specifically for non-diabetic use.
**Studied in humans:** The review synthesizes existing human studies rather than presenting new data.
**How promising:** Not promising for healthy users — the authors warn that normal glucose swings can be overinterpreted, leading to unnecessary dietary restriction or anxiety.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether any subgroup of healthy people, such as those at elevated future diabetes risk, benefits from CGM use for early prevention; the authors say more research is needed there.
**Availability:** Widely available now — Dexcom and Abbott both sell non-diabetic CGM lines. Availability isn't the issue here; proof of benefit is.

**Source:** JAMA Internal Medicine (DOI 10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.2772), via Yale School of Medicine, August 4, 2026 — https://medicine.yale.edu/internal-medicine/news-article/who-benefits-most-from-glucose-monitoring-devices/

## Mass General Brigham to help design a large-scale NAD+ trial — but it hasn't started yet

**What happened:** Niagen Bioscience, maker of the NAD+ precursor supplement Tru Niagen, announced a start-up agreement with Mass General Brigham investigators — led by Brigham and Women's Hospital epidemiologist Howard Sesso — to begin detailed planning for what the company describes as the largest, most comprehensive randomized, placebo-controlled trial yet of nicotinamide riboside (NR) in adults 50 and older.
**Why it matters:** NAD+ boosters, sold as pills and IV infusions, are heavily marketed for anti-aging and energy despite thin human outcome data. A trial at this scale, if it launches, could finally produce a real answer on clinical benefit rather than just NAD+ blood-level changes.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary — this is a trial-design announcement, not results.
**Studied in humans:** Smaller NR studies exist, including a 2025 Niagen-funded trial in long COVID that showed increased NAD+ levels, but no large outcome trial in healthy aging has completed.
**How promising:** The scale and the academic partner are notable, but the trial hasn't started enrolling, and the announcement comes from the supplement maker itself.
**What we don't know yet:** Final trial design, sample size, duration, and whether it will actually launch and successfully recruit participants — none of that is settled.
**Availability:** NAD+ supplements and IV infusions are already widely sold and administered; for a specific prevention or longevity benefit, availability has outpaced proof.
