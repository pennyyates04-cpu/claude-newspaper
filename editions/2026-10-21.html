Cells' Internal "Recycling" Process Doesn't Simply Decline With Age — It Depends on Cell Type and Sex

Researchers at Sanford Burnham Prebys Medical Discovery Institute studied autophagy — the process cells use to break down and recycle their own damaged components — in skin and blood samples from healthy adults aged 23 to 72, and in neurons grown in the lab from those same donors' skin cells. Autophagy did not decline uniformly with age. In skin cells from male donors, autophagy activity fell with age, but it stayed stable in skin cells from female donors; in lab-grown neurons, autophagy from female donors' cells actually rose with age. In a small subsample, more physically active older adults had blood-cell autophagy patterns closer to those of younger adults, alongside better physical performance. The work was published in Aging Cell (Moreno et al., DOI 10.1111/acel.70734), appearing online September 27, 2026, with press coverage running through mid-October. Autophagy decline is considered one of the core "hallmarks of aging," and boosting it is a target of many longevity interventions (fasting mimetics, rapamycin, and others); this study suggests a simple "autophagy always falls with age, so boost it" model is wrong — the direction of change depends on which tissue and which sex you're looking at — with implications for how such therapies should be designed and tested.

Mixed / uncertain evidence
In humans
Yes — human skin/blood samples and donor-derived neurons, but cross-sectional, not an intervention trial
How promising
Reshapes a foundational assumption in aging biology rather than offering a treatment itself; the exercise finding came from a very small subsample

Adding a Muscle-Protecting Antibody to a Weight-Loss Drug Roughly Halved the Muscle Lost Alongside Fat

In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase 2 trial of 102 adults with overweight or obesity, researchers tested whether apitegromab — an antibody that blocks myostatin, a protein that limits muscle growth — could reduce muscle loss during weight loss on tirzepatide (a GLP-1/GIP weight-loss drug) over 24 weeks. Total weight loss was similar between the apitegromab and placebo groups, but lean mass accounted for only 14.6% of total weight lost in the apitegromab group, versus 30.2% in the placebo group. Adverse-event rates were similar between arms. The trial (sometimes called EMBRAZE) was published in Nature Medicine on June 8, 2026 (Pratley et al., DOI 10.1038/s41591-026-04440-4). Rapid weight loss from GLP-1-class drugs like tirzepatide and semaglutide often comes with substantial muscle loss — commonly estimated at 25–40% of total weight lost — raising concern about long-term frailty and metabolic health even as patients lose fat; myostatin blockers like apitegromab are among several approaches now in human trials specifically to address that trade-off.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — 102 adults, randomized and placebo-controlled, over 24 weeks
How promising
A sizable effect on a meaningful surrogate (lean-mass share of weight lost), but no clear gains shown in physical function or cardiometabolic outcomes yet; Phase 3 still needed
Unknowns
Whether preserved lean mass translates into meaningful strength or long-term health benefits, and performance in larger/longer trials
Availability
Experimental — not approved for this use (separately in development for spinal muscular atrophy)

Starting Hormone Therapy Near Menopause Linked to Lower Cardiovascular Risk — Timing Appears to Matter a Lot

Using 20 years of data from the Study of Women's Health Across the Nation (SWAN), researchers analyzed 2,737 perimenopausal and recently postmenopausal women who reported hot flashes or night sweats, using statistical methods designed to emulate a series of clinical trials. Starting hormone therapy during perimenopause or early postmenopause was associated with a 22% lower risk of cardiovascular disease events compared with not using it (adjusted hazard ratio about 0.78). Starting within 6–10 years of menopause onset showed a similar reduced risk (adjusted HR roughly 0.71–0.73), while starting more than 10 years after menopause onset was associated with higher risk (adjusted HR 1.53). The study, led by Wang and colleagues, was published in JAMA Internal Medicine in September 2026. Whether hormone therapy helps or harms the heart has been fiercely debated since the Women's Health Initiative trials raised alarms in the early 2000s; this adds large, long-running cohort evidence to the "timing hypothesis" — that starting hormone therapy early in menopause may be protective, while starting it late may not be — which could reshape how clinicians counsel patients about when, not just whether, to start treatment.

Evidence
Mixed / uncertain evidence
In humans
Yes — observational analysis of 2,737 women followed over roughly 20 years
How promising
Adds meaningfully to consistent observational evidence for the timing hypothesis, but is not a randomized trial and cannot rule out healthier women simply being more likely to start and continue therapy
Unknowns
Whether the association reflects a true causal benefit, how it holds up in a randomized design, and fuller subgroup detail
Availability
Hormone therapy is already FDA-approved for menopausal symptoms; using it specifically for cardiovascular prevention is not an approved indication

A Computational Screen of Existing Drugs Flagged an Over-the-Counter Nasal Spray as a Possible Longevity Candidate

Researchers at Northeastern University, working with Harvard collaborators, built a computational network model linking genes tied to the recognized biological "hallmarks of aging" and used it to screen already-approved drugs for unexpected effects on those pathways. Of the drugs analyzed, 370 were flagged as potentially affecting at least one hallmark of aging — 14 predicted to promote longevity and 14 predicted to accelerate aging. Oxymetazoline, the decongestant in sprays like Afrin, was among the predicted longevity-promoting candidates, hypothesized to work by improving cell-to-cell communication, a process that weakens with age. The team says it is now running cell-line experiments with Harvard collaborators to test the prediction directly. The work was published in Nature Aging (DOI 10.1038/s43587-026-01161-8), with Northeastern's release dated June 26, 2026. Rather than developing new anti-aging compounds from scratch, this approach mines medications whose human safety profile is already well established, which could — if validated — offer a much faster route to testing a longevity effect in people.

Evidence
Little or no credible evidence
In humans
No — a computational prediction; no cell, animal, or human validation published yet
How promising
An interesting proof-of-concept for drug repurposing, but the oxymetazoline effect is still a hypothesis, not a demonstrated result in any living system; chronic use also carries known risks like rebound congestion
Unknowns
Whether the predicted effect replicates in cells or animals, what dose or exposure could plausibly help without harm, and details on the other flagged candidates
Availability
Oxymetazoline spray is already sold over-the-counter, but not for anything related to aging — using it for that purpose is unsupported by evidence

Adding a Cold Plunge Between Sauna Rounds Changed How the Body Handled Heat Stress, Small Study Finds

In a crossover study of 16 healthy adults, researchers tested two 15-minute sauna sessions at 85°C separated by a 10-minute break, either in 23°C air alone or air plus a brief 90-second, 20°C cold-water immersion. Adding the cold-water dip lowered core body temperature and heart rate going into the second sauna round compared with air-cooling alone, and participants rated the second round as more tolerable. Systolic blood pressure and mean arterial pressure were similar between conditions, though diastolic blood pressure was higher with the cold-water immersion. The study (Owen et al.) was published in Experimental Physiology in July 2026. Large observational cohorts have linked frequent sauna use to lower rates of sudden cardiac death and stroke, and cold exposure has become a popular recovery practice; contrast routines that combine both are increasingly common at gyms and wellness clubs, but little controlled data exists on what that combination actually does physiologically.

Evidence
Mixed / uncertain evidence
In humans
Yes — 16 healthy adults, within-subject crossover design
How promising
Useful, real controlled data on acute physiological response, but the sample is tiny and measures are short-term, so it says nothing about longer-term benefit or harm
Unknowns
Whether the diastolic blood pressure increase matters clinically, effects of repeated long-term use, and generalizability beyond healthy adults
Availability
Sauna and cold-plunge facilities are already widely available; this is about sequencing an existing practice, not a new treatment
