## First Human Patient Dosed With a Partial Epigenetic Reprogramming Therapy

**What happened:** Life Biosciences dosed the first participant in a Phase 1 trial of ER-100, a gene therapy delivered by injection directly into the eye that uses a controlled, switchable version of three Yamanaka factors (OCT4, SOX2, KLF4 — "OSK") to reset the epigenetic code of retinal ganglion cells toward a more youthful state, without altering the underlying DNA. The FDA cleared the IND application in January 2026, and the first dose was announced in June 2026. The trial will enroll up to 18 people with open-angle glaucoma or non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION) — a form of vision loss with no approved treatment — across four U.S. sites.
**Why it matters:** This is the first time a partial epigenetic reprogramming therapy — a technology built on the Nobel Prize-winning discovery that cells can be reset to a younger state — has been given to a human being. The eye was chosen partly because it's an accessible, contained place to test safety first.
**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** Yes, as of June 2026 — but only for safety so far; this is a first-in-human Phase 1 trial, not an efficacy study.
**How promising:** The reprogramming approach has restored vision-related function in animal models of optic nerve injury, which is why Life Biosciences chose the eye as its first human target. Whether it is safe, and whether it does anything measurable for vision in people, is exactly what this trial is designed to find out.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether reprogramming retinal cells in a living human eye is safe over time (reprogramming technology carries a theoretical tumor risk if cells are pushed too far back toward a stem-like state), and whether any visual benefit will show up at all — efficacy readouts are not expected for some time.
**Availability:** Experimental only — enrolling at four U.S. clinical trial sites (Boston, New York, Los Angeles, Charleston).

**Source:** Washington Times, June 16, 2026 — https://www.washingtontimes.com/news/2026/jun/16/first-human-dosed-cellular-reprogramming-drug-longevity-trial/

## Stanford Study: A Gut Bacterium Blocks a Memory Circuit in Aging Mice — and Reversing It Restores Memory

**What happened:** Stanford researchers found that as mice age, a gut bacterium called *Parabacteroides goldsteinii* becomes more abundant and triggers immune cells in the gut to release fatty acids that spark inflammation. That inflammation impairs the vagus nerve's ability to signal the hippocampus, the brain region responsible for memory formation and spatial navigation. The study was published in Nature in March 2026.
**Why it matters:** It offers a specific, mechanistic explanation for why gut health and age-related memory decline are linked, rather than just a correlation — and points to two very different ways to intervene (the microbiome, or the nerve itself).
**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** No — mouse model only.
**How promising:** Notably strong within the animal model: both stimulating the vagus nerve directly and resetting the gut microbiome restored memory and spatial-navigation performance in old mice to roughly the level of 2-month-old (young) mice — a full reversal, not just a slowed decline.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether the same bacterium-inflammation-vagus pathway operates the same way in the human gut and brain, and whether vagus nerve stimulation devices or microbiome-targeted treatments could reproduce this reversal in people.
**Availability:** Experimental only — not tested in humans.

**Source:** Stanford Medicine, March 2026 — https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2026/03/gut-brain-cognitive-decline.html

## AI-Designed Lung Drug Shows a "Biological Age Reversal" Signal Across Six Independent Aging Clocks

**What happened:** Researchers reanalyzed blood samples from a previously published 12-week Phase 2a trial of rentosertib, an AI-discovered anti-fibrotic drug being tested in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), applying six different proteomic "aging clocks" (algorithms that estimate biological age from blood protein patterns) to the same samples. All six clocks independently indicated a reduction in predicted biological age in patients who received the drug. The work was published in Nature Biotechnology on September 7, 2026.
**Why it matters:** It's the first time multiple independent aging-biomarker algorithms have been compared head-to-head inside an actual drug trial, and the clocks agreed with each other — a step toward treating "biological age reversal" as something that could be measured alongside a drug's intended effect in future trials, rather than requiring a separate longevity-specific study.
**Evidence:** Mixed/uncertain evidence
**Studied in humans:** Yes — but the age-reversal finding is a secondary biomarker analysis of an already-completed 12-week IPF trial, not a trial designed or powered to test aging itself.
**How promising:** Intriguing but still a surrogate signal: the dose that produced the strongest biological-age-reversal reading was not the same dose that produced the best lung-function improvement, suggesting the two effects are at least partly independent — interesting biologically, but not yet proof the aging-clock shift means anything clinically.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether a shift in these proteomic clocks actually predicts any real-world health or longevity benefit, and whether the effect would replicate in a trial designed from the outset to test it.
**Availability:** Rentosertib remains experimental and is only in trials for pulmonary fibrosis, not available as a longevity treatment.

**Source:** Nature Biotechnology, September 7, 2026 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41587-026-03286-y

## Small Pilot Finds Time-Restricted Eating Eases a Blood Marker of Nerve Damage in Huntington's Disease

**What happened:** Oregon Health & Science University researchers ran a 12-week, open-label pilot trial of time-restricted eating — a self-selected 6- to 8-hour daily eating window — in 20 adults with early-stage Huntington's disease (average age 45). It is the first time this dietary approach has been tested in this patient group. The study was published in Nature Metabolism on September 29, 2026.
**Why it matters:** Huntington's disease has very limited treatment options and no approved way to slow its progression, so any intervention — especially one as low-risk and accessible as changing eating timing — that moves a biomarker of neurodegeneration is notable.
**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** Yes — 20 patients, but open-label with no placebo/control group, so part of the improvement could reflect factors other than the diet itself.
**How promising:** Modest but real signals: blood levels of neurofilament light chain (a protein that rises as nerve cells degenerate) fell by an average of roughly 12.6%, and scores on a standard Huntington's severity scale improved by about 0.5 points on average, versus the roughly 1-point annual decline typically seen in early-stage patients.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether the effect holds up in a larger, placebo-controlled trial, and whether it reflects a genuine slowing of disease progression or a shorter-term metabolic effect that wouldn't persist.
**Availability:** Time-restricted eating itself is freely available to try; using it specifically to slow Huntington's progression is not an established clinical protocol.

**Source:** OHSU News, September 17, 2026 — https://news.ohsu.edu/2026/09/17/intermittent-fasting-shows-promise-in-slowing-huntingtons-disease-progression

## Large Real-World Study Links Continuous Glucose Monitors to Lower Death Risk in Type 2 Diabetes

**What happened:** Abbott presented data at the European Association for the Study of Diabetes (EASD) annual meeting, which opened September 28, 2026 in Milan, from a roughly 28,000-person study of adults with type 2 diabetes on basal insulin. Using "target trial emulation" — a statistical method designed to mimic a randomized trial using real-world data — the analysis found continuous glucose monitor (CGM) users, including those on Abbott's Libre devices, had a 45% lower risk of death at 12 months and 36% lower risk at 24 months than non-users. A separate analysis of more than 253,000 people found CGM use nearly halved acute diabetes-related hospitalizations.
**Why it matters:** CGMs are increasingly marketed to the general wellness market, but this is a large-scale signal that, specifically in insulin-treated type 2 diabetes, continuous glucose data may meaningfully affect hard outcomes like death and hospitalization — not just convenience or day-to-day glucose control.
**Evidence:** Mixed/uncertain evidence
**Studied in humans:** Yes — roughly 28,000 and 253,000 people respectively, but both are observational analyses of real-world data, not randomized controlled trials, and the research was conducted and presented by Abbott, which sells the Libre CGM.
**How promising:** The size of the effect is large and consistent across two separate analyses, but target trial emulation cannot fully rule out that healthier, more engaged patients are simply more likely to use a CGM in the first place (confounding by indication).
**What we don't know yet:** Whether a randomized trial would show the same magnitude of benefit, and how much of the effect is the sensor itself versus the behavior changes and closer medical follow-up that tend to come with using one.
**Availability:** CGMs are already widely available and commonly prescribed for insulin-treated type 2 diabetes.

**Source:** Abbott, September 27, 2026 — https://abbott.mediaroom.com/2026-09-27-Landmark-study-in-people-with-Type-2-diabetes
