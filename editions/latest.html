Gene therapy shows early, unproven signal for a currently untreatable eye disease

Life Biosciences announced first-in-human interim data from its Phase 1 trial (NCT07290244) of ER-100, a gene therapy using partial epigenetic reprogramming (the same Yamanaka-factor approach studied for whole-body rejuvenation) delivered directly into the eye, for optic neuropathies including glaucoma and NAION. In the first three patients, a single injection plus daily oral doxycycline was well tolerated through day 56, and two of the three patients with open-angle glaucoma showed improvement in visual field testing — at least 28% of tested points gained more than 4 dB of sensitivity. NAION has no approved treatment, and glaucoma-related nerve damage is normally considered irreversible; if this holds up, it would be one of the first signals that a gene therapy can partially reverse, not just halt, optic nerve damage.

Stem-cell back-pain trial finishes dosing; blinded data hint at meaningful relief

BioRestorative Therapies announced it has completed dosing all patients in its Phase 2 trial of BRTX-100, an autologous (the patient's own) disc-derived stem cell injection for chronic lumbar disc disease, moving the program toward topline data expected in 2027. Earlier in 2026, the company presented expanded blinded interim data at the ISCT conference on a 25-patient, 52-week-follow-up subgroup: 52% of patients achieved at least a 50% improvement on both a pain scale (VAS) and a function scale (ODI), with no dose-limiting toxicities or related serious adverse events reported. Chronic lumbar disc disease is usually managed with pain medication, injections, or surgery; a regenerative option that repairs rather than just numbs the disc would be a genuinely different approach for a condition that affects millions.

Grip strength and chair-stand speed predict survival in older women, independent of activity level

A JAMA Network Open study (LaMonte et al., 2026) followed 5,472 ambulatory women aged 63–99 for a mean of 8.4 years. Women in the top quartile of grip strength had 33% lower all-cause mortality than those in the bottom quartile (HR 0.67), and women who completed a five-chair-stand test in 11.1 seconds or less had 37% lower mortality risk than those taking more than 16.7 seconds. These associations held after adjusting for accelerometer-measured physical activity, sedentary time, walking speed, and the inflammation marker CRP — suggesting muscular strength itself, not just overall movement, carries independent information about longevity.

A year of resistance training slowed a brain-aging clock — and the effect lasted

A randomized controlled trial published in GeroScience (Gonzalez-Gomez et al., online Feb. 10, 2026) assigned 309 adults aged 62–70 (the LISA trial) to heavy-resistance training, moderate-intensity training, or a non-exercise control group for one year. Using brain-age models trained on resting-state fMRI scans from 2,433 healthy adults, both exercise arms showed reduced estimated brain age (roughly 1.4 to 2.3 years younger than predicted, statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons), and the heavy-training group also showed increased prefrontal connectivity. The benefit was still measurable a year after the program ended — one of the more rigorous demonstrations that lifting weights measurably changes a brain-imaging marker of aging, not just self-reported cognition.

FDA advisers back BPC-157 and five other peptides for compounding — over their own agency's objections

At its July 23–24, 2026 meeting, the FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee voted to recommend adding six peptides — BPC-157, TB-500, KPV, MOTS-c, Epitalon, and Semax — to the list of substances pharmacies may legally compound, while rejecting a seventh, emideltide (DSIP). The BPC-157 vote passed narrowly (reported as 8–6 by Healio), for use in ulcerative colitis, despite FDA scientific staff recommending against adding all seven substances over limited human clinical evidence of safety and effectiveness. A recommendation is not final: FDA must still act before compounding status actually changes, and as of early October, BPC-157 remained off both the approved 503A bulks list and Category 1. BPC-157 and similar peptides are already widely sold and used off-label for injury recovery and inflammation, well ahead of the clinical evidence base — this vote is a case study in how regulatory momentum and public enthusiasm can outrun the data.
