Stem-Cell Retinal Therapy OpRegen Shows Durable Vision Gains at Three Years

At the Foundation Fighting Blindness Retinal Therapeutics Innovation Summit on May 1, 2026, researchers presented 36-month follow-up data from Lineage Cell Therapeutics' Phase 1/2a trial of OpRegen (RG6501), a transplant of stem-cell-derived retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells, in patients with geographic atrophy — the advanced, vision-destroying form of dry age-related macular degeneration. In the trial's less-advanced-disease cohort (roughly 10–12 patients), mean best-corrected visual acuity improved by about +6.2 letters at 36 months, building on a +5.5-letter gain at 24 months; patients whose transplant covered more of the lesion gained as much as +9 letters. Imaging suggested partial restoration of outer retinal structure, including reappearance of an RPE layer and signs associated with photoreceptor recovery. Most experimental stem-cell retinal treatments show a brief functional bump that fades; a continued gain at three years, in a disease with no approved treatment that restores vision, is a rare durability signal for the field.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — Phase 1/2a trial, roughly 10–12 patients in the relevant cohort followed for 36 months; not yet peer-reviewed or published, reported via company and conference disclosure.
How promising
Encouraging for the specific patients who still had less-advanced disease at baseline, but the group is tiny and this remains a single-arm, non-blinded, company-funded study.
Unknowns
Whether gains persist past three years, how OpRegen performs in a randomized controlled trial, and whether it still works once disease is further along. Roche/Genentech are now testing it in a larger Phase 2a trial called GAlette.
Availability
Experimental only — not FDA-approved; available solely through clinical trials.

Daily Multivitamin Modestly Slows Biological Aging in Large COSMOS Trial Analysis

A new analysis of the COSMOS randomized trial, published in Nature Medicine on March 9, 2026, measured five blood-based "epigenetic clocks" in 958 participants (average age about 70) randomized to a daily multivitamin-multimineral (Centrum Silver), cocoa extract, both, or placebo for two years. People taking the multivitamin aged significantly slower on two of the five clocks — including PCGrimAge, which predicts mortality — by an amount researchers equated to roughly four fewer months of biological aging over the two-year period. The effect was larger in people who were already aging faster than average at baseline. Cocoa extract had no measurable effect on any clock. It's one of the larger, better-designed randomized trials to test a cheap, over-the-counter multivitamin against an objective, lab-measured aging marker rather than self-reported health outcomes.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — randomized, placebo-controlled trial, 958 participants, two years of follow-up.
How promising
Modest and statistically significant on two of five clocks, not all five; the clocks carry roughly ±3-year margins of error, so a four-month effect is small relative to that noise.
Unknowns
Whether this biomarker shift translates into any measurable gain in healthspan, disease risk, or lifespan — the trial measured blood markers, not clinical outcomes — and whether it persists after supplementation stops.
Availability
Clinical now — a standard, widely available over-the-counter multivitamin, not a prescription or clinic treatment.

Study of 300+ People Finds Blood NAD+ Doesn't Decline With Age, Challenging a Core Supplement Claim

Researchers at Amsterdam UMC and collaborators directly measured NAD+ in whole blood from more than 300 people across seven independent cohorts, published in Nature Metabolism in May 2026. NAD+ levels did not meaningfully decline with age in any cohort, didn't differ between elite athletes and sedentary people, and didn't shift significantly with exercise or high-protein diets. Nicotinamide riboside supplementation did raise blood NAD+ as expected, but measured levels proved highly sensitive to how blood samples were frozen and handled — which the authors suggest may explain conflicting results in earlier, smaller studies. The sales pitch behind NAD+ pills and IV drips rests on the idea that NAD+ falls with age and needs replenishing; this larger, more rigorous direct-measurement study found no such age-related decline in human blood.

Evidence
Mixed/uncertain evidence — the new data argue against the "NAD+ declines with age" premise, but a separate 2026 study in frail, older Japanese adults did find an association between lower NAD+ and frailty.
In humans
Yes — over 300 people across seven cohorts, direct blood measurement.
How promising
This is a biomarker study, not a treatment trial — it doesn't test whether boosting NAD+ helps anyone; it mainly undercuts the premise marketers use to sell NAD+ products.
Unknowns
Whether NAD+ in other tissues (not blood) declines with age, and whether any controlled human trial shows IV or oral NAD+/precursors produce a measurable anti-aging benefit.
Availability
IV NAD+ and NAD+ precursor supplements are widely available now at wellness clinics and over the counter, but none is FDA-approved for anti-aging use.

JAMA Review: No Evidence Continuous Glucose Monitors Help Healthy People Without Diabetes

A narrative review in JAMA Internal Medicine, published online August 3, 2026, examined the evidence for continuous glucose monitors (CGMs) — now widely marketed as wellness wearables to people without diabetes. The authors concluded there's no scientific evidence that CGMs improve health or prevent disease in people who don't have diabetes, and that evidence even in people with prediabetes or obesity is limited and indirect. In people with type 2 diabetes, CGM use was linked to a modest roughly 0.3-percentage-point reduction in HbA1c in randomized trials. CGMs have become one of the most visible "biohacking" devices, marketed on the idea that real-time glucose data helps optimize health even without diabetes — this review found the direct evidence for that specific claim doesn't yet exist.

Evidence
Little or no credible evidence for benefit in people without diabetes; evidence is stronger, though still modest, in type 2 diabetes.
In humans
Yes — the review synthesizes existing randomized trials and observational studies in humans.
How promising
Not promising as currently marketed to healthy people; the authors warn healthy users may be over-interpreting normal glucose swings as problems.
Unknowns
Whether tracking glucose changes behavior in ways that eventually show up in longer-term health outcomes for people without diabetes — the authors say that question hasn't been well tested.
Availability
Clinically available and FDA-cleared for sale to people without diabetes since 2024, but this review argues against routine use outside a specific clinical reason.

JAMA Internal Medicine, August 3, 2026 — https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.2772

FDA Advisory Committee Recommends BPC-157 and Five Other Peptides for Pharmacy Compounding

On July 23, 2026, the FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee voted 8-6, with one abstention, to recommend adding BPC-157 to the FDA's Section 503A list of substances pharmacies may legally compound. Over the two-day meeting, the committee reviewed seven peptides popular in longevity and recovery circles — BPC-157, TB-500, KPV, MOTS-c, semax, epitalon, and emideltide (DSIP) — and recommended six of the seven, voting against emideltide. FDA staff had recommended against including BPC-157, so the committee's vote went against the agency's own staff recommendation. These peptides, especially BPC-157 and TB-500, are already widely used off-label for injury recovery and inflammation despite having no FDA-approved use; a formal recommendation toward legal compounding access would be a significant regulatory shift, though it is not yet a final rule.

Evidence
Mixed/uncertain evidence — most human data comes from small, uncontrolled studies, with much supporting mechanistic evidence from animal models rather than large randomized human trials.
In humans
Limited — mostly small or observational human studies to date.
How promising
Uncertain; the committee's vote reflects demand and perceived safety more than new clinical-trial evidence of benefit.
Unknowns
Whether the FDA will finalize the addition to the 503A bulks list, which still requires further agency review and rulemaking, and basic dose-response and long-term safety data in humans for most of these peptides.
Availability
Experimental/off-label only for now — none is FDA-approved for any indication, and compounding access, if finalized, would still require a prescription.
