Can anti-obesity drugs slow aging? Mouse data looks strong; human evidence is still early

What happened: UC Berkeley researchers led by Danica Chen gave semaglutide (the drug in Ozempic and Wegovy) to 20-month-old mice — roughly the equivalent of 60-year-old humans — for the rest of their lives, comparing them to mice on a placebo and mice on 25% calorie restriction, a classic life-extension intervention. The semaglutide mice lived an average of 834 days versus 742 for placebo mice, and did better than the calorie-restricted group on maze-navigation and blood-sugar control. Separately, Eli Lilly's aging-research lead, Kevin Duffin, has reported that tirzepatide (Mounjaro/Zepbound) users over 50 showed signs of "biological age" reductions of roughly three to six years on some markers, and Novo Nordisk has reported a preprint finding that semaglutide reduced markers of the heart's biological age by two to four years versus placebo. Why it matters: GLP-1 drugs are already used by tens of millions of people for weight loss and diabetes. If they turn out to meaningfully slow aging itself, rather than just its downstream effects like obesity and diabetes, that would be a significant and unplanned side benefit — but the gap between a mouse lifespan study and proof in healthy humans is large.

Promising but preliminary — strong in mice, early and unreplicated in humans
In humans
Yes for weight loss/diabetes; the "slows aging" claim rests on secondary biomarker analyses (one still an unpublished preprint), not dedicated aging trials

Taken seriously by outside aging researchers, who still stress it's far too early to use these drugs specifically to live longer

Whether any benefit comes from weight loss itself or the drug independently, whether it applies to non-obese people, and whether it extends human lifespan at all

Lab-grown brain cells for Parkinson's pass a one-year safety test, with early signs of helping

What happened: A phase 1/2 trial called STEM-PD transplanted dopamine-producing nerve cells, grown in the lab from human embryonic stem cells, directly into the brains of people with Parkinson's disease. One-year results, published in Nature Medicine, reported no graft-related adverse events, no dyskinesias, and no tumor formation, with PET imaging confirming the transplanted cells survived and are producing dopamine. The findings were also presented at the ISSCR 2026 annual meeting in Montréal in July. Six of the seven participants who completed the trial showed signs of needing less of their usual Parkinson's medication, though outcomes varied between patients. Why it matters: Parkinson's destroys the brain's own dopamine-producing cells, and current drugs only manage symptoms. A safely engrafting, dopamine-producing cell transplant is a step toward actually replacing what the disease destroys, rather than just compensating for it.

Largest trial yet finds glucose monitors meaningfully help diabetics who don't use insulin

What happened: The Dexcom-sponsored CONNECT trial, published in the New England Journal of Medicine on October 14, 2026, randomized 283 adults with type 2 diabetes who weren't on insulin — and whose blood sugar was running above target — to either a Dexcom G7 continuous glucose monitor or usual care, across 22 U.S. primary care practices. After 26 weeks, the CGM group's average HbA1c fell from 8.8% to about 7.2%, nearly a full percentage point more than the usual-care group's drop from 8.7% to 8.0%. At week 26, 68% of CGM users got below 7.5% HbA1c and 46% got below 7.0%. Why it matters: CGMs are heavily marketed to healthy people for general "metabolic optimization," an application a recent JAMA review found no evidence for — but this trial shows a real, substantial benefit in a population that actually has elevated blood sugar and isn't yet using insulin, a much larger group than insulin users alone.

Evidence
Strong, for this specific population — a sizeable randomized trial with a clinically meaningful effect size
In humans
Yes — 283 adults, 265 of whom completed the 26-week trial
How promising
One of the clearer wins yet for CGM technology, likely to expand which non-insulin diabetics are told to use one
Unknowns
Effects beyond 26 weeks, and whether the benefit holds for diabetics closer to target at baseline (enrollment required HbA1c ≥7.5%)
Availability
Dexcom G7 and similar CGMs already widely available and prescribed; this trial strengthens the case for earlier use in non-insulin type 2 diabetes

Stem-cell therapy for frailty misses its main goal at six months, but shows a later effect

What happened: Longeveron's phase 2b trial of laromestrocel (Lomecel-B), a stem-cell therapy derived from donor bone marrow, gave a single IV infusion to 148 adults aged 70–85 with mild-to-moderate frailty. Results published in Cell Stem Cell (reported February 2026) showed the therapy did not significantly beat placebo on its primary endpoint — six-minute walk distance at day 180 — but a prespecified exploratory look at day 270 found a meaningful improvement, and higher doses tracked with greater gains and lower levels of a biomarker (soluble Tie-2) linked to vascular aging. Why it matters: Frailty — a measurable decline in strength, mobility, and resilience with age — has no approved drug treatment. A missed primary endpoint with a possible delayed benefit is a genuinely ambiguous result, not a clean win, and it illustrates how hard this kind of trial is to read.

Evidence
Mixed/uncertain — the primary endpoint failed, but a secondary signal at a later timepoint, plus a dose-response pattern, keeps the therapy's case alive
In humans
Yes — a 148-patient, placebo-controlled, dose-ranging trial
How promising

Unknowns
Whether the day-270 effect is real or a multiple-comparisons artifact, and whether it would replicate in a trial built around that timepoint
Availability
Experimental only; no approved frailty indication exists for this or any other stem-cell product

Sports-medicine groups warn "healing peptides" like BPC-157 are sold on promise, not proof

What happened: Sports-medicine surgeons writing in a July 2026 newsletter from the American Association of North American Surgeons (AANA), along with a separate peer-reviewed review, say injectable peptides marketed for injury recovery and regeneration — most prominently BPC-157 and TB-500 — have almost no rigorous human clinical-trial data behind them, despite widespread online sale and use. The FDA has never approved either peptide for any use and in 2023 classified BPC-157 as ineligible for legal compounding. The surgeons note there's no approved pharmaceutical-grade formulation, no validated dosing, and no standardized route of administration. Why it matters: These peptides have become popular in fitness and biohacking circles on the strength of animal studies and anecdote, sold by compounding pharmacies and online vendors operating in a regulatory gray zone — exactly the gap the evidence-strength rule exists to flag.

Evidence
Little or no credible evidence in humans, despite promising animal data
In humans
Essentially no controlled human trials; existing support comes from animal models and low-quality case reports
How promising
Not promising as currently sold and used — the clinical case is unproven, and product quality/purity from unregulated suppliers is a real added risk
Unknowns
Basic human dosing, safety over repeated use, and whether animal-model benefits translate to people at all
Availability
Widely available through compounding pharmacies and online sellers despite no FDA approval — availability has far outrun evidence
