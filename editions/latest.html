Daily Cortisol Rhythm, Not Just Its Average Level, Tracks Cognitive Decline in Older Adults

Researchers analyzed salivary cortisol samples taken at three points across the day (morning, afternoon, evening) in 3,895 older adults (2,503 Black, 1,392 White; average age 76.7) from the Chicago Health and Aging Project, following participants for up to 11 years. People with greater variation in cortisol across the day tended to show slower cognitive decline, while those with the highest sustained overall cortisol levels tended to decline faster. Black participants had flatter daily cortisol curves than White participants, but the pattern linking cortisol shape to cognitive decline looked similar across both groups. The associations with Alzheimer's disease risk specifically were less clear than the associations with general cognitive decline. The study was published in JAMA Network Open in September 2026. Chronic stress and dysregulated cortisol have long been suspected contributors to cognitive aging, but this is one of the largest and most racially diverse cohorts to track the daily cortisol rhythm — not just a single blood draw — against long-term cognitive outcomes, suggesting the shape of the daily curve carries information beyond the average level.

Evidence
Mixed / uncertain evidence
In humans
Yes — observational cohort of 3,895 older adults followed for up to 11 years
How promising
A well-powered, long-running cohort finding, but associational — it doesn't establish that intervening on cortisol rhythm would change outcomes
Unknowns
Whether changing the daily cortisol curve through intervention would actually slow decline, and why the pattern didn't clearly track Alzheimer's-specific risk
Availability
Not a treatment — cortisol testing exists clinically for other purposes; using daily rhythm to manage cognitive decline is not validated practice

First Controlled Trial of Rapamycin Plus Exercise Finds No Added Benefit — and a Possible Modest Downside

In the first randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial to pair weekly rapamycin (sirolimus) with a structured exercise program, researchers randomized 40 sedentary adults aged 65–85 to 6 mg sirolimus or matched placebo once weekly for 13 weeks, while both groups followed the same home-based chair-stand and stationary-bike program three times a week. Both groups improved on chair-stand performance, but sirolimus did not outperform placebo; a sensitivity analysis found sirolimus modestly underperformed placebo (a complete-case difference of −2.46 repetitions, p=0.045). Sirolimus was also linked to more adverse events, including one serious case of pneumonia judged possibly related to the drug. The trial, led by Stanfield and colleagues, was published in the Journal of Cachexia, Sarcopenia and Muscle in April 2026. Rapamycin is one of the most talked-about longevity drugs and is already used off-label by biohackers, partly on the theory that it amplifies exercise's benefits; this is the first controlled human test of that specific combination, and it found no added benefit — and a signal of possible harm to exercise gains.

Evidence
Little or no credible evidence (for the exercise-enhancing claim — the trial itself is a small but rigorous negative result)
In humans
Yes — 40 adults aged 65–85, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, over 13 weeks
How promising
Not promising for this specific use; a rigorous small trial found no benefit and a possible modest downside
Unknowns
Whether a different dose, timing, or longer duration would change the result, and whether rapamycin's other claimed effects hold up on their own
Availability
FDA-approved for organ-transplant rejection and other indications; not approved for aging or exercise enhancement — used off-label for that

Large Yale Study Finds Nearly Half of Older Adults Improve Physically or Cognitively Over Time — and Positive Age Beliefs Predict Who

Using data from 11,314 community-dwelling adults aged 65 and older in the Health and Retirement Study, Yale researchers Becca Levy and Martin Slade tracked walking speed and cognitive performance over up to 12 years of follow-up. About 45% of participants improved in cognition, walking speed, or both (32% improved cognitively, 28% physically) rather than simply declining. People who held more positive beliefs about aging at baseline were significantly more likely to show these improvements, even after adjusting for age, sex, education, chronic disease, depression, and a genetic Alzheimer's risk marker (APOE). The study was published in the journal Geriatrics in March 2026. Most aging research assumes decline is the default trajectory; this study's scale — over 11,000 people followed for up to 12 years — makes a harder case that meaningful improvement is common, and that self-perception of aging is independently associated with it.

Evidence
Mixed / uncertain evidence
In humans
Yes — 11,314 adults 65+, followed for up to 12 years
How promising
A large, well-adjusted observational association, but the authors note it shows correlation, not causation
Unknowns
Whether deliberately shifting age beliefs would causally produce improvement, and what biological mechanism would explain it
Availability
Not a treatment — no approved "age-belief" intervention exists; small trials exist but aren't validated clinical care

UTMB Launches Trial Testing Whether a Weight-Loss Drug Can Slow Biological Aging Itself

University of Texas Medical Branch researchers, funded by the Moody Endowment, launched the "Moody Longevity Trial," testing whether tirzepatide (the GLP-1/GIP drug marketed as Zepbound) can slow biological aging. Participants aged 55 to 70 take the drug for 24 weeks, followed by a 12-week observation period after stopping, while researchers track biological age using DNA-methylation ("epigenetic") clocks alongside physical performance, mood, and inflammation markers. Lead investigators have said they hope to see biological aging slow by 10–15%, but the trial is still underway and has not reported results. UTMB announced the trial in February 2026. GLP-1 drugs are already reshaping cardiology and metabolic medicine; this is one of the first trials designed specifically to test their effect on biological aging itself, including whether any benefit persists after stopping the drug.

Trial underway in humans; no results reported yet
How promising
Too early to say — the "10–15% slowing" figure is the researchers' stated goal, not a measured outcome
Unknowns
Essentially the entire outcome — whether tirzepatide affects biological-age markers at all, and whether any effect would persist after stopping
Availability
Tirzepatide is FDA-approved for diabetes and weight loss; using it to slow aging is not an approved use and remains unproven

Rheumatologist Argues GLP-1 Drugs May Fight Inflammation Directly, Not Just Through Weight Loss

At the Congress of Clinical Rheumatology West in September 2026, Dr. Leonard Calabrese of the Cleveland Clinic presented an argument, drawing on existing trial data, that GLP-1 receptor agonist drugs (such as semaglutide and tirzepatide) may reduce inflammation directly and independent of the weight loss they cause, potentially through a proposed "brain-immune axis" mechanism. He suggested this could eventually support using these drugs as broader anti-inflammatory or anti-aging treatments, beyond diabetes and obesity. If true, it would help explain reported benefits in conditions like heart failure and kidney disease that go beyond what weight loss alone would predict, and would strengthen the case for studying these drugs as general anti-inflammatory agents rather than purely metabolic ones.

Not tested as a standalone claim here — a mechanistic hypothesis built on secondary signals from existing GLP-1 trials
How promising
Biologically plausible given secondary findings in cardiovascular/kidney trials, but this presentation is interpretation, not new primary data
Unknowns
Whether the effect is truly independent of weight loss, the actual mechanism, and whether it would justify prescribing for inflammation alone
Availability
GLP-1 drugs are FDA-approved for diabetes and obesity; prescribing for anti-inflammatory or anti-aging effects is not an approved use
