Semaglutide Extended Lifespan in Old Mice — Without Calorie Restriction

A University of California, Berkeley-led team, funded by the NIH, treated 20-month-old (functionally elderly) female mice with the GLP-1 drug semaglutide for three months, continuing treatment for some animals until end of life. Treated mice had a median lifespan of 834 days versus 742 days for untreated controls — about 12% longer — and showed improved muscle function, better spatial memory, more exploratory behavior, and better blood-sugar control than mice on a comparable calorie-restricted diet. Semaglutide (Ozempic/Wegovy) is known for weight loss and metabolic effects in humans; this is direct evidence in a mammal that starting the drug late in life can extend lifespan and blunt multiple aging hallmarks, not just treat obesity or diabetes.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary — a single, well-designed animal study, published in Nature, but conducted only in female mice.
In humans
No — mouse model only. (A separate human HIV-trial analysis, covered in our September 9 edition, found semaglutide slowed epigenetic "aging clocks" in people, but that is a different, narrower dataset.)
How promising
A real, measured lifespan extension plus consistent functional benefits across multiple systems is a strong signal, but it comes from one lab, one sex, one species.
Unknowns
Whether the effect replicates in male mice or humans, the long-term risk profile for non-obese people using the drug for decades, and the dose/timing needed.
Availability
FDA-approved for diabetes, obesity, and cardiovascular risk reduction — not for anti-aging or longevity. Off-label longevity use is not supported by this evidence.

Nature, September 2, 2026 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-10940-7

Aging Doesn't Follow One Molecular Script, Eight-Year Twin Study Finds

Researchers at King's College London, led by Julia El-Sayed Moustafa, used the TwinsUK cohort's MultiMuTHER study to track whole-blood gene expression (~16,000 genes) and roughly 1,197 serum metabolites in 335 women ages 32–80, with repeated visits over up to eight years (median six years between first and last visit). More than 5,000 genes and 181 metabolites changed at the population level over time, but individual participants' own trajectories frequently diverged from — and sometimes ran opposite to — those population-wide trends. Most "biological age" clocks and aging biomarkers are built and validated against population averages, so if people genuinely age along different molecular paths, a single popular clock could mischaracterize an individual's real risk trajectory.

Evidence
Strong evidence — a large, repeated-measures multi-omic dataset — for the finding that individual variation is substantial; this is a mapping study, not a treatment claim.
In humans
Yes — entirely human, but limited to women of European ancestry who were mostly already past menopause when the project began.
How promising
Reshapes how aging biomarkers should be interpreted rather than pointing to a new intervention.
Unknowns
Whether the same pattern holds in men and other ancestries, and which individual trajectories actually predict future disease.
Availability
Not applicable — a basic-science finding, not a clinical test or treatment.

Science, 2026 (reported by LiveScience, September 8, 2026) — https://www.livescience.com/health/aging/...individualized-and-context-dependent

A Single Evening Workout Protected Memory After a Short Night of Sleep

A team led by Emmanuel Frimpong at Concordia University's Sleep, Cognition & Neuroimaging Laboratory in Montreal had 88 healthy young adults do 30 minutes of moderate-intensity evening cycling before a night restricted to 4.5 hours in bed. The next morning, participants who exercised beforehand scored better on a declarative (face-name) memory test than those who underwent the same sleep restriction without exercising, with slow-wave sleep appearing to help explain the effect. It offers a simple, low-cost behavioral way to blunt one of the clearest cognitive costs of short sleep — relevant to shift workers, new parents, or anyone facing an unavoidable short night.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary — one randomized study; needs replication across ages and with additional memory measures.
In humans
Yes — 88 healthy young adults.
How promising
A specific, statistically significant exercise-by-sleep interaction on delayed recall is encouraging but comes from a single trial.
Unknowns
Whether the effect holds for older adults, chronic (not just single-night) sleep restriction, or different exercise types, timing, or intensity.
Availability
Not a clinical treatment — the "intervention" (evening moderate exercise) is already freely available to anyone.

Sleep, published online August 26, 2026 (reported by Medscape) — https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/moderate-exercise-before-bed-may-protect-against-sleep-2026a1000v05

FDA Panel Votes Against Capricor's Duchenne Heart-Damage Cell Therapy

On July 29, 2026, the FDA's Cellular, Tissue, and Gene Therapies Advisory Committee voted 9-3 that Capricor Therapeutics had not shown "substantial evidence" that its cell therapy deramiocel (allogeneic cardiosphere-derived cells) effectively treats cardiomyopathy in Duchenne muscular dystrophy, based on the Phase 3 HOPE-3 trial. After Capricor submitted additional analyses, the FDA extended its target decision date to November 22, 2026. Deramiocel would be one of the first cell therapies aimed specifically at the heart damage that is a leading cause of death in Duchenne patients, and the panel's non-binding but lopsided vote is a reminder of how hard it is for cell therapies to clear the FDA's effectiveness bar even after years of trial data.

Evidence
Mixed / uncertain evidence — FDA reviewers and the company disagreed sharply over how to interpret the same randomized Phase 3 trial's statistics.
In humans
Yes — the Phase 3 HOPE-3 randomized trial in Duchenne patients.
How promising
Uncertain on the cardiomyopathy endpoint the panel reviewed; some panelists saw more potential in the therapy's effect on upper-limb function, which was not the primary focus of the vote.
Unknowns
Whether the additional data Capricor submitted will change the FDA's final decision by November, or whether the therapy shows clear benefit on any endpoint.
Availability
Not approved — experimental only, with the Biologics License Application still under FDA review.

Supplement Maker Touts NR's Effect on Muscle Aging — Read the Fine Print

A reanalysis pooling a five-month human trial of 1,000 mg/day nicotinamide riboside (NR, sold as Niagen), separate short high-intensity interval training (HIIT) studies, and new experiments in human muscle cells — using seven different epigenetic clocks — found NR supplementation was associated with roughly a 2.5-year reduction in muscle epigenetic age acceleration on one clock (MEAT), with smaller but statistically significant reductions on two others. The paper's own title describes "divergent effects" of NR versus exercise training, meaning the two interventions did not move all the clocks the same way. NAD+ boosters like NR are heavily marketed for anti-aging despite thin human evidence — a gap our September 9 edition also flagged — and this more tissue-specific dataset was announced and publicized by Niagen Bioscience, the company that sells the supplement, a direct financial conflict of interest.

Evidence
Mixed / uncertain evidence — a company-publicized reanalysis of small pre-existing trials, not a new independently funded randomized study, with effects that didn't line up consistently across all seven clocks used.
In humans
Yes for the underlying NR trial (muscle biopsies), but small and only five months long; some supporting data came from cells in a dish, not people.
How promising
A real biological signal worth an independent follow-up, but the industry sponsorship and inconsistent clock results argue for caution.
Unknowns
Whether an independently funded, adequately powered trial would replicate the effect, and whether any epigenetic-clock change in muscle translates into a measurable functional or clinical benefit.
Availability
NR supplements are already sold over-the-counter; this analysis does not establish a proven anti-aging benefit.

Aging Cell, published August 2026 — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/acel.70638
