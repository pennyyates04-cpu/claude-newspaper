A Single CRISPR Infusion Kept Cholesterol Down for a Full Year

In a Cleveland Clinic-led Phase 1 trial, 15 patients with medication-resistant lipid disorders received a one-time IV infusion of CTX310, a CRISPR-Cas9 therapy from CRISPR Therapeutics that permanently switches off the ANGPTL3 gene in liver cells. Twelve months after dosing, the highest-dose group showed average reductions of 79% in ANGPTL3 protein, 53% in LDL cholesterol, and 48% in triglycerides from baseline, with no treatment-related dose-limiting toxic effects. Results were presented at the European Society of Cardiology Congress 2026 and published simultaneously in the New England Journal of Medicine. Most cholesterol-lowering drugs require daily or periodic dosing for life — this suggests a single treatment could produce durable, whole-body metabolic change, a landmark test case for in-vivo gene editing beyond cholesterol.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary — a real human trial with a clean one-year durability signal, but small (n=15), open-label, and without a placebo comparison group.
In humans
Yes — this is a first-in-human Phase 1 trial.
How promising
The size and persistence of the lipid drop at one year is striking, but the trial wasn't designed to show it prevents heart attacks or strokes.
Unknowns
Long-term safety beyond one year, whether the effect holds indefinitely, and whether it reduces cardiovascular events.
Availability
Experimental only — Phase 1 data; years from possible approval.

Walking Faster Can Make Up for Walking Less, Large UK Study Finds

Researchers analyzed data from 103,684 UK Biobank participants (ages 40–69) who wore wrist accelerometers for seven days between 2013 and 2015, then tracked mortality over roughly the following decade. People logging 7,500–10,000 steps a day had the lowest death risk, in line with existing guidance. But among people who took fewer than 5,000 steps a day, those who walked at a brisker pace (more than about 80 steps per minute during their walking bouts) had mortality risk comparable to people logging far more steps at a slower pace. Step-count targets can feel unreachable for people with mobility limits or sedentary jobs — this offers intensity as a second, equally evidence-backed lever.

Evidence
Strong evidence in scale — a large, device-measured prospective cohort — but still observational, so it shows association rather than proof of causation.
In humans
Yes — entirely human, objectively measured activity data.
How promising
Practical and immediately actionable; reverse causation (healthier people naturally walk faster) remains a live alternative explanation.
Unknowns
Whether deliberately training to walk faster produces the same risk reduction as being a naturally brisk walker.
Availability
Not a treatment — free, actionable lifestyle guidance today.

UCLA Review: The Evidence for Trendy "Healing" Peptides Doesn't Match the Hype

UCLA Health researchers systematically reviewed 565 studies on six peptides widely marketed online and in wellness clinics for injury recovery, muscle building, and anti-aging: BPC-157, TB-500, CJC-1295, MK-677, ipamorelin, and GHK-Cu. More than two-thirds of the studies were animal-only, and the handful of human studies showed at most modest benefits for non-orthopedic uses, with essentially no rigorous evidence of musculoskeletal benefit. Reviewers also flagged a halted MK-677 trial linked to congestive heart failure risk and a conflict of interest in one of the largest BPC-157 studies. These unregulated peptides are a fast-growing part of the biohacking and regenerative-medicine market, often sold without FDA approval for the uses being marketed.

Evidence
Little or no credible evidence for the marketed musculoskeletal/anti-aging claims — a systematic review of existing (mostly weak) studies, not new data.
In humans
Partially — most underlying research is in animals; human data is sparse and low quality.
How promising
Not promising as currently sold; authors say the safety and efficacy evidence for long-term human use "just isn't there."
Unknowns
Whether properly designed human trials would show real musculoskeletal benefit, and what's actually in unregulated products.
Availability
Not FDA-approved for the marketed uses; sold off-label through wellness clinics and online.

First Human Study Links Specific Gut Bacteria Directly to Brain Chemical Levels

University of Surrey and University of Roehampton researchers studied 61 healthy young women, combining shotgun metagenomic sequencing of stool samples with proton MR spectroscopy brain scans measuring GABA and glutamate in three brain regions. Microbial genetic pathways for producing and breaking down GABA, glutamate, short-chain fatty acids, and other neuroactive compounds correlated with region-specific brain chemical levels, and with participants' anxiety, depressive symptoms, and sleep quality. The gut-brain axis is usually inferred indirectly, through behavior or blood markers — this is a more direct human link between gut microbes' metabolic capacity and actual brain neurochemistry.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary — a well-designed mechanistic study, but small and narrow in population.
In humans
Yes — entirely human, using live neuroimaging plus stool metagenomics.
How promising
Mechanistically compelling, but shows correlation in one demographic slice only, with no intervention tested.
Unknowns
Whether the relationship holds in men, older adults, or people with diagnosed mental health conditions, and whether changing gut microbes shifts brain chemistry or mood.
Availability
Research only — no diagnostic or therapeutic product exists from this yet.

Molecular Psychiatry, published online September 1, 2026 (University of Surrey release) — https://www.surrey.ac.uk/news/gut-bacteria-linked-key-brain-chemicals-first-its-kind-human-study

Gut Microbiome Predicts Inflammation and Disease Risk Better Than Age Alone

Researchers analyzed fecal microbiota, 30 plasma cytokines, and 19 metabolic/physiological measures in 1,199 adults ages 20–72 from Denmark's population-based DanFunD cohort. Gut microbiota composition explained more of the variation in inflammatory markers and physiological measures than chronological age did — outperforming age for 97% of the cytokines and 84% of the physiological variables studied. People with a specific "Bacteroides 2" gut-microbiome profile showed elevated inflammatory markers and adverse metabolic signs as early as their 20s and 30s, resembling an "inflammaging" pattern up to 37 years ahead of schedule. If confirmed, gut-microbiome typing could flag people heading toward chronic inflammation and metabolic disease decades before symptoms appear.

Evidence
Mixed / uncertain evidence for real-world use — a large, well-characterized human cohort, but purely observational (cross-sectional).
In humans
Yes — 1,199 adults, entirely human data.
How promising
A striking association in a sizable cohort, but a single-country population with no intervention tested to confirm it lowers disease risk.
Unknowns
Whether the Bacteroides 2 profile causes inflammaging or just marks it, and whether the pattern replicates elsewhere.
Availability
Research-stage only — not an available clinical test.

Nature Communications, published online September 8–9, 2026 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-77499-9

Hidden Brain Wiring May Help Preserve Language as Gray Matter Shrinks

USC's Stevens Neuroimaging and Informatics Institute analyzed brain scans and cognitive testing from 459 community-dwelling adults age 60 and older in India, focusing on "superficial white matter" — short nerve-fiber bundles just beneath the cortex that connect nearby brain regions, distinct from the deep white-matter tracts usually studied in aging research. Gray-matter loss was the strongest overall predictor of cognitive decline, but healthier superficial white matter was specifically tied to better-preserved language ability, and appeared to weaken the link between gray-matter loss and cognitive impairment when it was in good condition. This points to a distinct, understudied brain structure that may buffer the cognitive effects of gray-matter aging.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary — a reasonably sized human cohort study, but cross-sectional, so it can't show causation.
In humans
Yes — 459 adults, brain imaging and cognitive testing.
How promising
A plausible and novel structural finding that needs replication and longitudinal follow-up to confirm the buffering effect is real.
Unknowns
Whether interventions could improve superficial white matter health, and whether the pattern holds in other populations and age groups.
Availability
Basic research only — no diagnostic or clinical application yet.

Alzheimer's & Dementia: The Journal of the Alzheimer's Association, ScienceDaily coverage September 13, 2026 — https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/09/260913081921.htm
