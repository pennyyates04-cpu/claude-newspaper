A Large Study Undercuts the Idea That NAD+ Levels Fall With Age

Researchers led by Riekelt Houtkooper at Amsterdam UMC, with first author Maria Trętowicz, measured whole-blood NAD+ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide, a molecule cells need to produce energy) across seven independent human cohorts totaling more than 300 people. They found NAD+ levels stayed stable across age groups and did not shift with the lifestyle interventions tested, though levels did change in response to supplementation with nicotinamide riboside, an NAD+ precursor. The study was published in Nature Metabolism and covered by NutraIngredients on June 2, 2026. A separate, not-yet-peer-reviewed preprint posted in February 2026 reached the same conclusion. The idea that NAD+ declines with age and that boosting it can slow aging underpins a large and growing market for NAD+ pills and IV infusions. This is one of the largest look-backs yet at whether the basic premise — that blood NAD+ actually falls with age — holds up.

Evidence
Mixed / uncertain evidence
In humans
Yes — over 300 people across seven cohorts, though the design pools existing cohort data rather than running a new prospective trial.
How promising
Credible challenge to a widely repeated claim given the sample size and consistency, but other studies have found NAD+ declines in specific tissues like muscle and brain — whole-blood levels may be the wrong thing to measure.
Unknowns
Whether NAD+ declines in tissues that matter for aging even if blood levels don't, and whether any NAD+-boosting product provides a measurable benefit to healthy people.
Availability
Not a clinical treatment — NAD+ pills and infusions remain widely sold despite the unresolved evidence.

FDA Lets a Daily Osteoporosis Pill Skip Years of Fracture-Rate Testing

Entera Bio's EB613 is a once-daily oral tablet containing a parathyroid hormone analog, designed to build bone rather than just slow its loss — the mechanism behind existing injectable drugs like teriparatide (Forteo). The FDA agreed in June 2026 to let the company run a single, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 3 trial in roughly 750 postmenopausal women with osteoporosis, using the percent change in total hip bone mineral density (BMD) at 12 months as the primary endpoint, rather than requiring a longer trial powered to detect actual fracture reduction. In an earlier placebo-controlled phase 2 trial, EB613 produced bone-density gains in the spine, hip, and femoral neck after six months. Entera plans to start the phase 3 trial in late 2026, with topline data expected in the second half of 2028. Most osteoporosis treatments are injectable or infused; an effective oral pill could be easier for patients to stay on long-term, and the FDA's willingness to accept a bone-density endpoint instead of years of fracture data — a regulatory shift finalized in December 2025 — could also speed up development of other osteoporosis drugs.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — phase 2 testing showed bone-density gains after six months; the pivotal phase 3 trial (about 750 women) has not yet begun.
How promising
Real phase 2 density signal with an already-validated mechanism, but bone density is a surrogate for fracture risk, not fracture risk itself.
Unknowns
Whether the bone-density gains translate into fewer fractures, and the drug's long-term safety profile in a large population.
Availability
Experimental only — not FDA-approved; the registrational trial has not yet enrolled patients.

South Korean Hospital Launches a Randomized Trial of a Patient's Own Fat Stem Cells for Knee Arthritis

Severance Hospital, together with Seoul National University Hospital and Inha University Hospital, was selected in July 2026 for South Korea's "Advanced Regenerative Medicine Clinical Research Activation Support Project," backed by roughly 2.9 billion won ($2.1 million) in government funding over 21 months. Led by orthopedic surgeon Kim Sunghwan, the multi-center trial will enroll 165 patients with knee osteoarthritis in a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled design, injecting each patient's own adipose (fat)-derived mesenchymal stem cells into the knee to assess pain relief and possible cartilage regeneration. Stem-cell injections for knee arthritis are already sold at clinics in many countries, but rigorous, blinded, placebo-controlled evidence for autologous fat-derived cells specifically has been limited. A trial of this size and design could produce some of the more credible human data yet on whether the approach does more than a placebo injection.

Evidence
Little or no credible evidence (current commercial use); trial is designed to test this directly
In humans
Trial just launched with no data yet; a similar but distinct therapy (JointStem) has reported five-year benefits in company-issued, not independently peer-reviewed, materials from a separate earlier trial.
How promising
The randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled design clears a meaningfully higher evidence bar than most stem-cell-for-arthritis offerings, but there are no results yet to evaluate.
Unknowns
Whether the therapy outperforms placebo, and if so, by how much and for how long.
Availability
Not yet available through this trial (still enrolling); similar injections are already sold commercially elsewhere, generally without comparable evidence.

A Meta-Analysis Finds Platelet-Rich Plasma Doesn't Beat Placebo for Tennis Elbow

A meta-analysis pooling six randomized controlled trials covering 355 patients with lateral epicondylitis (tennis elbow) found that platelet-rich plasma (PRP) injections — which use a concentrate of a patient's own blood platelets to try to stimulate healing — produced no statistically significant improvement in pain or function compared with placebo injections at any time point measured. The analysis was published in the American Journal of Sports Medicine in early 2026 and presented at the International Congress on Shoulder and Elbow Surgery in September 2026, with results reported by Healio on September 25, 2026. PRP is widely marketed and used for tendon injuries, including by professional athletes, often at significant out-of-pocket cost. This adds to a growing body of placebo-controlled evidence — including earlier negative trials for Achilles tendinopathy and ACL reconstruction — suggesting PRP's benefit for tendon conditions may be smaller than its popularity implies.

Evidence
Little or no credible evidence (for lateral epicondylitis specifically)
In humans
Yes — six randomized, placebo-controlled trials totaling 355 patients.
How promising
Consistent with an earlier 2020 meta-analysis of five placebo-controlled trials that also found no benefit. Some regenerative-medicine researchers dispute the conclusion, arguing higher-platelet-concentration formulations may still help — not yet confirmed in placebo-controlled trials.
Unknowns
Whether specific PRP formulations (platelet concentration, leukocyte content) perform better, and whether PRP works for other conditions where evidence is more mixed.
Availability
Already widely available and commonly sold at orthopedic and sports-medicine clinics, despite this trial evidence.

A Short Cold Dip Between Sauna Rounds Eases Heat Strain, Bangor University Study Finds

Researchers at Bangor University (Tomos Owen, Callum Giles, Samuel Leaney, Geoff Coombs, and Samuel Oliver), working with a Welsh sauna business, tested 16 healthy adults in a crossover design: two 15-minute sauna bouts at 85°C, each followed by a 10-minute cooling break either in 23°C air alone or in air plus a 90-second, 20°C whole-body cold-water immersion. The cold-water cooling break reduced the rise in heart rate and core temperature during the second sauna bout and lowered how hot and strained participants felt, while temperature and sweat rate returned to normal faster afterward. The study was published online ahead of print in Experimental Physiology on July 2, 2026. Sauna use is popular and growing, often paired informally with cold plunges, but there's been little controlled data on how a brief cold dip during a multi-round sauna session actually affects the body's heat and cardiovascular strain in the moment.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — 16 healthy adults in a controlled, counterbalanced crossover design.
How promising
The within-person crossover design reasonably isolates the cold dip's effect, but the sample is small and the outcomes are acute physiological strain markers, not long-term health outcomes.
Unknowns
Whether easing acute strain translates into any longer-term health benefit or risk difference, and whether the effect holds at different water temperatures, dip durations, or sauna temperatures.
Availability
Freely available now — this describes a way of using an existing practice (sauna bathing), not a drug or device.

Experimental Physiology, July 2, 2026 — https://doi.org/10.1113/EP093626
