Stem-Cell Heart Therapy Misses Its Goal in Pivotal Infant Trial, but Safety Signal Holds

Longeveron announced topline results from ELPIS II, a Phase 2b trial testing its stem-cell product laromestrocel as an add-on to standard heart surgery in 40 infants with hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS), a fatal birth defect in which the heart's left side is critically underdeveloped. Infants received a single intramyocardial dose of laromestrocel or surgery alone during a routine palliative procedure, and were followed for 12 months. The trial did not meet its primary endpoint — change in right ventricular ejection fraction at month 12 showed almost no difference between groups (−0.7 percentage points; 95% CI −7.3 to 5.9; p=0.83). HLHS is one of the deadliest congenital heart defects, and current surgical palliation alone leaves many children facing heart failure or transplant — a stem-cell adjunct that improved heart function could change how these infants are managed long-term, but this trial shows that promise from smaller, earlier studies didn't translate into a clear functional benefit here.

Evidence
Mixed / uncertain evidence — a randomized, controlled Phase 2b trial, but it missed its primary efficacy endpoint.
In humans
Yes — 40 infants with HLHS, randomized 1:1 to treatment or standard-of-care surgery.
How promising
The efficacy signal on heart function was essentially flat, but no deaths occurred among treated infants versus one in the control group, prompting a closer look at the full dataset with the FDA.
Unknowns
Whether secondary and exploratory endpoints (still being analyzed) show any benefit, and what the FDA will conclude from a meeting on next steps.
Availability
Experimental only — a pivotal trial toward potential approval, now complicated by the missed primary endpoint.

A Gene-Activation Tool Half the Size of CRISPR Could Widen What In Vivo Gene Therapy Can Reach

Stanford Medicine researchers, led by ophthalmology professor Yang Sun, developed TIGRa, an ultra-compact programmable gene activator built to fit inside a single AAV (adeno-associated virus) — the workhorse delivery vehicle for in vivo gene therapy. At under half the size of dSpCas9-based CRISPR activators, TIGRa can switch on up to 12 genes at once from one compact construct. In a mouse model of retinal injury, a single AAV-delivered dose of TIGRa activated a neuroprotective gene directly inside the eye, helping retinal ganglion cells survive and preserving visual function. Many CRISPR-based gene-activation tools are too bulky to package into a single AAV, forcing researchers toward less-efficient split-vector workarounds — a smaller, multiplexed tool like TIGRa could make single-shot, in vivo gene therapies feasible for a wider range of diseases, including ones needing multiple genes turned on at once. It's a platform advance, not a treatment for one disease — its real test will be whether it works as well in other tissues and eventually in humans.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary — peer-reviewed and mechanistically clean, but the therapeutic result has so far only been shown in mice.
In humans
No — mouse retinal-injury model only.
How promising
The compactness and multiplexing address a real bottleneck in gene therapy delivery, but efficacy in a disease-relevant human context hasn't been tested.
Unknowns
Whether TIGRa performs as well in other organs and larger animals, its long-term safety profile, and how it compares to existing CRISPR activators.
Availability
Basic/preclinical research only — a laboratory tool, not a clinical product.

NAD+ Levels in Blood Don't Actually Track With Age, Undercutting a Popular IV-Drip Rationale

A narrative review published in Frontiers in Aging examined the clinical evidence behind intravenous NAD+ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) and its precursors, a fast-growing wellness-clinic offering marketed for energy, recovery, and anti-aging. The review highlights a large cross-sectional analysis showing that whole-blood NAD+ concentrations were not independently associated with chronological age once disease burden and comorbidity were factored in — echoing a separate seven-cohort, 303-person study that found no consistent NAD+ decline with age once blood-handling artifacts were corrected for. "Boosting declining NAD+ levels" is the core marketing pitch for NAD+ IV drips, which can cost hundreds of dollars per session at wellness clinics — if NAD+ doesn't reliably decline with age in the way assumed, that specific rationale for IV NAD+ as an anti-aging intervention weakens considerably, even though NAD+ still plays a real role in cellular energy metabolism.

Evidence
Mixed / uncertain evidence for the anti-aging marketing claim — solid underlying data on the NAD+/age relationship, but the review synthesizes existing (mostly small, non-placebo-controlled) studies rather than new trial data.
In humans
Yes — the underlying NAD+/age data comes from human cross-sectional cohorts; the review also covers small human IV-NAD+ tolerability studies.
How promising
Not promising for the anti-aging rationale specifically, though the review notes IV NAD+ may still have narrower, unproven uses under investigation.
Unknowns
Whether IV NAD+ produces any measurable functional or longevity benefit in properly controlled human trials, independent of the age question.
Availability
Not FDA-approved for anti-aging or wellness use; widely sold off-label at IV therapy clinics.

A Single Workout Measurably Changes How the Brain Handles Mental Tasks

Researchers from Japan's AIST, working with UT Southwestern Medical Center, used repeated fMRI scans to track brain activity in healthy young and older adults before and after a single session of aerobic exercise, while participants repeated the same cognitive task multiple times. By separating the effect of exercise itself from the effect of simply practicing the task, the team found aerobic exercise measurably altered regional brain activity patterns during the cognitive task, and that in older adults, a bigger drop in brain activity with repeated practice tracked with better task performance — suggesting more efficient neural processing rather than decline. Much of the "exercise is good for your brain" research relies on activity trackers or long-term outcomes; this adds direct, controlled neuroimaging evidence of an acute brain-activity change from a single workout, and a method for telling exercise effects apart from simple task-practice effects.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary — a controlled, well-designed crossover neuroimaging study, though likely a modest sample typical of fMRI research.
In humans
Yes — both young and older healthy adults, using live fMRI before and after acute exercise.
How promising
Mechanistically informative and methodologically careful, but a single-session, lab-based effect that doesn't yet show durable cognitive benefits from repeated exercise.
Unknowns
Whether the acute brain-activity changes translate into durable improvements in memory or executive function, and how exercise intensity affects the size of the effect.
Availability
Not a clinical intervention — basic research; exercise itself is freely available today.

First NHS-Run Trial Finds Single-Dose Psilocybin Eases Treatment-Resistant Depression

King's College London researchers ran the first publicly funded, placebo-controlled UK trial testing psilocybin-assisted therapy inside Britain's National Health Service. In this randomized, double-blind Phase 2 feasibility trial, 60 adults with treatment-resistant major depression (an inadequate response to at least two prior antidepressants, or one antidepressant plus therapy) received a single 25-mg dose of psilocybin or placebo, each paired with structured psychological support before, during, and after dosing. The single dose was feasible to deliver within routine NHS care and produced large preliminary reductions in depressive symptoms among the psilocybin group. Most psilocybin depression trials to date have run in well-resourced private research settings — showing the treatment can be delivered feasibly inside a public health system, with a genuine placebo arm, is a practical step toward psilocybin therapy someday being offered as routine care rather than remaining confined to specialty trial centers.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary — a real randomized, placebo-controlled trial, but explicitly a small feasibility study, not designed to be definitive on efficacy.
In humans
Yes — 60 adults with treatment-resistant depression in an NHS clinical setting.
How promising
The symptom reduction was described as large, and delivering it within standard public healthcare infrastructure is itself a meaningful feasibility result, but the trial wasn't sized to confirm durability or cost-effectiveness.
Unknowns
Whether the benefit holds up in larger, longer-term efficacy trials, how durable the effect is, and what it would cost to deliver at scale within a public health system.
Availability
Experimental only — psilocybin is not an approved depression treatment in the UK or US outside of clinical trials.
