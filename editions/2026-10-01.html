Bone Marrow Transplant Lets Healthy Cells "Donate" Mitochondria to Rescue a Mitochondrial Disease

Researchers at Stanford Medicine gave bone marrow transplants to mice with a model of Friedreich's ataxia, an inherited disease in which a gene defect cripples mitochondria (the cell's energy-producing structures), causing progressive nerve damage and heart failure. Using a combination of chemotherapy conditioning and a drug that clears out the animals' own immune cells, the team replaced the mice's resident microglia (brain immune cells) and tissue macrophages with donor cells from healthy bone marrow. Those donor cells then physically transferred their own healthy mitochondria to neighboring neurons, other brain cells, and heart muscle cells. The work was published in Nature Communications in 2026. Friedreich's ataxia currently has no treatment that addresses its root cause. If intercellular mitochondrial transfer — effectively using transplanted immune cells as mitochondria "couriers" — works in a mitochondrial disease, it could point to a broader strategy for other conditions where failing energy production drives tissue damage.

Treated mice showed better coordination, strength, growth, survival, and heart function, with donor mitochondria measurably boosting ATP production in both brain and heart tissue

Experimental only — not in human trials yet

Study Pinpoints Why Exercise Reverses Muscle Aging: It's the Mitochondria

Researchers combined several mouse models — including a strain engineered to lack mitochondrial function specifically in muscle — with a cross-sectional study of muscle biopsies from 30 human donors ranging from 17 to 99 years old, to test whether exercise's well-known ability to preserve muscle strength and reduce frailty in aging actually depends on changes to mitochondria. The study was published in PNAS in March 2026. Exercise is already recommended for healthy aging, but this helps explain the mechanism: when the researchers blocked mitochondrial function in muscle, old mice stopped getting the usual functional benefits from exercise, meaning the mitochondrial remodeling isn't just a side effect — it's apparently required for exercise to work.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes, for the correlational piece — 30 human biopsies; the causal mitochondria-blocking experiment was mice only
How promising
Strong mechanistic evidence in animals, corroborated by a consistent pattern in human tissue
Unknowns
Whether a drug could mimic this remodeling without exercise, and how it translates to specific exercise types, doses, or clinical populations
Availability
Not a treatment — exercise itself is already available to anyone; this is new evidence for why it works

Largest Trial Yet of a GLP-1 Drug for Alzheimer's Comes Up Empty

Novo Nordisk's EVOKE and EVOKE+ trials — phase 3, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled studies run across 566 sites in 40 countries — tested oral semaglutide (up to 14 mg daily) in roughly 3,800 adults aged 55–85 with amyloid-confirmed, early-stage Alzheimer's disease. At the two-year mark, change in the CDR-SB cognitive/functional scale was essentially identical between semaglutide and placebo in both trials (differences of −0.08 and 0.10 points, not statistically significant). The results were published in The Lancet on March 19, 2026. GLP-1 drugs like semaglutide (Ozempic/Wegovy) have generated enormous interest for potential benefits beyond weight loss and diabetes, including neuroprotection. This is the largest, most rigorous test of that idea in Alzheimer's to date, and it was negative on the primary outcome — an important, if disappointing, result for a much-hyped drug class.

Yes — two phase 3 RCTs totaling roughly 3,800 patients

Not promising as an Alzheimer's treatment; biomarker reductions in neuroinflammation didn't translate into slowed decline
Unknowns
Whether GLP-1 drugs could help at an earlier disease stage, in combination with other therapies, or via a different formulation
Availability
Semaglutide is available for diabetes and weight loss, not approved or shown effective for Alzheimer's

Adding a Daily Probiotic to Antidepressants Modestly Eases Depression in Older Adults

In the PRODG trial, researchers randomized 58 adults aged 60 and older with moderate depression to receive either a daily probiotic (containing Lactobacillus helveticus and Bifidobacterium longum) or a placebo for 12 weeks, on top of their existing antidepressant treatment. The multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial was published in the Journal of the American Geriatrics Society in June 2026. The gut-brain axis — the idea that gut bacteria influence mood via inflammation, the vagus nerve, and neurotransmitter production — is a major area of nervous-system research, but most human evidence in depression has come from smaller or lower-quality studies. This is a randomized, placebo-controlled test specifically in older adults, a population often underrepresented in psychiatric trials.

Yes — 58 participants, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicenter
How promising
Modest — both groups improved substantially on existing antidepressants, with a slightly greater additional improvement in the probiotic group

Genetic Study Finds Testosterone Trades Longevity for Fertility — But Only in Men

Using Mendelian randomization — a method that uses naturally occurring genetic variation to approximate a randomized experiment — researchers analyzed genetic instruments for testosterone in 184,205 men and 199,569 women from the UK Biobank, testing testosterone's relationship to lifespan (proxied by parental attained age), fertility (number of children), and blood pressure. The study was published in The Aging Male in 2026. It's long been debated whether testosterone is protective or harmful for long-term health, with wellness clinics increasingly marketing testosterone "optimization" to aging men. This study tests a specific evolutionary hypothesis — that testosterone trades off survival for reproductive success — using genetics rather than observational data, which helps sidestep the usual confounding.

Yes — genetic association study in nearly 384,000 UK Biobank participants
How promising
Sex-specific and internally consistent: higher genetic testosterone predicted shorter lifespan, higher blood pressure, and more children in men, with no association in women
Unknowns
Whether this lifelong genetic effect applies to adults starting testosterone therapy later in life, and the exact mechanism linking testosterone to blood pressure and mortality
Availability
Not a treatment — a genetic epidemiology finding relevant to how testosterone therapy is evaluated
