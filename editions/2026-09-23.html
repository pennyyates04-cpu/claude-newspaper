Human Neural Stem Cells Repair Stroke Damage and Restore Movement — in Mice

Researchers at the University of Zurich's Institute for Regenerative Medicine, led by Christian Tackenberg and postdoctoral researcher Rebecca Weber, working with Ruslan Rust at USC, transplanted human induced-pluripotent-stem-cell-derived neural progenitor cells into mice one week after a stroke. Over five weeks, most of the transplanted cells matured into neurons that formed working connections with the animals' own brain cells. The team also saw new blood vessel formation, reduced inflammation, and improved blood-brain-barrier integrity — signs the graft was triggering broader repair, not just adding new neurons. Treated mice climbed and walked measurably better than untreated controls. Brain tissue lost to stroke is normally considered permanent damage; this is one of the more complete pictures yet of a human-derived cell graft not only surviving and integrating in an injured adult brain, but apparently kicking off several parallel repair processes at once.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
No — a mouse model using human-derived cells; no human trial has been run.
How promising
Encouraging on survival, integration, functional recovery, and vascular repair, but preclinical mouse results frequently fail to replicate in human brain-injury trials.
Unknowns
Whether the graft survives and drives recovery in a human's much larger, more complex brain, and what an effective, safe dose or delivery method would be.
Availability
Experimental only — an animal-model finding, not in any clinical pipeline yet.

Ozempic-Type Drugs May Work by Recruiting Hunger Neurons, Not Just Silencing Them

A Yale-led team published a study in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences on August 4, 2026, testing what actually happens to AgRP neurons — brain cells known for driving hunger — during prolonged GLP-1 drug treatment. The long-standing assumption was that drugs like semaglutide (Ozempic/Wegovy) suppress appetite by silencing these neurons. Instead, the researchers found that in female mice on sustained semaglutide treatment, AgRP neurons were recruited and activated, not shut down. When the researchers disabled these neurons, the mice kept eating less as expected, but sustained fat loss weakened — the neurons appeared necessary for the durability of the weight loss, not just for driving hunger. It's a direct test of a core assumption about how GLP-1 drugs work, run in living animals rather than inferred from cell studies; if the same mechanism holds in humans, it could reshape thinking about why GLP-1 drugs outperform older appetite suppressants.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
No — tested only in female mice; semaglutide itself is already FDA-approved and widely used in humans, but this specific brain-circuit finding is unconfirmed in people.
How promising
A surprising, mechanistically clean result overturning a widely repeated assumption, but it's one study in one sex of one species.
Unknowns
Whether the same pattern occurs in male mice or humans, and whether it explains why some patients maintain weight loss better than others on GLP-1 drugs.
Availability
Not a product finding — basic mechanistic research; semaglutide is already approved and in clinical use.

Unpublished Trial Claims a Daily Supplement Improves Arterial Stiffness and Blood Pressure

NOVOS, a longevity-supplement company, released preprint results from a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of its multi-ingredient "NOVOS Core" supplement (magnesium, ginger root extract, and other compounds) in adults over 40, run with researchers including a University of Surrey cardiovascular physiology group. After six months, the supplement group showed carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity (arterial stiffness) fall by 1.18 m/s more than placebo, systolic blood pressure drop by 6.1 mmHg more, and endothelial function (flow-mediated dilation) improve by 2.9 percentage points more — effect sizes the authors say exceed what's typically reported in resistance-training or high-intensity-interval-training studies. Vascular aging is a major driver of cardiovascular disease risk, and a supplement with this size of effect would be notable if it holds up — but the trial was funded by the company selling the product, and the results have not yet passed peer review.

Evidence
Mixed / uncertain evidence — sound randomized design, but sponsor-funded, sponsor-run, and not yet peer-reviewed.
In humans
Yes — a randomized controlled trial in adults over 40, but published only as a preprint so far.
How promising
The effect sizes are unusually large for a supplement, which is itself a reason for caution until independent replication and peer review confirm the numbers.
Unknowns
Whether the numbers hold up under peer review and independent (non-sponsor) replication, and which ingredient(s) are doing the work.
Availability
Sold commercially now — supplements don't require FDA efficacy approval before sale, regardless of peer-review status.

A Blood-Based "Aging Clock" Predicts Dementia Risk Years Before Symptoms

King's College London researchers validated a blood-based metabolomic aging clock, called MileAge, that estimates a person's biological age from plasma metabolites and compares it to their chronological age. Using UK Biobank data from 223,496 participants, of whom 3,976 later developed dementia, they found that people whose biological age exceeded their chronological age by more than one standard deviation — about 16% of the group — had a 20% higher overall dementia risk, and a 60% higher risk specifically for vascular dementia. People with both accelerated biological aging and two copies of the APOE ε4 gene variant were up to ten times more likely to develop dementia than average, with the two risk factors appearing to act independently. Most dementia risk tools rely on age, genetics, or late-stage symptoms; a blood test flagging elevated risk years before symptoms appear could open a window for earlier intervention.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — a large prospective cohort analysis (UK Biobank), not an interventional trial.
How promising
The cohort size and clear dose-response relationship are strong points; it's an association study, not proof that lowering biological age would lower dementia risk.
Unknowns
Whether intervening to reduce the biological-age gap actually reduces dementia risk, and how the test performs outside the mostly-European UK Biobank population.
Availability
Not a clinical test yet — validated in one large cohort study, not currently offered as a doctor-ordered diagnostic.

Alzheimer's & Dementia (Wiley), published online May 13, 2026 — https://alz-journals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/alz.71280

A Single Sauna Session Sends Every Type of White Blood Cell Surging

Finnish researchers put 51 adults (average age 50) through a 30-minute Finnish sauna session that included a brief cold-shower cooling break, then tracked their blood. Every major type of circulating white blood cell — including neutrophils and lymphocytes, key infection-fighting cells — rose sharply during the session, independent of the fluid-concentration changes that come from sweating. Levels of neutrophils and lymphocytes returned to baseline within about 30 minutes after the sauna ended, while the increase in immune cell counts was notably stronger than the accompanying rise in inflammatory signaling molecules (cytokines). Sauna use is repeatedly linked in observational studies to lower cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, but the biological mechanism has been unclear; a rapid, reversible mobilization of infection-fighting white blood cells is a plausible piece of that puzzle.

Evidence
Promising but preliminary
In humans
Yes — a controlled physiological study in 51 adults.
How promising
A clean, well-measured acute physiological effect, but one exposure in one small study; it doesn't by itself show changed long-term infection risk.
Unknowns
Whether repeated exposure over weeks or months translates into fewer infections or better long-term immune function, and how the effect varies by temperature, duration, or frequency.
Availability
Not a clinical intervention — describes a widely available practice (sauna bathing); no device or protocol is being marketed off this study.

Temperature (Taylor & Francis), published March 31, 2026 — https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23328940.2026.2645467
