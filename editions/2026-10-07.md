## MD Anderson Identifies a New Molecular Trigger Behind "Inflammaging" — and a Drug That Already Blocks It

**What happened:** Researchers led by Rugang Zhang at MD Anderson found that as cells become senescent ("retired" but not dead), they export DNA-RNA hybrid structures called R-loops out of the nucleus and into the cytoplasm, using a transport complex formed by the proteins DDX1 and XPO1. Once in the cytoplasm, the R-loops bind leftover DNA debris and switch on the cGAS-STING pathway — an immune-sensing system — which drives the chronic, low-grade inflammation known as "inflammaging." In mice and cell models, the cancer drug selinexor (which blocks XPO1) prevented this export and produced significant improvements in inflammation, liver damage, fat gain, muscle loss, and overall lifespan. The study was published in Nature Aging on June 16, 2026.
**Why it matters:** It's a concrete, mechanistic explanation for one of the most pervasive drivers of age-related disease, paired with a drug that's already been proven safe in humans for a different use — which could meaningfully shorten the path from discovery to treatment.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** No — the R-loop mechanism and selinexor's effects were shown in cell cultures and mouse models only; selinexor itself is FDA-approved, but for certain blood cancers, not for aging-related inflammation.
**How promising:** The mechanism is backed by both genetic and pharmacological blockade experiments pointing the same direction, and reusing an already-approved drug is a real head start — but mouse biology doesn't always translate.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether blocking this pathway chronically is safe and effective in humans for aging-related inflammation, what dose and duration would be needed, and whether the benefits hold up outside the mouse model.
**Availability:** Experimental only for this purpose — selinexor is approved for certain cancers, not for inflammaging or any aging-related condition.

**Source:** Nature Aging, June 16, 2026 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s43587-026-01147-6

## A Network Map of 2,358 Aging Genes Flags an Over-the-Counter Nasal Spray as a Longevity Candidate

**What happened:** A team including network scientist Albert-László Barabási mapped 2,358 longevity-associated genes onto the human protein-interaction network, grouping them into "hallmark modules" representing distinct aging processes. They then measured how close 6,442 approved and experimental drugs sit to each hallmark module in that network, combined with a new metric (pAGE) that checks whether a drug's effect on gene expression pushes cells toward a more youthful or more aged pattern. One standout: oxymetazoline — the decongestant in brands like Afrin and Sinex, also used in eye drops and rosacea creams — scored well for a hallmark related to how cells communicate with each other, a process that degrades with age. The work was published in Nature Aging in 2026.
**Why it matters:** Rather than testing anti-aging candidates one at a time, this approach generates a ranked, mechanistically grounded list of already-approved drugs to prioritize for future testing — a scalable shortcut if it holds up.

**Evidence:** Mixed/uncertain evidence
**Studied in humans:** No — this is a purely computational analysis; oxymetazoline has not been tested for any anti-aging effect in cells, animals, or people.
**How promising:** It's a clever, large-scale hypothesis-generating tool, but a high network/transcriptional score is a prediction, not a demonstrated biological effect.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether oxymetazoline (or any other flagged candidate) actually slows aging processes when tested in cells, animals, or clinical trials.
**Availability:** Not a treatment — oxymetazoline is already sold over the counter for congestion and eye redness, but using it for anti-aging purposes is untested and not supported by this study alone.

**Source:** Nature Aging, 2026 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s43587-026-01161-8

## Yale Study of Over 11,000 Older Adults Finds Nearly Half Improve With Age — and Positive Beliefs About Aging Predict Who

**What happened:** Yale researchers Becca Levy and Martin Slade analyzed data on 11,314 community-dwelling U.S. adults aged 65 and older from the Health and Retirement Study, following them for up to 12 years. They measured physical function via walking-speed tests and cognitive function via tests of memory, orientation, attention, and executive function. Overall, 45.15% of participants improved in cognitive function, physical function, or both over the follow-up period — and people who held more positive beliefs about their own aging at the start were significantly more likely to be among those who improved, even after adjusting for other factors. The study, titled "Aging Redefined," was published in Geriatrics in March 2026.
**Why it matters:** It pushes back on the assumption that aging is a uniform, one-way slide into decline, and points to a modifiable psychological factor — attitudes toward aging — that tracks with who actually gets better over time.

**Evidence:** Mixed/uncertain evidence
**Studied in humans:** Yes — 11,314 adults 65 and older, followed for up to 12 years.
**How promising:** The size and duration of the cohort make the pattern hard to dismiss, but the design is observational, so it can't establish that holding positive beliefs causes improvement rather than simply reflecting it.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether deliberately shifting someone's beliefs about aging would produce the same gains, and how much of the association is explained by other factors like baseline health, income, or social support.
**Availability:** Not a clinical intervention — a research finding about an association, not a treatment or program.

**Source:** Geriatrics, March 2026 — https://www.mdpi.com/2308-3417/11/2/28

## The Largest Long-Term Study Yet Finds a Strength-Training "Sweet Spot" for Living Longer

**What happened:** A Harvard-led team (Yiwen Zhang, Dong Hoon Lee, Leandro Rezende, Yuan Ma, and Edward Giovannucci) pooled three long-running U.S. cohort studies covering 147,374 adults (79% women), who self-reported strength-training and aerobic-exercise habits every two years and were followed for up to 30 years, with 35,798 deaths recorded. People who averaged 90 to 119 minutes of strength training per week had a 13% lower risk of death from any cause, a 19% lower risk of cardiovascular death, and a 27% lower risk of death from neurological disease (primarily dementia), compared with those who did none. More than 120 minutes a week showed no additional benefit, and combining strength training with aerobic exercise produced the largest risk reduction of all. It was published in the British Journal of Sports Medicine on June 12, 2026.
**Why it matters:** It replaces vague "lift weights sometimes" advice with a specific, achievable weekly target — roughly two sessions — backed by the largest and longest dataset yet assembled on the question.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** Yes — 147,374 adults tracked for up to 30 years using self-reported exercise habits.
**How promising:** The dose-response pattern and its consistency across cardiovascular and neurological causes of death strengthen the case, but the study is observational — people who strength-train regularly may differ in other health-relevant ways from those who don't.
**What we don't know yet:** Whether a randomized trial assigning people to strength training would show the same mortality benefit, and whether the "sweet spot" shifts by age, sex, or starting fitness level.
**Availability:** Freely available now — this is a lifestyle recommendation, not a drug or device; roughly two 45-to-60-minute resistance sessions per week is the practical takeaway.

**Source:** British Journal of Sports Medicine, June 12, 2026 — https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/60/12/874

## First U.S. Trial of Psilocybin for Veterans With Severe, Treatment-Resistant PTSD Shows 75% Remission at One Month

**What happened:** In an open-label pilot trial, 12 veterans with PTSD that had not responded to psychotherapy or medication received eight hours of preparatory therapy, then two doses of synthetic psilocybin (15 mg, then 25 mg, weeks apart), followed by six to eight more hours of integration therapy, over an 11-week protocol. Clinician-rated PTSD symptoms dropped by an average of about 27.5 points, and 9 of the 12 veterans (75%) no longer met diagnostic criteria for PTSD one month after the trial ended. There were no serious adverse events and no increase in suicidal thinking or behavior. The results were published in Communications Medicine on July 30, 2026, with Stacey Armstrong as first author.
**Why it matters:** This group had already failed standard treatments, so a 75% remission rate — even in a tiny trial — is a notable signal in a population that is otherwise hard to help, and it adds to growing interest in psychedelic-assisted therapy for trauma.

**Evidence:** Promising but preliminary
**Studied in humans:** Yes — but only 12 participants, in an open-label design with no placebo group and no blinding.
**How promising:** The remission rate is striking for severe, treatment-resistant illness, but without a control group, some of the effect could reflect the intensive accompanying therapy and attention rather than the drug itself.
**What we don't know yet:** How much of the benefit comes from psilocybin versus the surrounding therapy, whether the gains persist beyond one month, and how the approach performs in a larger, randomized, placebo-controlled trial.
**Availability:** Experimental only — psilocybin remains a Schedule I substance in the U.S. outside of clinical trials and limited expanded-access or state programs; it is not an approved PTSD treatment.

**Source:** Communications Medicine, July 30, 2026 — https://www.news-medical.net/news/20260730/Psilocybin-trial-shows-striking-remission-rates-for-veterans-with-PTSD.aspx
